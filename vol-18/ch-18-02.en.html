1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Wednesday evening, the day before the release described in ch-18-01. Site engineering runs the software-in-the-loop testing (SIL testing) suite of the reference mine against the release candidate: 18 test cases, each a registered scenario with an injected fault, a seed and an expected end state. All 18 pass. One of them is new. Last week the night-shift controller reported that a grant for LV07 at AP-02 had appeared late and twice on the field terminal; the area stayed locked and LV07 asked again. The event became case TC-16 — a delayed and duplicated grant — and it passes too.

The Pit Supervisor has one more question: "If the terminal software had a bug and accepted old grants, would your suite notice?" The engineer seeds exactly that defect into the model and runs the suite again. One case fails: TC-18, a grant 20 s late. TC-17, the case built on the registry's own timing of a grant 30 s late, still passes — the old grant had also expired, so a second check rejected it and hid the defect.

The questions this raises:

  • What does a passed SIL-testing suite show, and which conclusions does it not support?
  • Which faults can be injected in software, and which must be injected on real controllers with HIL testing?
  • How does a suite know that it would catch a defect, and how does a field event become a test case?
  • How can controllers use the same replay to review the CT3 comms-loss procedure, or to rehearse rerouting over R24 before it is used in production?

This chapter covers:

  • the architecture of a SIL-testing harness, and how hardware-in-the-loop testing (HIL testing) differs from it;
  • fault-injection points along the FMS–AMS–ADS–VCS chain, and which require HIL testing;
  • requirement oracles, regression oracles and invariants, and a mutation check that measures what a suite can catch;
  • seeded fault injection, determinism and the regression rule for adding a case after a field event;
  • the limits of simulation fidelity;
  • replay of SCN-COMMS-R05 for controllers and rehearsal of a playbook in SIL testing.

"SIL testing" and "HIL testing" are always written in full; SIL alone means safety integrity level. The verification levels and the coverage matrix are those of ch-18-01; this chapter does not repeat them. The harness, its cases, seeds and seeded defects are educational [H]/[A]; they do not describe any vendor's test system.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Design a SIL-testing harness that runs the AMS model against registered scenarios with injected network and positioning faults and compares outcomes with expected results.
  2. Explain what HIL testing adds over SIL testing (real controllers, timing, I/O) and specify which faults must be tested on HIL rather than in software alone.
  3. Analyse the limits of simulation fidelity and state which conclusions a SIL-testing or HIL-testing result does not support.
  4. Specify a regression suite for SIL testing built from the scenario registry with deterministic seeds, and the rule for adding a new case after a field event.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Use SIL-testing replay of SCN-COMMS-R05 to review the comms-loss procedure with controllers before a release.
  2. Specify the operational scenarios that must pass in SIL testing before a software release is accepted at the reference mine.
  3. Rehearse a new playbook in SIL testing — for example rerouting pit traffic via the west ramp R24 when R05 is closed — before it is used in production.
  4. Report field events with the data needed to turn them into new SIL-testing cases.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

ch-18-01 showed that the system column of the coverage matrix is the strongest at the reference mine and that most of it is filled by SIL testing. That is no accident. SIL testing is the only method that can place a fault exactly — a grant delayed by 20.0 s, a re-registration report lost on its 20th copy, three trucks stopped inside Intersection X at the same restart — and run the same case again tomorrow with the same result. It is cheap enough to run before every release and fast enough to run hundreds of seeds.

The same properties make SIL testing easy to over-trust. A SIL-testing case exercises models: of the network, of the vehicle, of the field terminal. Where the model is the specification, a passed case says the software follows the specification under the injected faults. It does not say that the real radio behaves like the model, that the real ADS detects degraded GNSS integrity, or that the VCS reacts to a lost bus frame within its watchdog. Those questions need real controllers on a simulated plant — HIL testing — and in the end real trucks and real sites (ch-18-03).

Three problems show up at the reference mine:

  • A suite that cannot fail. Cases are added from the supplier's experience and pass every release. Nobody knows whether they would detect anything. A suite has value only if it fails when the software is wrong, and that can be measured.
  • Field events that stay in the shift log. A controller sees a grant shown twice on a terminal; the report says "terminal glitch, area stayed locked, LV07 asked again". Without the scenario, the times and the configuration, the event cannot become a case, and the next release is tested without it.
  • Procedures rehearsed on production. A new playbook — the R05 closure with all circuits over R24 — is used for the first time on a real closure. The same replay engine that tests the AMS could show controllers the routes, cycle times and alarm stream first.

Published practice for SIL testing and HIL testing of vehicle and mining automation — test-rig architectures, GNSS signal simulation, real-time plant models, fidelity criteria — is not in the source register; nothing here is taken from it [TBV] (SRC-PUB-INTHELOOP-TESTING). Methods for judging the strength of a test suite by seeding defects are described in the software-testing literature, which is not in the register either [TBV] (SRC-PUB-MUTATION-TESTING). What ISO 17757:2019 (SRC-STD-ISO17757, verified at catalogue level only) or other standards expect of simulation-based verification is not stated [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AHS-VERIFICATION-EXPECTATIONS). The harness, its oracles and rules are the handbook's [H].

3. Terminology and foundations

SIL testing. Testing software against simulated environment and plant models (glossary software-in-the-loop). Everything runs as software, usually on one computer, on a simulated clock.

HIL testing. Testing real controller hardware connected to a simulated plant and environment (glossary hardware-in-the-loop). The simulator runs in real time and exchanges real signals with the controllers.

Test harness. The software that runs test cases: it builds the scenario state, injects faults, runs the system under test and its models, applies the oracles and writes the test record.

Fault injection and injection point. Deliberately introducing a fault to test how the system detects and handles it (glossary fault-injection); the injection point is where in the chain the fault enters — at a message link, at the output of a function, at a sensor input or at an electrical interface.

Oracle. The part of a test case that decides the verdict (a pass/fail criterion, ch-18-01). A requirement oracle checks a property stated by a requirement and must hold for every seed. A regression oracle compares an observed value with a golden value published earlier, so that any change of behaviour is noticed. An invariant is a requirement oracle checked over the whole trace, not only at the end.

Seed and determinism. Every stochastic run takes an explicit seed; the same case with the same seed on the same model version gives the same result. A suite is deterministic when its complete result can be reproduced bit for bit.

Regression suite. The set of cases run before every release. Cases are added, never removed; a case is changed only with a record.

Mutation check. Running the suite against deliberately seeded defects (mutants) of the software under test to see which cases fail. A mutant that no case detects shows a hole in the suite.

Simulation fidelity. How closely a model reproduces the behaviour of the real element for the question asked. A model can be faithful for one question (does the AMS keep the area locked?) and useless for another (how long does the radio take to recover?).

Replay. Running a recorded or registered event through the models to show what happened, step by step, without the real system — for analysis, for controller review or for rehearsal.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The harness and the two architectures

FIG-18-02-01 compares the two architectures. Both are driven by the same scenarios, the same fault injector and the same oracle; what changes is the platform under test.

In SIL testing the AMS software, a network model and models of the vehicles and terminals run together on a simulated clock. A case at the reference mine takes a fraction of a second, so hundreds of seeds are affordable. In HIL testing the real ADS computer, VCS ECU, OSS controller or remote-stop receiver is wired to a plant and environment simulator that runs in real time and exchanges bus frames, discrete signals and, for GNSS, radio-frequency signals with the controller. A run takes as long as the event; rig hours limit the number of runs.

LAB-18-02-01 is a minimal SIL-testing harness [H]. A test case is a record:

Field Meaning Example (TC-10)
ID, scenario Case ID and registered scenario TC-10, SCN-COMMS-R05
Injection Fault and where it enters Re-registration reports lost with probability 0.0 / 0.5 / 0.8 after CT3 returns
Seeds Seeds of every run 1 to 200
Requirement oracle Properties that must hold in every run Grants blocked if and only if a truck is unknown; every unknown truck escalated
Regression oracle Golden values published by other chapters (none for this case: the statistics are reported, not asserted as requirements)
Requirements Where the oracle comes from REQ-02-08-005, REQ-10-08-003

The harness runs each case through existing models: ahs_sim.control_room (voice and digital positive communication, alarm streams, event clusters), ahs_sim.messaging (link faults, access exchanges), ahs_sim.reconciliation, ahs_sim.recovery (retransmission with a retry budget, restart gates), ahs_sim.ams (reservations, wait-for cycles, victim choice) and ahs_sim.haul_cycle with ahs_sim.mine (routes). The states of SCN-COMMS-R05, SCN-AMS-RESTART and SCN-DEADLOCK-X are read directly from LAB-02-08-01, so that the harness reproduces the values ch-02-08 published. The lab does not use ahs_sim.network (Volume 11) or ahs_sim.localisation (Volume 05). Network faults come from messaging.Faults and recovery.deliver_with_retry; positioning faults are lab-local seeded integrity reports injected at the output of the localisation function [A].

Each result records the case, the seeds, the injection, the model version (ahs_sim 0.9.0) and both oracles' verdicts, and the whole suite result has a digest. Two runs give the same digest; a test of the lab checks it.

The rule behind the "minimum platform" column is short [H]. SIL testing suffices when both the fault and the response live in software logic and the response does not depend on the timing of real hardware — message acceptance, reservations, reconciliation, permission states. HIL testing is required when the response depends on a real controller's timing, its I/O or its electrical interface, or when the element is a safety-related controller (ISF, OSS, VCS) whose own firmware is the thing to verify. Vehicle and site tests are required when the response depends on physics or on the real environment — stopping distance on a real grade, radio coverage at the RL 330 bench.

The injection point decides what a test can show. TC-13 injects degraded integrity at FP5, as a report from the localisation function. It verifies that the AMS recognises the pattern of SCN-GNSS-R05 — DT17 at T+0 s and DT13 at T+40 s at R05 point to an infrastructure cause by T+60 s — and nothing about whether a real ADS would have flagged DT17's integrity in the first place. That second question starts at FP6, at the receiver input, and needs HIL testing with a GNSS signal simulator or recorded signals [TBV]. A SIL-testing pass at FP5 and a HIL-testing pass at FP6 verify different links of the same chain; neither substitutes for the other.

4.4 Oracles, invariants and the mutation check

A case that checks only end states can pass while the system goes through a wrong state on the way. TC-17 and TC-18 show it. Both delay a GRANT on its way to LV07's terminal; the terminal must reject a message older than 15 s [A], and the GRANT itself is valid for 30 s [A]. In TC-17 the delay is 30.3 s, the registry's timing: the GRANT is both too old and expired, and two separate checks in the terminal reject it. In TC-18 the delay is 20 s: the GRANT is too old but not expired, and only the age check stands between the driver and an entry on an old grant.

The mutation check makes this measurable [H]. LAB-18-02-01 runs the suite against three seeded defects:

Mutant Defect seeded Cases that fail Through which oracle
M1 The terminal accepts grants of any age TC-18 only Requirement (no entry; no stale grant shown) and regression
M2 Reconciliation takes its list of expected trucks from the trucks that reported TC-09, TC-10, TC-15 Requirement (no grant while a truck is unknown); TC-15 also regression
M3 The restart gate checks reconciliation only, not wait-for cycles TC-11, TC-12 Requirement (gate closed while a cycle exists)

Under M1, TC-18 ends with LV07 entered and the AMS permission ACTIVE: the area was locked, so the fail-closed invariant of the AMS holds, but the driver entered on a grant that had arrived 20.0 s after issue. TC-17 passes under M1, because the expiry check rejects the 30.3 s GRANT anyway. Without TC-18, a suite built only on the registry's timing would have no case that detects M1. The lesson for case design is the boundary value: wherever two defences overlap, place one case between their limits.

M2 shows the other dependency: a defect is found only if the injected faults reach it. If TC-10 ran only with loss 0.0 and 0.5, no re-registration would ever be lost in the 200 seeds, every truck would report, and M2 would pass TC-10. Only at 0.8 are reports lost (24 reports missing over 20 of the 200 runs), and only then does the defective reconciliation grant with a truck unknown.

The mutants are not a proof of adequacy. They are three defects someone thought of. What the check shows is that each of the suite's requirement oracles can fail, and which case is responsible for which kind of defect; that is more than a suite of always-passing cases can say about itself.

4.5 Seeded injection, determinism and statistics

Seeds turn a probabilistic fault into a reproducible test. TC-10 injects loss on the re-registration reports after CT3 returns; each report is retransmitted under the RetryPolicy defaults of ahs_sim.recovery — 5 copies 2 s apart, up to 3 re-issues 20 s after the copies, then escalation to the AHS Controller [A]:

Report loss Runs complete Runs with grants blocked Latest completion
0.0 200 0 T+932.3 s
0.5 200 0 T+968.3 s
0.8 180 20 T+1022.3 s

The requirement oracle holds in all 600 runs: grants stay blocked exactly when a truck is unknown, and every unknown truck is escalated. The statistics are not requirements; they describe the model under an assumed loss. Two observations are useful nonetheless. At loss 0.5 four runs escalate a truck whose report the AMS has in fact received — the AMS's acknowledgements were lost, not the reports. At loss 0.8 that happens in 179 of the 200 runs. An escalation therefore says "this truck never heard back", not "the AMS does not know this truck"; section 8.1 turns that into a controller check.

TC-12 places the three deadlocked trucks of SCN-DEADLOCK-X inside or short of their conflict cells at random. A cycle appears in 21 of 200 seeds — when all three stopped inside — and the restart gate is closed in exactly those runs. A site restart would meet this configuration rarely and could not repeat it on demand; the seed list can.

Determinism has a price that must be paid deliberately: the golden values depend on the model version. When ahs_sim or the AMS release changes, the suite's regression oracles may fail without any defect. The rule [H] is that a regression value is changed only with a record of why it changed and who accepted the new value — never by re-running until the suite is green.

4.6 What HIL testing adds

HIL testing adds the controller itself. Three classes of behaviour appear only there:

  • Timing. A watchdog in the VCS that must bring the truck to a stop when ADS frames stop arriving, the time between a fire-detection signal and the safe-state request on the bus, the scheduling of the ADS computer under load. A SIL-testing model has whatever timing the modeller wrote.
  • I/O and electrical interfaces. Bus frames with real identifiers and rates, discrete signals of the remote-stop receiver, a GNSS receiver fed with simulated satellite signals and GB1 corrections. Faults such as a lost bus, a stuck input or a corrupted frame exist only at this level.
  • Firmware. The software actually loaded on the controller, built with its real toolchain, with its configuration and calibration data.

Which faults must be tested on HIL, then? Those at FP6 to FP10 of FIG-18-02-02 [H]:

Scenario Fault that needs HIL testing Why SIL testing is not enough
SCN-GNSS-R05 Degraded satellite geometry and multipath at the receiver input; loss of GB1 corrections Whether the real ADS computes reduced integrity and derates in time depends on the receiver and its firmware
SCN-FIRE-DT17 Fire-detection and suppression signals from the OSS controller The safe-state request crosses a real interface with its own timing (REQ-02-04-006)
SCN-RSTOP-AANORTH Stop command and loss of the stop link at the receiver The independent stop path must act regardless of AMS and ADS (REQ-02-04-001); only the real receiver and VCS show it
SCN-COMMS-R05 Loss of the ADS–VCS frames during a link loss The truck's stop within its grant relies on the vehicle side, not on the AMS model

The ch-18-01 matrix shows the consequence: SCN-GNSS-R05 has a HIL-testing item at component level (EV-25) and a planned one at vehicle level (VI-06); SCN-FIRE-DT17 a planned HIL-testing item (VI-07); SCN-RSTOP-AANORTH a done one (VI-18). The SIL-testing harness of this chapter has no case at FP6 to FP10, and the lab's test of the figure checks that it claims none.

4.7 Limits of simulation fidelity

A SIL-testing result supports a conclusion only within the fidelity of its models for that question. Four limits recur:

  1. The model is the specification. TC-09 checks that trucks stop within their granted path; the stop times (3.0 s to 14.1 s) come from reconciliation.link_loss_timeline, which brakes at 1.0 m/s² [A] by construction. The case verifies that the AMS logic keeps reservations frozen around trucks that behave as the model says. It does not verify that a loaded truck on lower R05 can stop that way.
  2. Timing is simplified. Detection at T+3 s, re-registration every 4 s, a 0.3 s link delay: all model parameters [A]. A SIL-testing case can show that the logic is correct for those times, and with a sweep that it stays correct over a range; not that the real radio produces them.
  3. Shared assumptions. When the harness models and the software under test come from the same specification, an error in the specification passes both. A case written from the requirement, by someone other than the author of the model, reduces that risk; it does not remove it.
  4. Exploration finds questions, not answers. The lab's exploratory injection for SCN-GNSS-R05 places degraded reports from six trucks at random over 30 minutes [A] and compares control_room.clusters, which groups events in a window anchored at the first event of a group, with an independent oracle written from the registry's wording — two trucks degraded within 60 s at the same place. In 200 seeds they agree 199 times. In seed 139 they do not: DT32 reports at 488.6 s and 535.0 s, DT36 at 568.8 s; DT32 and DT36 are 33.8 s apart, but the anchored window starting at 488.6 s ends before 568.8 s, so clusters sees two single-truck groups. Whether the AMS should use a sliding window is a design question for the engineer (from ahs_sim 0.9.1, clusters(…, sliding=True) offers one); once decided, the seed becomes a regression case. It is reported here, and the default of clusters is not changed: ahs_sim.control_room is shared, and changing the default would change the results of the chapters that use it.

A HIL-testing result has its own limits: the plant simulator replaces the truck, the GNSS simulator replaces the sky over the RL 330 bench, and the rig runs one truck. Neither SIL testing nor HIL testing shows behaviour on real roads, under real radio coverage or with people; those belong to vehicle and site tests (ch-18-03, ch-18-04).

4.8 The regression suite and the field-event rule

The SIL-testing regression suite of the reference mine follows six rules [H]:

  1. Every case names a registered scenario; a case for a behaviour that has no scenario starts with a registry proposal (REQ-18-01-008).
  2. Every stochastic case lists its seeds; the suite result is reproducible and carries the model and software versions.
  3. Every case has a requirement oracle; regression oracles are added where another chapter or an accepted earlier run published a value.
  4. Cases are added, never removed; a case is changed only with a record of the reason.
  5. The suite is run against the mutants before it is used to accept a release; a requirement oracle that no mutant can make fail is reviewed.
  6. A field event that the suite did not reproduce becomes a case before the next release.

The last rule needs data from the field. The lab's helper new_case_from_field_event shows the minimum: an event ID, the scenario, the injection that reproduces the event and the expected end state from the requirement. TC-16 was made that way from FE-2026-10-27 [A]: the scenario SCN-POSCOM-STALE, the faults stale grant and duplicate grant together, the expectation from REQ-16-03-004 that any disagreement is treated as not granted. Section 8.3 lists what the controller's report must contain for the engineer to build it.

Two boundaries matter. The harness owner is not the author of the software under test. Site engineering owns the suite at the reference mine [A]; the supplier's own tests are evidence the site uses, and the mutation check is the site's way of knowing what its own suite catches. A replay for controllers is not a test of controllers. It shows what the system does; the controller's decisions are recorded as training (ch-16-09) or, with a test record, as a site item (ch-18-01).

after T+1016 s Seven trucks known, DT20 unknown; no grant to any truck Grants blocked because a truck is unknown (REQ-02-08-005)

The case passes: the AMS fails closed. For the controller the run carries a message the nominal run hid. Three escalations arrive, but only one truck is unknown to the AMS. The controller must read the AMS's list of unknown trucks, not the escalations, before deciding to send a Field Technician (section 8.3).

6.3 Degraded operation — restoration on a lossy link (TC-10, loss 0.5)

CT3 returns but the link to the pit floor stays marginal; reports are lost with probability 0.5 [A]. In all 200 seeds every truck eventually reports and reconciliation completes; the latest completion is T+968.3 s instead of T+932 s. The staged resume starts later accordingly, and in 4 of the 200 seeds a truck is escalated although the AMS has its report.

For the strategy this run answers a narrow question: under a lossy return link, does the AMS still wait for every truck and still resume in stages? Yes, in the model. It does not say how often CT3 comes back marginal, how long the trucks on lower R05 actually stand, or what the real radio does; those belong to the network volume and to the CT3 coverage test planned for commissioning (VI-13 in ch-18-01).

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

  • Cases are scenarios plus injections. Every case names a registered scenario, so the suite and the coverage matrix of ch-18-01 speak the same language. The price is a registry that must grow with the suite.
  • Two kinds of oracle. Requirement oracles carry the safety argument; regression oracles catch any change. Keeping them separate lets a deliberate model change update golden values without weakening a requirement.
  • States of other chapters read in place. The CT3, restart and deadlock states come from LAB-02-08-01, not from copies, so the harness cannot drift from the chapter that published them.
  • A mutation check as part of the suite. Three seeded defects cost little and show which cases carry which requirement. The price is maintaining the mutants with the software.
  • No fault point that the platform cannot reach. FP6 to FP10 are marked HIL testing and have no SIL-testing case; a SIL-testing case at those points would test a model of a controller, not the controller.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

On the shared timeline the harness depends on its clocks:

  • Simulated clock. T+0 s to T+1172 s of TC-09 run in a fraction of a second. Any behaviour that depends on real elapsed time — a watchdog, a radio retry timer in firmware — is not exercised.
  • T+3 s and T+10 s. Detection and marking are model parameters; a sweep over them shows sensitivity, not the real values. The harness reports which parameters a case depends on.
  • T+904 s to T+1016 s. Retry intervals, copies and re-issues are those of RetryPolicy [A]. The escalation at T+1016 s is the end of DT20's retry budget, 92 s after its first copy; a different budget moves it.
  • One time base. All times in the harness are AMS true time. When a case mixes clocks — the terminal's offset in ch-02-07 — the record says which clock each time comes from (ch-18-01, section 7.3).
  • HIL rigs run in real time. A case that takes 1172 s of scenario time takes 1172 s on a HIL rig; the suite for HIL testing is therefore shorter and chosen from the SIL-testing results.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees — a replay of SCN-COMMS-R05 before the release

Before the release, the controllers on both shifts sit with Site engineering for a replay of SCN-COMMS-R05 (TC-09, and seed 1 of TC-10). The replay script of LAB-18-02-01 merges the registry events, the per-truck times and the alarm counts of section 6.1 into one timeline. What the controllers see:

  • in the first minute 22 alarms raised, 3 when grouped by area — and they practise reading the truck list instead of the alarm list;
  • eight trucks stopped between T+3.0 s and T+14.1 s, and no truck moving until T+962 s;
  • the reconciliation progress from T+904 s to T+932 s, and the staged resume to T+1172 s;
  • in the abnormal run, three escalations and one unknown truck, DT20, and the rule that the AMS's unknown list, not the escalation list, decides who goes to the pit floor.

The replay is not the release. It shows the controllers what the release does in a registered scenario, in the model; anything that looks different on the real HMI after the release is a field event.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — from a field event to a SIL-testing case

[H], times illustrative [A].

  1. AHS Controller (during the event): note the time on the AMS clock, the scenario if one fits (for example SCN-POSCOM-STALE), the trucks, people and devices involved, and what the HMI, the terminal and the radio showed.
  2. AHS Controller (before the end of the shift): file a field-event report with: event ID, AMS time, scenario or "no scenario", equipment IDs, what was expected, what happened, the state at the end (for example "area locked, LV07 asked again"), the AMS release and map version shown in CR1, and the event-log extract from T−60 s to T+60 s around the event.
  3. Pit Supervisor (next day): confirm the report and its scenario; if no scenario fits, propose one for the registry (REQ-18-01-008).
  4. Site engineering: reproduce the event in the harness with an injection and a seed; write the expected end state from the requirement, not from the event; add the case to the suite.
  5. Site engineering: if the case fails on the current release, the event has found a defect: raise it with the supplier and in the hazard-log review triggers of ch-04-01. If it passes, record that the suite now covers it.
  6. AHS Controller who reported it: review the replay of the new case and confirm that it shows what they saw.

For a playbook rehearsal — the R05 closure with all circuits over R24 — the Pit Supervisor and the controllers review TC-14 before the playbook is used: EX02's loaded route becomes R18, R08, R09, R24, R25, R12, R02, R21; its cycle grows from 1564.1 s to 1800.1 s in the steady-state model; no loading unit is held. EX01 also loses 236.0 s per cycle, while EX03 and EX04 gain 39.4 s, because in the model the climb on the 6.3 % west ramp is faster than on the main ramp. The rehearsal shows what the playbook asks of the fleet before a real closure does.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

SIL testing and HIL testing produce no delay for the fleet. The scenarios they replay do: trucks stopped inside the CT3 dead zone are coded DLY-OD-05 (stopped on communication loss), trucks held short of it DLY-OD-04, trucks held in the deadlock at X DLY-OD-17, and trucks stopped by an AMS failover DLY-OD-12, under the coding rules of delay-codes.yml (schema 2); all are Operating Delay in the GMG Time Usage Model [F] (SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020). A replay that shows how long each code runs in the model helps controllers code the real event; it does not predict the real duration.

The suite has its own indicators [H]: cases passing (18 of 18), mutants detected (3 of 3), cases added from field events since the last release (1, TC-16), and regression values changed with a record. No target is set.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Controllers learn to read a replay as a model of the system, not as a recording of the mine, and to report field events with the data of section 8.3. Pit Supervisors learn to specify the operational scenarios that gate a release, and to run a playbook rehearsal before first use. Site engineers learn to build cases from requirements, to run the mutation check and to explain to controllers what a SIL-testing pass does not cover. Replay sessions are simulator-based training in the sense of ch-16-09; they count as verification only with a test record and a released configuration.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-18-02-001 [H] Every SIL-testing case shall name a registered scenario, the injection and its point in the chain, the seeds of every run, a requirement oracle and the requirement IDs it comes from; every suite result shall record the model and software versions and be reproducible from them. Rationale: an irreproducible or untraceable result cannot be compared after a change (sections 4.1 and 4.5). Verification: test (LAB-18-02-01 suite digest equal on repeated runs); inspection of the suite.

REQ-18-02-002 [H] Faults at the GNSS receiver input, perception sensor inputs, the ADS–VCS interface, onboard safety-related system signals and the remote-stop path shall be verified by HIL testing or on the vehicle; a SIL-testing case shall not be counted as verification of the response of those controllers. Rationale: their responses depend on real controller timing, I/O and firmware (sections 4.2 and 4.6). Verification: inspection of the fault-point allocation; test (LAB-18-02-01 has no case at FP6 to FP10).

REQ-18-02-003 [H] For every requirement oracle, the injected faults shall include a value or range that would violate the requirement if the software were defective, and wherever two defences overlap, at least one case shall lie between their limits. Rationale: a fault that never reaches a defect cannot reveal it (section 4.4: TC-18, TC-10). Verification: mutation check; review of case design.

REQ-18-02-004 [H] Before the SIL-testing suite is used to accept a release, it shall be run against a maintained set of seeded defects, and every requirement oracle that no seeded defect makes fail shall be reviewed. Rationale: a suite that cannot fail shows nothing (section 4.4). Verification: test (LAB-18-02-01 mutation check: M1 by TC-18, M2 by TC-09, TC-10, TC-15, M3 by TC-11, TC-12).

REQ-18-02-005 [H] A regression (golden) value shall be changed only with a record of the cause of the change and the person who accepted the new value; cases shall be added to the suite, never removed. Rationale: re-running until the suite passes turns a regression into a new baseline (section 4.5). Verification: inspection of the suite change log.

REQ-18-02-006 [H] Every SIL-testing and HIL-testing result used as evidence shall state the model parameters it depends on and the conclusions it does not support, including behaviour that the platform does not contain. Rationale: a model can be faithful for one question and silent on another (section 4.7). Verification: inspection of test records.

REQ-18-02-007 [H] A field event that the SIL-testing suite does not reproduce shall be reported with the AMS time, scenario, equipment, the expected and observed behaviour, the end state, the AMS release and map version, and the event-log extract around the event, and shall become a SIL-testing case before the next release is accepted. Rationale: an event that stays in the shift log is not tested in the next release (sections 4.8 and 8.3). Verification: inspection of field-event reports and of the suite against them.

REQ-18-02-008 [H] Before a new abnormal-operations playbook is used in production, it shall be rehearsed with the controllers in SIL testing, showing the routes, cycle times and loading units held or rerouted for the registered scenario it serves. Rationale: the first use of a playbook should not be its first test (sections 2 and 8.3). Verification: inspection of rehearsal records; test (LAB-18-02-01 TC-14).

10. Verification and validation

Verification. make test CH=ch-18-02 checks that the suite passes and is deterministic; that every case uses a registered scenario and the registry times; that the harness reproduces the values published by ch-16-03 (fault matrix), ch-02-07 (sweep row), ch-02-08 (CT3 timeline, restart curve, deadlock), ch-10-05 (victim) and ch-16-07 (GNSS pattern, alarm counts, R24 reroute); the report-loss statistics and the seed-1 timeline; the seeded deadlock; the mutation check; the exploratory GNSS result; the field-event rule; that the fault points agree with FIG-18-02-02 in both languages and no HIL-testing point has a SIL-testing case; and that the script runs.

Validation. Whether a SIL-testing suite is good enough is decided by how it behaves when the system is wrong: the mutation check is a first measure; field events the suite did not reproduce are the second, and the stronger one. For HIL testing, validation of the rig itself — that the plant simulator and the GNSS simulator are faithful for the question asked — is a separate task [TBV]. The reference mine is fictional, so neither the check against field events nor the validation of a HIL rig is possible here; ch-18-03 continues with vehicle and site testing.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Testing the model with the model. Oracles that restate the model's own rules pass by construction; write them from the requirement.
  • Cases only at nominal timing. The registry's 30 s delay exercises two defences at once; TC-18 is the case that separates them.
  • Injecting where it is convenient. A positioning fault at the output of localisation is easy; it does not test the receiver.
  • Golden values updated silently. The suite stays green and the behaviour changed.
  • HIL rigs used for logic. Rig hours spent on cases that SIL testing could run in seconds leave no time for timing and I/O faults.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Treating a replay as a recording. The replay shows the model; the real event may differ, and the difference is a field event.
  • Field reports without times and versions. "Glitch at AP-02" cannot become a case.
  • Reading escalations as unknown trucks. Three escalations, one unknown truck in seed 1: send the Field Technician for DT20, not for DT21 and DT32.
  • First use of a playbook on a real closure. The R24 rehearsal costs an hour; a surprise during a real R05 closure costs more.

15. Key takeaways

  • SIL testing places faults exactly and repeats them for free; HIL testing adds real controllers, timing and I/O; neither shows real roads, radio or people.
  • A test case is a registered scenario, an injection at a named point, seeds and oracles; the suite result is reproducible from the versions.
  • Faults at the GNSS receiver, sensors, the ADS–VCS interface, onboard safety-related systems and the remote-stop path need HIL testing.
  • Requirement oracles carry the argument, regression oracles catch change, invariants catch transient wrong states.
  • A mutation check shows what a suite can catch: M1 only by the boundary case TC-18; M2 only when reports are really lost.
  • Fidelity limits: the model is the specification; timing is assumed; exploration finds questions such as seed 139.
  • Field events become cases before the next release; controllers review replays and rehearse playbooks in SIL testing before first use.
