1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is Monday morning at the reference mine. Two changes are proposed for Thursday: a new AMS release and the AHS map version AA-North v15 [A], which opens the west ramp R24 and its access road R25 to autonomous routes. The supplier's release note says that its regression suite passed. The site engineer opens the verification plan of the mine and runs the scenario coverage matrix of this chapter's lab. Of the 93 combinations of a registered scenario and a verification level that the plan requires, 26 are covered by counted evidence. SCN-POSCOM-STALE, the delayed grant that must leave the area locked, has evidence at component and system level but nothing at site level. The map change touches 16 of the 29 applicable scenarios, and 13 evidence items were produced before it. The Pit Supervisor asks a simple question: "Thursday's commissioning drive will test the new road. Isn't that enough?"

The questions this raises are the subject of the chapter:

  • What does "the regression suite passed" tell anyone about a scenario that has no test?
  • Which levels must a scenario such as a stale grant or a fire on DT17 be verified at, and by which methods?
  • Which evidence does a map version that opens R24 make out of date, and how would anyone know?
  • Why can a drive on the new road not replace the tests that were never run below it?
  • What must the control room check on site before normal production resumes after the release?

This chapter covers:

  • verification levels (component, vehicle, system, site) and methods (analysis, inspection, simulation, SIL testing, HIL testing, track test, site test, drill), and the rule that allocates each scenario to levels;
  • the scenario coverage matrix built from the scenario registry, and how to read its gaps;
  • how verification evidence feeds the safety case of ch-04-10, using the same evidence register and the same rules;
  • pass/fail criteria and test records that make scenario-based results reproducible and traceable;
  • change impact: which evidence a map version or a release makes out of date;
  • the evidence operations should demand, and the operational acceptance checks after a release.

Software-in-the-loop testing and hardware-in-the-loop testing are written "SIL testing" and "HIL testing" throughout; SIL alone means safety integrity level. How a SIL-testing harness works is the subject of ch-18-02; staged vehicle and site testing that of ch-18-03; commissioning that of ch-18-04. The verification plan, its dates and producers are illustrative [A]; the levels, methods, rules and record format are the handbook's [H].

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Design a verification strategy for an AHS that allocates requirements to component, vehicle, system and site levels and to analysis, simulation, SIL testing, HIL testing, track and site methods.
  2. Construct a scenario coverage matrix from the scenario registry and identify scenarios with no test at any level.
  3. Explain how verification evidence feeds the safety case and why site testing cannot replace lower-level verification.
  4. Specify pass/fail criteria and evidence formats for scenario-based tests so that results are reproducible and traceable to requirements.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Identify the verification evidence operations should request before accepting a new software release, an AHS map version or a new autonomous area.
  2. Contribute operational scenarios and acceptance criteria — for example from SCN-POSCOM-STALE — to the verification plan.
  3. Review the verification evidence for a map version that adds a road, such as the west ramp R24, and decide whether it covers the intended operational use.
  4. Define the operational acceptance checks controllers perform on site after a release before normal production resumes.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine runs 40 autonomous trucks in three autonomous areas from one control room, CR1. Its AHS changes all the time: AMS and ADS releases, field-terminal firmware, AHS map versions after every road change, new loading areas, a new autonomous area. Each change is a decision that someone accepts on behalf of the operation, and each acceptance rests on evidence that the changed system still behaves as the hazard log and the safety case say it does (ch-04-01, ch-04-10).

Three habits make that evidence weaker than it looks.

Counting tests instead of covering behaviour. A release note that reports a passed regression suite says how many tests passed, not which behaviours were tested. The reference mine registers its operational scenarios in one place (scenario-registry.yml); 30 were registered when this chapter's verification plan was drawn up. A strategy that starts from those scenarios can ask, for each one, "where is it verified?", and can show the answer as a matrix. A strategy that starts from the supplier's test list cannot ask the question at all.

Testing on site because site tests feel real. A drive on R24 with a real truck is convincing to watch. It shows that one truck followed one route on one afternoon. It does not show what the AMS does when a grant arrives 30 s late, when CT3 loses power with eight trucks in its coverage, or when three trucks restart into Intersection X in an unlucky order. Those behaviours must be provoked, and they can be provoked safely and repeatedly only below the site level: in analysis, in simulation, in SIL testing and HIL testing, on a closed track. Site tests are the last level, not the only one.

Treating evidence as permanent. A test of the AMS access-permission logic run in January says nothing certain about the release proposed for Thursday, and a replay of SCN-POSCOM-LV07 on the old map says nothing about routes over R24. ch-04-10 introduced the rule that evidence predating a change to its control is not current (REQ-04-10-003). A verification strategy needs the same rule for releases and map versions, and a way to compute which evidence a change touches.

The operations side has its own stake. Controllers are the first to see when a release behaves differently, and they hold knowledge the test plan lacks: which playbooks use R24, what a delayed grant looks like on the HMI, what the field crew does while an area stays locked. If that knowledge does not reach the verification plan, the plan tests what the supplier thought of.

Standards address the subject at a level this handbook has not verified. ISO 17757:2019, Earth-moving machinery and mining — Autonomous and semi-autonomous machine system safety, gives safety requirements for autonomous and semi-autonomous machines and their systems and infrastructure, hardware and software, across the life cycle [F] (SRC-STD-ISO17757, catalogue level). What it, or any related standard or guidance, expects of verification and validation — levels, methods, independence, records — is not stated here [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AHS-VERIFICATION-EXPECTATIONS). Functional-safety standards such as the IEC 61508 series (SRC-STD-IEC61508, catalogue level) and the road-vehicle ISO 26262 series (SRC-STD-ISO26262, catalogue level [F]: part 1 is ISO 26262-1:2018, for E/E systems in series production road vehicles) organise verification along a safety life cycle [TBV]; their requirements are not paraphrased here. Published methods for scenario-based testing and coverage measurement of automated driving have not been verified in the source register either [TBV] (SRC-PUB-SCENARIO-BASED-TESTING). The levels, methods, allocation rule and record format below are the handbook's own [H].

3. Terminology and foundations

Verification and validation. Verification confirms that the system meets its specified requirements ("built it right"); validation confirms that it meets its intended use in its operational context ("built the right thing") (glossary). In this chapter verification evidence is produced against requirements and registered scenarios; operational records such as positive-communication audits and event reviews are validation evidence and are kept out of the verification matrix, although the safety case uses them (ch-04-10).

Verification level. The scope of the item under test [H]. Component: one function or unit — an ADS module, the field terminal's message acceptance, a remote-stop receiver, an onboard safety-related system controller. Vehicle: one truck with its ADS, VCS, autonomy kit and onboard safety-related systems. System: FMS, AMS, trucks, network and field devices working together. Site: the system in the mine, on real roads and radio, with people and procedures.

Verification method. How evidence is produced [H]: analysis, inspection, simulation, SIL testing (software against simulated environment and plant models), HIL testing (real controller hardware connected to a simulated plant and environment), track test (real trucks in a closed test area), site test (in the live mine under test permissions), and drill (people and system performing a sequence together).

Scenario-based testing. Testing in which the test case is a registered operational scenario with a defined trigger, initial state and key events, so that the same event can be verified at several levels and traced to the chapters that walk it through.

Verification item. One piece of evidence: a test run, an analysis, an inspection record or a drill record, with its level, method, producer, date and the scenarios it covers. An item counts when its record is complete; a planned item does not count.

Test coverage. Here: the share of required (scenario, level) cells that have at least one counted item. A cell is required when the scenario exercises an element that the allocation rule assigns to that level. Coverage counts cells, not tests: a hundred tests of the same branch do not cover another.

Pass/fail criterion (oracle). The condition that decides a test's verdict: an expected end state ("area stays locked") or an invariant that must hold throughout ("no entry without an active permission"). Criteria come from requirements; every numeric limit in them is a site decision [A].

Test record. The structured record of a verification item [H]: case, requirements, configuration (software, map and firmware versions), seed for any stochastic run, expected result, result and sign-off.

Change impact. The set of scenarios and evidence items affected by a change to the system or its map. Evidence affected by a change no longer counts until re-confirmed.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 Levels and methods

FIG-18-01-01 shows the four levels and the methods that can produce evidence at each. The dots are the rule the lab uses (METHODS in LAB-18-01-01) [H].

The levels differ in what they control. At component level a fault can be placed exactly — a GRANT 20.0 s old, a dropped bus frame — and the response observed in detail, thousands of times. At site level almost nothing can be placed: the test waits for the conditions, or stages them at a cost. Moving up the levels, realism, cost and risk of a run increase; control of faults, repeatability and the number of runs fall.

Level Item under test at the reference mine What a result can show What it cannot show
Component ADS localisation module; field-terminal acceptance rules; remote-stop receiver; OSS controller That the unit's logic handles each injected input as specified That the unit works with the others, on the truck, in time
Vehicle DT17 with its autonomy kit, VCS and onboard safety-related systems That one truck stops, follows, reports as specified, with real actuators Fleet interactions, central logic, people
System FMS, AMS, trucks, network and field terminals together Reservations, permissions, reconciliation and recovery across trucks and links Real roads, real coverage, real people's actions
Site AA-North with CR1, real roads, CT1–CT3, GB1, crews That the system, the procedures and the people perform together Rare faults that cannot be staged; anything not tested

The methods map onto the levels unevenly. Analysis can be applied at any level and shows that behaviour has been reasoned through, not that it happens. Inspection checks that something exists and is in condition (a remote-stop functional check, EV-16). Simulation and SIL testing exercise software and models at component or system level; HIL testing puts real controllers on a simulated plant at component or vehicle level; track tests put real trucks in a closed area; site tests and drills happen in the live mine. ch-18-02 explains what SIL testing and HIL testing each add; here only their place in the strategy matters.

4.2 Allocating scenarios to levels

A strategy must say, for every scenario, the levels at which it must be verified. The lab derives that from the architecture elements each registered scenario exercises (the registry's elements field) [H]:

Element exercised Required levels Reason
OSS (onboard safety-related systems) component, vehicle Safety-related hardware must be verified as a unit and installed on the truck
ISF (independent safety functions) component, vehicle The stop path must work regardless of AMS and ADS, so it is verified on its own and on the truck
ADS component, vehicle Driving functions are verified as software modules and on a truck
VCS vehicle Actuation is verified on the truck
AMS, FMS system Central logic only shows its behaviour with several trucks and links
MVD (manned-vehicle and person devices) component, system The terminal's own rules, and its exchange with the AMS
PEOPLE site Procedures and people are verified only where they work

The rule is deliberately crude. It ignores scenarios in which an element appears only as an observer, and it does not weigh scenarios by hazard. Its value is that it is mechanical: nobody decides to skip site verification of SCN-POSCOM-STALE; the registry says that people take part (the controller re-issues), so the site cell is required and an empty cell is a visible gap.

Applied to the registry, the rule gives 93 required cells over 29 scenarios. One registered scenario, SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV, is recorded as not applicable with a reason: the baseline fleet is diesel and no BEV truck operates at the reference mine [A]. Recording "not applicable" with a reason is itself a verification decision; a blank row would look the same as a forgotten one.

Three examples show the rule at work:

  • SCN-POSCOM-STALE exercises AMS, MVD and PEOPLE: component (the terminal rejects a stale grant), system (the AMS keeps the area locked and alerts), site (the controller re-issues correctly).
  • SCN-FIRE-DT17 exercises seven elements and needs all four levels. It needs evidence from analysis, HIL testing and site drills together: no single method covers detection hardware, the controlled stop, the exclusion zone and the ERT.
  • SCN-AMS-RESTART exercises AMS, ADS and FMS: system level for the reconciliation, and component and vehicle levels because the trucks must stop within their granted paths when no AMS message arrives. No people are listed, so no site cell is required — central-system behaviour is verified mainly in simulation and SIL testing.

Read the matrix by column first. The system column is the strongest — 15 of 29 cells — because SIL testing is cheap and the supplier and Site engineering have run it. The vehicle and site columns are the weakest: 3 of 17 and 2 of 24. The mine has verified its central logic and has very little evidence of what its trucks and its people do. Then read by row. Ten scenarios have nothing at any level; several of them belong to subjects — water spray (ch-14-05), shovel moves (ch-13-05), payload (ch-13-04) — that the verification plan did not yet address when it was drawn up, and one, SCN-POSCOM-RADIO-ONLY, was registered from ch-18-07's investigation after this plan was drawn up, and the matrix makes that visible instead of hiding it in a test count.

Two cautions. Coverage is not adequacy: a cell with one supplier test is "covered" in the count and weak in the roll-up; the matrix tells a reviewer where to look, not what is there. The rule sets the denominator: a stricter rule (say, PEOPLE requires a drill, not any site item) makes the same evidence cover less. The number 0.280 is a property of the plan and the rule together [A].

4.4 From verification items to safety-case evidence

The safety case of ch-04-10 is a claim–argument–evidence graph generated from the hazard log; LAB-04-10-01 evaluates it with ahs_sim.safety.SafetyCase. Verification evidence must flow into that case without being copied, re-dated or counted under different rules. LAB-18-01-01 does this in three ways [H]:

  • One register, read in place. The 20 evidence items of the ch-04-10 register that are verification evidence (tests, simulations, analyses, inspections and drills that exercise a scenario) are read from the ch-04-10 file. Their kind, producer and date are not restated; only their link to scenario, level and method is added [A]. A test of the lab checks that the producer and date of every linked item equal the values in the ch-04-10 register, and that its method is compatible with its kind there (a "simulation" may be simulation or SIL testing; a "test" may be any of the testing methods).
  • The same rules. The drill-record rule of ch-04-10 applies unchanged: EV-17, the June remote-stop exercise without actions and sign-off, does not count here either. The age limit is the same (365 days [A]), so the same items are stale: of the five stale items in the ch-04-10 register, the two linked in the verification matrix — EV-05 and EV-15 — are stale here.
  • The same tool. Each required (scenario, level) cell is a claim in an ahs_sim.safety.SafetyCase graph; coverage, single-source claims and stale evidence come from SafetyCase.report. The lab does not use ahs_sim.traceability (Volume 03); as in LAB-04-10-01, the minimal graph of ahs_sim.safety is used instead [A].

What links the two graphs is the control. The verification claim "SCN-POSCOM-STALE is verified at system level" supports the safety-case claim CL-CTL-13 (fail-closed permissions: stale, duplicated or unacknowledged grants count as not granted) through the same evidence items, EV-19 and EV-20. Field logs — the positive-communication audit EV-13, the event review EV-26, the handover audit EV-34, the alarm statistics EV-38 — support control claims in ch-04-10 but are not linked in the verification matrix: they show what happened in operation, not that a scenario was provoked and judged against a criterion.

Why site testing cannot replace lower-level verification. The matrix gives the argument numbers. The site column covers 2 of 24 cells, and no plan could fill the other columns from the site alone, for five reasons:

  1. Faults cannot be placed. A grant that arrives 30.3 s after issue, on purpose, at the moment the driver reads back, cannot be produced on site without modifying the system under test. In SIL testing it is one parameter.
  2. Rare branches need many runs. Behaviour that appears in one configuration out of several — three trucks stopping inside Intersection X at the same restart — is reached in seeded runs, not in a few site days (ch-18-02).
  3. Observation is poor. On site the test sees the outcome. Below it the test sees the state of every component and can check invariants at every step.
  4. Risk to people. Obstacle-response and remote-stop tests on site use targets and test permissions, never people (ch-18-03); many failure responses cannot be tested on site at all without creating the hazard.
  5. A passed site test passes when nothing goes wrong. Thursday's drive on R24 will pass if the map is right and the weather is good. It cannot fail on a stale grant, a lost CT3 or a deadlock that did not happen during the drive.

Site testing adds what nothing else can: real roads, real coverage, real people following real procedures. It is the last link of the chain, and it is weakest exactly where the matrix is weakest today.

4.5 Pass/fail criteria and evidence formats

A scenario-based test is reproducible and traceable only if its record says what was tested, against what, on which version, with which inputs, and what counted as a pass. The handbook's test record has seven fields [H]:

Field Content Example (VI-03)
Case Test case IDs in the suite TC-01 to TC-08, TC-16 to TC-18 (LAB-18-02-01)
Requirements Requirement IDs the criteria come from REQ-02-07-001, REQ-16-03-004, REQ-16-03-006
Configuration Versions of every element involved AMS release R-26.10 [A], terminal firmware F-3.2 [A]
Seed Seeds of every stochastic run (SIL testing, simulation) 1 to 500
Expected The pass/fail criterion No entry without an active permission; 0 invariant breaches
Result Verdict and observed values pass
Signed off by Producer accepting the result Site engineering

A done item counts only when every field is filled [H], exactly as a drill record counts in ch-04-10 only when its seven fields are filled. VI-16, a replay of the cancellation of M-20481, has no seed and no sign-off; the lab excludes it, and SCN-MISSION-CANCEL-M20481 drops into the list of scenarios with no counted test. A result that cannot be reproduced is not evidence; it is an anecdote.

Criteria are written from requirements, in two kinds [H]:

  • End-state criteria: the state the system must reach. SCN-POSCOM-STALE: the permission ends NOT_ACKNOWLEDGED and the area stays locked (REQ-02-07-003; ch-16-03).
  • Invariants: properties that must hold at every step. SCN-POSCOM-STALE: the terminal never allows entry unless the AMS holds the permission active (REQ-02-07-001). An invariant catches a transient wrong state that a correct end state hides; ch-18-02 shows a defect that only an invariant detects.

Numeric limits in criteria — the 15 s age limit at the terminal, the 30 s acknowledgement deadline, the time within which all trucks must show stopped after a remote stop — are site configuration and are [A] here. A criterion copies the configured value with its source; it never invents a safety threshold.

The 19 ch-04-10 items that count in the matrix predate this format. They are counted as ch-04-10 counts them, and the lab lists them as legacy record format: before they are used to accept a release, their records should be completed or re-run in the new format [R].

4.6 Change impact: a map version that opens R24

A change makes evidence out of date where the behaviour it verified could have changed. For a map version, the levels that depend on routes, reservations and permissions — system and site — are affected; component- and vehicle-level evidence, such as a HIL test of the localisation module, is not [H]. The question is which scenarios.

The obvious rule is: a scenario is affected if its registered location contains a changed segment or node. For a map version that opens R24, R25, P3 and V, that rule finds one scenario, SCN-MAP-R05. It misses the point of the west ramp. R24 exists so that traffic can avoid R05: every scenario that closes or blocks a segment can push circuits onto R24.

LAB-18-01-01 therefore adds a second rule, computed from the road network of mine.yaml [H]: a scenario is affected if closing any one of its location segments moves the route of at least one loading-unit circuit onto a changed segment. With R05 closed, all four circuits reroute over R24 (EX01 and EX02 to C01, EX03 and EX04 to D01); with R01 closed, pit traffic reaches X over P3, R25, V and R12. The result:

Quantity Result
Directly affected (location) 1: SCN-MAP-R05
Affected in total (location or rerouting) 16, including SCN-FIRE-DT17 (R05), SCN-POSCOM-LV07 (R05, R06), SCN-COMMS-R05 (R05, R09), SCN-GNSS-R05, SCN-DEADLOCK-X (R01)
Not affected 13, among them SCN-POSCOM-STALE (no segment in its location) and SCN-AMS-RESTART
Items to re-verify (system or site level, dated before the change) 13: EV-01, EV-09, EV-10, EV-12, EV-19, EV-22, EV-23, EV-32, VI-01, VI-02, VI-04, VI-17, VI-20
Covered cells after the change 15 of 93

The table is the answer to the Pit Supervisor's question in section 1.1. Thursday's drive verifies R24 for one truck on one route. The map version changes what the AMS does in 16 scenarios, and the fire drill on R05 (EV-10), the AMS integration test of the exclusion zone and reroute (EV-09) and the SIL-testing cases of CT3 and the deadlock (VI-01, VI-02) were all run on the old map. Unless they are re-run, the change takes the matrix from 26 covered cells to 15.

The rule still under-reports in one direction: a scenario without segments in its location (SCN-POSCOM-STALE lists only AA-North) is never affected, although its permission areas could be. A reviewer adds such scenarios by judgement, and records the reason.

4.7 Trade-offs and limits

  • Rule versus judgement. A mechanical allocation rule makes gaps visible and arguments short; it also requires cells that a reviewer might judge unnecessary. The plan records "not applicable" with a reason rather than weakening the rule.
  • Cells versus depth. A covered cell may hide one weak test. The single-source and stale flags are a first filter; reading the test remains necessary.
  • Reuse versus copying. Reading the ch-04-10 register in place keeps the two cases consistent; it also means that a correction to an item happens in one place, through change control of that register.
  • Fast levels versus real levels. SIL testing fills the system column cheaply. Every cell it fills is a cell where the real radio, the real truck and the real controller were not involved; the vehicle and site columns must be filled separately.
  • The lab's graph is minimal. The lab does not use ahs_sim.traceability (Volume 03); the coverage graph is the flat claim → evidence graph of ahs_sim.safety.SafetyCase, with the level structure built lab-locally [A].

5. Responsibilities and interface boundaries

People site Drills (EV-10), site tests with controllers and crews, operational acceptance checks

Two boundaries matter. Producing evidence is not accepting it. The System supplier and Site engineering produce most items in the plan; the site decides whether they are sufficient, and the safety-case owner [A] accepts a gap for a period or does not. Operations owns its scenarios. Controllers and the Pit Supervisor contribute operational scenarios and acceptance criteria (section 8.3); they do not design the test, but a scenario they raise must appear in the matrix, with its cells, or the plan has silently dropped it.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

This section follows SCN-POSCOM-STALE through the verification strategy. The shared timeline is the scenario clock of the registry: T+0 s is the moment the AMS issues the grant to LV07, which is T+90 s of SCN-POSCOM-LV07. The registry gives T+0 s (grant issued), T+30 s (grant received after its window, rejected as stale) and T+31 s (area remains locked; controller alerted to re-issue). The detailed times come from other chapters, used unchanged: the grant arrives at T+30.3 s with an age of 30.3 s and the AMS acknowledgement deadline passes at T+30.0 s (ch-16-03); the re-issue runs from T+45 s to T+56.2 s (ch-02-07). The times before T+0 s are those of SCN-POSCOM-LV07 shifted by 90 s. Sections 7 and 8 use the same timeline.

T+45 s to T+56.2 s Re-issue: REQUEST, GRANT T+50.3 s, ACK T+55.6 s, confirmed T+56.2 s PEOPLE, AMS, MVD Controller re-issues; new exchange completes system: VI-03; site: gap

Each step has a natural level. The terminal's age rule is a component property: only a component test can show that a GRANT 30.3 s old is rejected for the right reason, and that one 20.0 s old is rejected too (ch-18-02 shows why that second case matters). The persistence of the lock and the alert are system properties, verified in SIL testing with the delay injected on the link. The controller's response at T+31 s and the re-issue are site properties: they depend on how the alert looks on the HMI, how the controller reads it, and how the radio exchange with the driver goes.

The matrix row says: component weak (EV-20), system weak (EV-19 and VI-03, both by Site engineering), site gap. The fail-closed behaviour that the safety case credits as CTL-13 is verified twice in software by one producer, and never with the people who must act on it.

6.3 Degraded operation — accepting a release with an open gap

On Thursday the release is ready and the site cell of SCN-POSCOM-STALE is still empty. Staging a stale grant on site would mean modifying the system under test or waiting for the fault. The plan has three options [H]:

  1. Delay the release until a site exercise of the stale-grant response has been designed and run. It is the cleanest option and costs the release date.
  2. Accept with a recorded gap and a compensating check. The safety-case owner [A] accepts the gap for a stated period; the operational acceptance checks after the release (section 8.3) include a controller exercise of the re-issue on the HMI with LV07 at AP-02, using a deliberately withdrawn request rather than a stale message; the result is recorded as a site item with its own test record.
  3. Restrict the use. Until the site cell is filled, permissions in AA-North are given only with the Pit Supervisor informed of each one, so that a permission left locked after a delayed grant is seen by two people [A].

What the strategy forbids is the fourth option that often happens: accepting because the system-level tests passed and nobody noticed the empty cell. The coverage matrix exists to make that impossible.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

  • Start from the scenario registry, not from the test list. Every registered scenario gets a row; a scenario without tests is visible. The price is that the registry must be maintained, and that operational scenarios raised by controllers must be registered before they can be planned.
  • Derive required levels from the architecture. The rule of section 4.2 is short enough to argue about and mechanical enough to apply to 30 scenarios without debate per row. It over-requires in places; "not applicable" with a reason is the release valve.
  • One evidence register for the safety case and the verification plan. The ch-04-10 register is read in place; new items follow a stricter record format. The price is two formats until the legacy items are re-recorded.
  • Count cells, flag quality. Coverage counts cells; single-source and stale flags mark weak cells. A count of tests passed is reported, if at all, as a footnote.
  • Compute change impact from the road network. The reroute rule finds the 15 scenarios that the location rule misses for R24. It needs the road network and the circuits, both of which the reference mine keeps in mine.yaml.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

On the shared timeline the strategy depends on clocks and versions:

  • T+0 s to T+30.3 s. The criterion "withdrawn at the deadline" is evaluated on the AMS clock; "rejected as stale" on the terminal clock. A test record that gives one time base for both is ambiguous by up to the clock offset (ch-02-07). Records state which clock each time comes from.
  • T+30.0 s versus T+30.3 s. The AMS deadline and the terminal age check are separate defences 0.3 s apart in this run. A test at the registry's 30 s timing exercises both at once and cannot show which one worked; criteria and cases must separate them (ch-18-02, TC-17 and TC-18).
  • T+31 s. The alert to the controller is an HMI event. Its verification needs the HMI version in the configuration field, not only the AMS release.
  • Versions. Every item names the AMS release, map version and terminal firmware it ran against. Without them the change-impact rule cannot tell old evidence from new.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

Before a release, CR1 sees a release note from the supplier and, under this strategy, an evidence summary for operations [H]: for each scenario the controllers rely on, the row of the coverage matrix in words — "SCN-POSCOM-STALE: verified in software at component and system level; not yet exercised with controllers" — and the list of scenarios the change affects. After the release, the controllers see the operational acceptance checklist of section 8.3, with a place to record each result as a site verification item.

The summary is deliberately short. Controllers do not need 93 cells; they need to know which scenarios they handle every week are weakly verified, and which of those the release changes.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — operational acceptance checks after a release

The steps run after the switchover to a new AMS release with the new map version, before normal production resumes in AA-North [H]. Times are illustrative [A]. The procedure adds to the staged resume of ch-10-08 and the return-to-autonomous checks of ch-16-05; it does not replace them.

  1. Pit Supervisor (before the switchover): confirm that the evidence summary lists every affected scenario, that every gap the release carries has an accepted record, and that the compensating checks are in this list.
  2. AHS Controller (switchover): record the AMS release, map version and HMI version shown in CR1 against the release note. A mismatch stops the procedure.
  3. AHS Controller (after reconciliation): check that every truck in AA-North has reported and that no reconciliation conflict is open before the first stage is released (REQ-10-08-005).
  4. AHS Controller with LV07 at AP-02: run one positive-communication exchange end to end and read the permission area on the HMI against the new map; then a deliberately withdrawn request, and check that the area stays locked and the alert to re-issue is presented as expected. Record both as site items.
  5. AHS Controller and Survey: watch the first loaded truck over R24 and the first over R05; compare the routes the HMI shows with the routes in the release's change-impact list.
  6. Pit Supervisor: for each affected playbook (R05 closure, fire on R05), confirm that the controllers on shift have seen what changed.
  7. AHS Controller: record each check with time, version, result and deviation in the test-record format; sign off.
  8. Pit Supervisor: resume normal production, or keep the restriction and inform Site engineering of the deviation.

Contributing scenarios is part of the same cycle. When a controller meets a behaviour the plan does not cover — a grant shown on the terminal after it was withdrawn, a reroute that crosses V at an unexpected time — the Pit Supervisor proposes it for the registry with the event-log extract; once registered it gets its row and its cells.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

The switchover to a new AMS release stops trucks within their granted paths, and they stay stopped while the AMS reconciles; that time is coded DLY-OD-12 (stopped on AMS failover or reconciliation). Trucks held outside an area while a site check runs under a test permission are coded DLY-OD-04 (held for access permission or work zone). Both are Operating Delay under the GMG Time Usage Model [F] (SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020); the codes are handbook conventions [H] (delay-codes.yml, schema 2). The handbook has no separate code for release acceptance: the switchover stop is DLY-OD-12 and the hold for a site check DLY-OD-04, as noted against both codes in delay-codes.yml.

The verification plan has its own indicators [H], read at every release: required cells covered (26 of 93 in the plan's snapshot), scenarios with no counted test (10), site-only scenarios (1), items excluded for incomplete records (2), items in the legacy record format (19), and, for a change, the affected scenarios and items to re-verify. No target is set.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Controllers learn to read an evidence summary and to recognise when a scenario they handle is verified only in software. Pit Supervisors learn to run the operational acceptance checks as verification — with versions, criteria and records — and to turn a field event into a scenario proposal. Site engineers learn to explain a coverage gap to an operations audience without the matrix. Simulator-based training of the new release (ch-16-09) is training, not verification; a session that exercises the stale-grant re-issue can produce a site item only if it is run with a test record against the released HMI.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-18-01-001 [H] The verification plan shall contain one row for every scenario in the scenario registry, with the verification levels the scenario requires derived from the architecture elements it exercises, and shall record a reason for every scenario marked not applicable. Rationale: a scenario without a row cannot show a gap (sections 4.2 and 4.3). Verification: test (LAB-18-01-01 evaluates the 30 scenarios of the plan's registry snapshot and lists every scenario registered later as not yet in the plan); inspection of the plan.

REQ-18-01-002 [H] Before a software release, AHS map version or new autonomous area is accepted, the coverage of required (scenario, level) cells shall be reported with the uncovered, planned-only, single-source and stale-only cells, and the scenarios with no counted test at any level, together with any count of tests passed. Rationale: a test count does not show which behaviour was verified (sections 2 and 4.3). Verification: inspection of the release acceptance record; test (LAB-18-01-01 reference values).

REQ-18-01-003 [H] Every scenario-based test case shall state the requirement IDs its pass/fail criteria come from, an end-state criterion, and, for safety-related behaviour, at least one invariant that must hold throughout the run; numeric limits in a criterion shall reference the configured value and its source. Rationale: an end state can be correct while a transient state is not (section 4.5; ch-18-02). Verification: inspection of the test cases; review of criteria against requirements.

REQ-18-01-004 [H] A verification item shall count as evidence only when its record states the case, requirements, configuration (versions of every element involved), expected result, result and sign-off, and, for SIL testing and simulation, the seed of every run. Rationale: an irreproducible or untraceable result cannot be re-checked or compared after a change (section 4.5). Verification: test (LAB-18-01-01 excludes VI-16); inspection of records.

REQ-18-01-005 [H] For every change to an AHS map, the change-impact analysis shall list the scenarios whose location contains a changed segment or node and the scenarios for which closing one of their segments routes a circuit over a changed segment, and shall mark system- and site-level evidence for those scenarios dated before the change as not current until re-run. Rationale: the location rule alone finds 1 of the 16 scenarios a map version that opens R24 affects (section 4.6). Verification: test (LAB-18-01-01 change-impact reference values); inspection of the change record.

REQ-18-01-006 [H] Site-level evidence shall fill only site-level cells; a site test or drill shall not be counted as evidence for a component, vehicle or system cell. Rationale: site tests cannot place faults, reach rare branches or observe component states (section 4.4). Verification: inspection of the plan; test (the method–level rule of LAB-18-01-01).

REQ-18-01-007 [H] After a software release or map version is deployed, normal production in an affected area shall resume only after the operational acceptance checks have been run and recorded as site verification items with versions, criteria, results and sign-off, including a check of every compensating measure the release acceptance relies on. Rationale: the site level is the only level at which people and procedures are verified (sections 6.3 and 8.3). Verification: inspection of the acceptance check records; drill.

REQ-18-01-008 [H] Operational scenarios raised by the control room shall be proposed for the scenario registry with the event-log extract of the event, and a registered operational scenario shall appear in the verification plan with its required cells before the next release is accepted. Rationale: controllers see behaviours the supplier's test list does not contain (sections 2 and 8.3). Verification: inspection of the registry and plan change records.

10. Verification and validation

Verification. make test CH=ch-18-01 checks that every registered scenario is evaluated; that the linked ch-04-10 items keep the producer and date of the ch-04-10 register, use a method compatible with their kind, and follow the exclusion and age rules of ch-04-10; the allocation and method–level rules; the summary, by-level, by-method and key-row values; the change-impact values for R24; the three parameter experiments; and that the figure sources exist and FIG-18-01-01 shows the lab's method–level rule.

Validation. Whether the strategy is adequate is a judgement, not a count. Reviewers validate it by asking whether the allocation rule requires the right levels for the hazards that matter most, whether "not applicable" reasons hold, whether covered cells contain tests that actually provoke the scenario, and whether operations recognises the scenarios it handles. The reference mine is fictional, so none of that is possible here; ch-18-03 to ch-18-05 continue the subject at vehicle, site, commissioning and readiness level.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Planning from the test list. Tests are added where the supplier has a rig, not where the scenarios are.
  • One level per scenario. "Fire on DT17 is covered by the drill", while the detection-to-stop chain has never been tested on the controllers.
  • Evidence without versions. A test that names no release cannot be marked out of date by a change.
  • Location-only change impact. A map version that opens a bypass changes every scenario that can close the main road.
  • Criteria written after the run. The expected result is whatever the system did.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Accepting a release on the supplier's word. The site, not the supplier, owns the decision and the evidence summary.
  • Treating the commissioning drive as verification of everything. It verifies what it drove.
  • Acceptance checks without records. The checks happened; the evidence did not.
  • Keeping field surprises in the shift log. A behaviour the plan does not know about stays unknown to the plan.

12. Worked examples

All values are computed by LAB-18-01-01 with ahs_sim 0.9.0; every item, date and assignment is [A]; the evaluation date is 2026-11-02 and the age limit 365 days [A], as in ch-04-10.

Example 1 — the denominator. 30 registered scenarios, 1 not applicable. Required cells from the element rule: 93 (component 23, vehicle 17, system 29, site 24). Counted items: 19 ch-04-10 items (20 linked, EV-17 excluded) plus 9 Volume 18 items (10 done, VI-16 excluded) = 28.

Example 2 — coverage. 26 cells have counted evidence: C = 26 / 93 = 0.280. Of the 26, 23 are weak (single source or stale only) and 3 are supported by two producers and not by stale evidence alone: SCN-FIRE-DT17 vehicle (EV-05, EV-08), system (EV-01, EV-09), SCN-POSCOM-LV07 system (EV-12, EV-19).

Example 3 — ages. EV-05, dated 2025-06-01: a = 519 days > 365, stale; it still shares the SCN-FIRE-DT17 vehicle cell with EV-08 (242 days), so the cell is covered. EV-15, dated 2025-02-15: a = 625 days, stale, and the only item in the SCN-PERSON-EX01 vehicle cell — weak, resting on stale evidence only.

Example 4 — completing a record. Add seed 12 and the System supplier's sign-off to VI-16 and re-run: covered cells 27; SCN-MISSION-CANCEL-M20481 system becomes weak; only EV-17 remains excluded.

Example 5 — the age limit is a decision. With 730 days [A], no item is stale and no cell rests on stale evidence only; coverage stays 26, because staleness marks cells weak, it does not uncover them.

Example 6 — a planned site test done. Record VI-09 (remote-stop function test in AA-North) as done with a complete record: SCN-RSTOP-AANORTH site becomes weak (one producer) and coverage rises to 27.

Example 7 — the R24 map version. Location rule: 1 scenario. Reroute rule: 16, including SCN-MAP-R05; 16 in total. Items at system or site level dated before 2026-11-02 for those scenarios: 13. Coverage after the change: 15 of 93.

15. Key takeaways

  • Start the strategy from the scenario registry: every scenario gets a row, and a scenario with no test is visible.
  • Required levels come from the elements a scenario exercises; at the reference mine that gives 93 cells, of which 26 are covered (0.280), 23 of them weakly.
  • The system column is strong (15 of 29) and the vehicle and site columns are weak (3 of 17, 2 of 24): central logic is verified, trucks and people much less.
  • Verification evidence feeds the safety case through the same register, the same rules and the same tool as ch-04-10.
  • Site testing cannot replace lower levels: it cannot place faults, reach rare branches or observe states, and it passes when nothing goes wrong.
  • A result counts only with a complete, reproducible record; criteria come from requirements and include invariants.
  • A map version that opens R24 affects 16 scenarios, not 1; 13 items must be re-run, or coverage falls to 15 of 93.
  • Operations demands an evidence summary before a release and runs recorded acceptance checks after it.
