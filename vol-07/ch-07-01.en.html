1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Mid-morning on a DAY shift the Pit Supervisor parks at the crest P1 and watches the main ramp. The sign at the top of R05 says 40 km/h. Empty trucks come down at about that speed; loaded trucks come up at a little over 11 km/h, as they do every shift. Then DT17 appears, loaded on M-20481, at about 7 km/h, with two loaded trucks closed up behind it. The Pit Supervisor calls the control room: "Is there a restriction on R05? Seventeen is crawling and holding up the two behind it." The restriction list shows nothing on R05. A minute and a half later the AHS Controller has the answer from DT17's platform state — propulsion power limited to 60 % for a drive-system temperature — and the Maintenance Planner is asking whether DT17 can finish its load.

The call raises the questions this chapter answers:

  • Why do loaded trucks climb R05 at 11 km/h when the limit is 40 km/h, and would anything make them faster?
  • What changes when a truck loses 40 % of its power, and why does it slow the trucks behind it?
  • How does a controller tell a propulsion derate from an ADS speed restriction before deciding how to record it?
  • What do an overload, a rougher ramp or a closure of R05 cost per cycle and per shift?
  • How far can the steady-state cycle model of Volume 01 be trusted?

This chapter covers:

  • the longitudinal equation of motion of a 220 t class truck on grade, with grade and rolling resistance and the effective (rotating) mass, and what the steady-state model of ch-01-01 (ahs_sim.truck) leaves out;
  • Rimpull and retarding curves and the operating point of a truck on R05, R24 and the flat roads;
  • a time-stepped model of the whole M-20481 route compared segment by segment with the steady-state model;
  • the effect of payload, rolling resistance and the R24 reroute on climb time, cycle time and shovel utilisation;
  • the propulsion derate of SCN-DERATE-DT17 seen from the vehicle and from the control room.

Every Rimpull, retarding, effective-mass, axle-share and acceleration value is an illustrative assumption [A] — the defaults of ahs_sim.dynamics — and the curves are generic shapes, not any manufacturer's performance chart. Energy and fuel per cycle are the subject of ch-09-01; this chapter stops at forces, speed and time.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Derive the longitudinal equation of motion of an HT220 truck including grade resistance, rolling resistance and effective (rotating) mass, and state which terms the steady-state model in ahs_sim.truck neglects.
  2. Calculate acceleration, time-to-speed and steady-state speed of DT17 on the R05 climb, loaded and empty, from an assumed Rimpull–speed curve and the equipment.yaml parameters [A].
  3. Analyse how payload variation (including an overload such as SCN-PAYLOAD-OVER-EX02) and changes in rolling resistance move the operating point on the Rimpull–grade chart and the resulting R05 climb time.
  4. Compare the loaded climb of the main ramp R05 (~9 %) with the longer, gentler west ramp R24 (~6.3 %) in climb speed, climb time and drivetrain load, and determine under which payload and rolling-resistance assumptions each complete route is faster [A].
  5. Quantify the error of the steady-state cycle model against the time-stepped model on every M-20481 segment, and state when acceleration phases can no longer be neglected.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Use a Rimpull–speed–grade chart to explain to a Pit Supervisor why loaded trucks on R05 climb well below the 40 km/h site limit.
  2. Estimate the change in M-20481 cycle time and shift productivity caused by a rolling-resistance increase on R05, and use it to argue road-maintenance priority.
  3. Distinguish, from telemetry and HMI indications, a platform propulsion derate (SCN-DERATE-DT17) from an ADS speed restriction before choosing the delay code.
  4. Estimate for the Pit Supervisor the cycle-time penalty of hauling EX02 ore over R24 instead of R05 (e.g. during R05 road maintenance) and decide how many trucks the pit shovels can still keep busy.
  5. Specify the per-shift ramp-performance view operations needs (climb time by truck, payload, grade position, power state) to flag trucks that climb consistently slower than the fleet.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Every ore load at the reference mine is lifted 120 m, from the pit floor at RL 300 to the crest P1 at RL 420 (mine.yaml). M-20481 takes DT17 from EX02 over R08 and R06 to the ramp toe P2, up R05 to P1 and on to C01 over R01, R02 and R21, 4500.5 m loaded. In the steady-state model of ch-01-01 DT17 spends 421.4 s of its 1564.1 s cycle on R05 — more than a quarter of the cycle on 30 % of the loaded distance. Whatever slows the climb, slows production.

Volume 01 gave that climb one number, 11.4 km/h, from one equation: wheel power divided by the force needed to lift the truck and overcome rolling resistance. The equation is right, and every cycle number in the handbook is based on it. But four questions keep coming back from the pit.

"Why so slow?" Visitors and supervisors new to the site often suspect the autonomous system of being cautious on the ramp. A controller who can show the operating point on a Rimpull chart ends the discussion and prevents the wrong fix — such as raising a speed limit that loaded trucks never reach.

"Is it the truck or the system?" In SCN-DERATE-DT17 the vehicle limits its own power; in SCN-GNSS-R05 the ADS limits its speed because localisation is degraded (ch-05-06). Both look like "a slow truck on R05" and lead to different people, actions and records. Derated and restricted running carry no delay code (ch-04-06); the cause must still be recorded, or the cost disappears into "slow cycles".

"What does the road cost us?" Rolling resistance is a road property the mine controls through grading, watering and drainage (ch-14-04, ch-14-05). Expressed in loads per shift, road maintenance competes on equal terms with other work.

"What if R05 is closed?" Closing R05 sends all pit traffic over the west ramp R24 and adds 236.0 s to every M-20481 cycle (ch-01-03). Is that a fixed cost, and how many trucks does EX02 then need?

Underneath sits an engineering question: when must the acceleration of a 385 t vehicle be modelled? Volume 07 adds a time-stepped model, ahs_sim.dynamics. It must agree with Volume 01 where Volume 01 is right, and explain where they differ.

3. Terminology and foundations

Grade and grade resistance. The road grade G is rise over horizontal run, signed in the direction of travel (R05 is +8.99 % from P2 to P1, mine.yaml). The grade resistance of a truck of mass m is the component of its weight along the road, mgsinθ≈mgG for the small angles of haul roads; the handbook model uses the small-angle form [H].

Rolling resistance. The force needed to keep a tyre rolling, as a fraction RR of the weight (glossary). Loose material, ruts, water and soft spots raise it. The reference mine assumes 0.025 on most roads and 0.03 on the ramps R05 and R24 [A].

Total resistance. Grade plus rolling resistance, mg(G+RR). The sum G+RR is often quoted as an "effective grade": loaded on R05 it is 0.0899 + 0.03 = 0.1199, about 12 %.

Tractive effort and Rimpull. Tractive effort is the force with which the drive wheels push the truck forward; Rimpull is the tractive effort available at the rim of the driving wheels as a function of speed (glossary). The model's Rimpull curve has two parts: a low-speed cap (motor torque or current limits) and, above a corner speed, the available wheel power divided by speed, ηP/v. For the HT220 (1800 kW, efficiency 0.80, equipment.yaml) the wheel power is 1440 kW; with the cap of 900 kN [A] the corner speed is 5.76 km/h. Real charts have more structure, and their shape and values are manufacturer data [TBV].

Retarding. Continuous braking used to hold speed downhill (glossary); a diesel-electric truck's drive motors act as generators. The model's retarding curve has the same two-part shape (2,700 kW, cap 900 kN [A]). Braking is ch-07-02.

Operating point and steady-state speed. On a constant grade the truck settles where Rimpull equals total resistance: the operating point. The speed there is the steady-state speed (glossary). It is power-limited on the hyperbola, speed-limited if the hyperbola meets the resistance above the speed limit, and the truck cannot climb at all if the cap lies below the resistance.

Effective mass. Accelerating a truck also accelerates its wheels, tyres, motor rotors and gears. Their inertia acts like extra mass; the effective mass meff gives the same kinetic energy. The model uses meff=1.05m [A].

Derate, speed derating, restriction. A derate is an automatic reduction of a machine's performance (glossary). This chapter separates a propulsion derate — the VCS limits drive power, e.g. for a drive-system temperature — from speed derating by the ADS (e.g. on degraded localisation) and speed restrictions applied through the AMS. Section 4.7 shows why the difference matters.

Time-stepped and steady-state models. A time-stepped model integrates the equation of motion in small steps (0.1 s here) and so includes acceleration and braking; the steady-state model of ch-01-01 gives each segment one speed.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The equation of motion and what the steady-state model leaves out

Figure FIG-07-01-01 shows the forces on DT17 climbing R05 loaded. Along the road, Newton's second law for the truck as a point mass with rotating parts reads

meffdvdt=Fdrive(v)−Fbrake−mg(G+RR)

with the propulsion force limited by the Rimpull curve,

Fdrive≤min(Fmax, ηPkv)

where Fmax is the low-speed cap, ηP the wheel power and k the fraction of power available (1 normally, 0.60 in SCN-DERATE-DT17). Rolling resistance is the sum over both axles; at small grades the axle loads add up to the weight, so it is RR·mg whatever the load split.

For DT17 loaded (385 t), LAB-07-01-01 gives on R05: grade resistance 339.7 kN, rolling resistance 113.3 kN, total 453.0 kN — 50.3 % of the 900 kN cap. The drive axle carries 2530 kN of the 3777 kN weight (share 0.67 [A]); that load matters for traction (ch-07-02), not for the climb in dry conditions.

At steady speed dv/dt=0 and no brake acts, so on the hyperbola

vss=ηPkmg(G+RR)

For k=1 this is the steady-state formula of ch-01-01: 1 440 000 W / 453 000 N = 3.18 m/s = 11.44 km/h. ahs_sim.dynamics is built so that its steady speed equals ahs_sim.truck.segment_speed_kmh in the power region; the test suite of ahs_sim 0.13.0 checks it for three payloads and three grades.

What the steady-state model neglects. Comparing the two equations term by term:

  • The inertia term meffdv/dt. The steady-state model jumps from one segment speed to the next. Accelerating from rest, slowing for a lower limit, and carrying speed into a climb are all invisible to it.
  • The Rimpull cap Fmax. The steady-state model assumes the hyperbola holds at every speed, so it would let a truck climb any grade, slowly. The cap tells us that a loaded HT220 cannot climb an effective grade above 900 / 3777 = 23.8 % (climb_regime reports "rimpull"); R05's 12 % is far from it.
  • Braking. The steady-state model has no brake force and no deceleration limit; downhill speeds come from the fixed caps of equipment.yaml (30 km/h loaded, 40 km/h empty [A]).
  • The effective mass. The steady-state model has no inertia, so it has no rotating inertia either.
  • Traction. Neither model in this chapter limits the force by adhesion; ch-07-02 does.

In the feasible power-limited regime, inertia (including the effective mass) changes the transient response without changing the steady speed; it changes the time. The Rimpull and adhesion (ch-07-02) limits can instead make the climb infeasible: they change whether the truck can climb, not the value of its steady speed.

The table answers the Pit Supervisor's question. On R05 the loaded truck needs 453 kN to hold its speed; at 1440 kW that force can be sustained only up to 3.18 m/s. The limit on the loaded climb is power, not the speed limit: the 40 km/h sign is three and a half times higher than the speed the truck can reach. Raising or lowering the R05 limit above 11.4 km/h has no effect on loaded trucks; only more power, less mass or less resistance would.

The flat-road line shows the opposite case: on R01 a loaded truck could reach 54.9 km/h on power alone, so the 50 km/h limit binds. That closeness matters for acceleration (section 4.4).

Panel (b) shows retarding effort against the net downhill force on the R05 descent: 97 kN for an empty truck, 226 kN for a loaded truck (a bounding case; no production route descends R05 loaded, ch-04-05). With the module's retarding curve [A] both lines lie below the curve at 30 and 40 km/h, so the downhill caps of equipment.yaml, not the retarding curve, set the descent speeds in this chapter. Ch-07-02 develops the braking side, including stopping distance, which in practice limits the descent.

4.3 Acceleration and time to speed on R05

The steady speed tells the planner how fast; it does not tell how long it takes to get there. LAB-07-01-01 starts DT17 at rest at P2 — as after a hold at the ramp toe — with the module's speed policy: accelerate at the comfort limit of 0.6 m/s² [A] while the Rimpull allows, then follow the curve to the steady speed.

  • Loaded. The truck needs 0.6 × 1.05 × 385 t = 242.6 kN for the acceleration plus 453.0 kN for the resistance: 695.6 kN, below the cap, so it starts at the full 0.60 m/s². It runs on the cap for 2.6 s; above the corner speed the force and the acceleration fall quickly. It reaches 95 % of 11.44 km/h after 8.0 s and 15.9 m, and the climb takes 424.6 s against 421.4 s in the steady-state model: +3.2 s.
  • Empty (an analysis case only). Target 26.70 km/h, reached to 95 % after 18.9 s and 88.3 m; climb 188.0 s against 180.6 s: +7.4 s. A faster target costs more time to reach.

Accelerating from rest to v at constant a covers v2/(2a) in v/a; travelling that distance at v would take v/(2a). The time lost is therefore about

Δt≈v2a

— 3.5 s for 15 km/h, 9.3 s for 40 km/h and 11.6 s for 50 km/h at 0.6 m/s². Where the acceleration falls below the comfort limit as the truck approaches a power-limited speed, the true loss is larger (section 4.4).

Effective mass. With the factor 1.05 the R05 climb takes 424.6 s; with 1.00, 424.5 s. Here the comfort limit, not the force, sets the acceleration, so rotating inertia costs force (11.6 kN of the 242.6 kN) rather than time. Where the truck accelerates on the force limit, the factor slows it in proportion. Whoever sizes acceleration demands, wheel-slip margins (ch-07-02) or controller gains (ch-07-06) must include it; a cycle-time estimate may not need it.

The empty return takes 515.7 s against 503.1 s (+12.6 s); R05 is identical there (120.6 s at the 40 km/h cap). With the same fixed times (257.0 s) the cycle grows from 1564.1 s to 1592.3 s, +28.2 s or +1.8 %. Figure FIG-07-01-03 shows the speed along the route; two segments carry the story.

R05 is quicker. DT17 arrives at P2 at 40 km/h from R06. The steady-state model drops it to 11.4 km/h at the ramp toe; the time-stepped truck keeps its kinetic energy and slows over the first metres of the climb, gaining 15.8 s. The model has no corners; planning.speed_profile in ch-07-05 shows the hairpin at P2, so this gain is an upper bound.

R01 is much slower. Leaving R05 at 11.4 km/h, the truck accelerates towards 50 km/h, but its power-limited flat speed is 54.9 km/h: the closer it gets, the less force is left, and the approach is slow. It reaches only 45.4 km/h before braking for R02's 40 km/h. The estimate v/(2a) = 11.6 s understates the loss (17.7 s).

When acceleration can no longer be neglected. The steady-state model's error is large on (1) short segments compared with the distance needed to reach their speed — R18, R08, R21 and any segment between stops; (2) targets close to the power-limited speed — R01 here; and (3) where speed is carried into a climb or lost before it — R05. Over a cycle the effects partly cancel (+1.8 %). For one segment — a climb after a stop at P2, an intersection crossing, the time a truck holds a reservation block (ch-10-04) — the segment error is what matters.

The reference stays the steady-state model. The handbook's cycle numbers (M-20481 1564.1 s, the reroute 1800.1 s) were computed with the steady-state model and remain its reference. Time-stepped results are additional: they show what the steady-state numbers leave out and in which direction.

4.6 R05 against R24

Ch-12-05 explained why the gentler west ramp is not quicker. At constant wheel power the climb time is the work done divided by the power,

tclimb=mg(G+RR)LηP=mgh+mgRRLηP

where h=GL is the height gained. On R05 LAB-07-01-02 finds 455.1 MJ of lift and 151.8 MJ of rolling work: 421.4 s at 1.44 MW. The reroute lifts the truck 30.1 m on R09 and 90.2 m on R24 — the same 120 m — but over 1887.0 m instead of 1339.6 m: 113.5 + 340.6 MJ of lift and 43.6 + 161.5 MJ of rolling work, 457.8 s. The lift is identical; the difference is rolling resistance on a longer road.

For objective 4 the comparison gives:

  • Climb speed and drivetrain load. On R24 the loaded truck climbs faster (14.71 against 11.44 km/h) with less tractive effort (352.3 against 453.0 kN): less load per metre, for longer.
  • Whole route, every payload. The R24 route is slower at every payload from 110 t to 260 t, by 225.6 s to 239.9 s per cycle (section 4.5): the extra rolling work on the longer road grows with the mass, so the gap widens with payload.
  • Rolling resistance. Raising it everywhere widens the gap (+257.1 s at +0.01). Raising it on R05 alone narrows it: the routes are equally fast only at RR 0.0972 on R05, more than three times the assumed 0.03. Long before that the mine would grade the ramp, or close it for braking and traction reasons (ch-07-02).
  • Time-stepped. The loaded legs via R24 take 925.4 s against 908.6 s in the steady-state model (+16.8 s), via R05 819.6 s against 804.0 s: the 236.0 s difference is not an artefact of the steady-state model.

So R05 is faster under every payload and rolling-resistance assumption the reference mine is likely to see. The value of R24 lies elsewhere: it keeps the pit connected when R05 is closed (ch-01-03) and stays inside the wet-road ODD when R05 does not (ch-04-02, ch-12-05).

Shovel utilisation. In the deterministic match of ch-01-05 a shovel needs cycle time ÷ service time trucks; EX02's service time is 35 s spotting + 132 s loading = 167 s. Via R05 that is 9.37 trucks, via R24 10.78. EX02's nine trucks (DT11–DT19) keep it 96.1 % busy via R05 and 83.5 % via R24. During an R05 closure the question is therefore also where the extra trucks per pit shovel come from — for the FMS and the Pit Supervisor (ch-07-03, ch-10-07).

4.7 Propulsion derate and speed restriction

A propulsion derate limits the power the drive can deliver. In the model it multiplies the hyperbola by k and keeps the low-speed cap (with_derate): a derated truck can still start on a grade, but it cannot hold its normal speed wherever it needs power. A speed restriction or ADS speed derating caps the speed the ADS will command; it changes nothing in the drive.

Their signatures on the M-20481 cycle are opposite (LAB-07-01-01):

Condition Loaded climb R05 Empty descent R05 Loaded on R01 M-20481 cycle
Normal 11.44 km/h 40.00 km/h 50.00 km/h 1564.1 s
Speed restriction R05 ≤ 20 km/h (SCN-GNSS-R05) 11.44 km/h 20.00 km/h 50.00 km/h 1684.6 s
Propulsion derate to 60 % (SCN-DERATE-DT17) 6.87 km/h 40.00 km/h 32.94 km/h 1895.9 s
  • The restriction costs 120.6 s per cycle — the value of ch-04-02 — and all of it is on the empty descent: the loaded climb at 11.4 km/h is already below 20 km/h. It affects every truck on R05 alike.
  • The derate costs 331.8 s per cycle if it lasts the whole cycle, all of it on loaded legs where power is needed — R05, but also R06, R01 and R02, where 60 % of the power gives only 32.94 km/h on the flat. The empty legs need little power and are unchanged (503.1 s). It affects one truck — and those that cannot pass it.

What the vehicle reports. A derated truck is not obeying a speed command; it cannot reach it. A good platform state [H] reports the derate as a capability: power fraction, cause, start time. The ADS needs that capability for planning — a derated truck takes longer to clear an intersection or to leave a reservation block, and it accelerates more slowly after a stop. The AMS and the HMI need it to explain the slow truck. Section 7 develops these interfaces.

4.8 Trade-offs and model limits

  • One point mass, straight legs: no corners (ch-07-05), no load transfer, no tyre slip (ch-07-02).
  • Generic curves: Rimpull, retarding, effective mass and axle shares are [A]; real curves depend on drive configuration, temperature and altitude and come from the manufacturer [TBV].
  • Simple speed policy: comfort limits of 0.6 and 0.8 m/s² [A] stand in for ch-07-05 to ch-07-07.
  • No thermal model: the derate of SCN-DERATE-DT17 is imposed, not predicted.
  • No energy or fuel: the work figures of section 4.6 explain time; energy per cycle is ch-09-01.

Two boundaries deserve emphasis. The ADS does not override the vehicle: it plans within what the VCS reports it can do. The controller does not "fix" a derate from the console: a derate is a vehicle-side protection, and only the maintenance process decides whether the truck may continue (section 8.2).

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — DT17 climbs R05 on M-20481

In the steady-state timeline of ch-01-01, DT17 reaches the ramp toe P2 at T+340 s of its cycle and the crest P1 at T+762 s, 421 s on the ramp at 11.4 km/h. Traffic on R05 is a line of loaded trucks in the climbing lane, each at full power, and empty trucks descending in the other lane at the 40 km/h cap. Because every loaded truck with the same payload climbs at the same speed, the spacing between them is set at the ramp toe and stays constant up the ramp. A truck that climbs slower than the fleet — by payload, rolling resistance under its wheels or loss of power — breaks that pattern. That is the signal operations should look for.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-DERATE-DT17 (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

Initial state (registry). DT17, loaded on M-20481, is climbing R05 about 400 m above P2 at its full-power speed of 11.44 km/h. Two loaded trucks follow on R05 [A]: DT14 at 230 m and DT13 at 60 m above P2, 170 m apart. Empty trucks descend in the other lane.

Under the coding rules (ch-04-06, REQ-16-07-009), a truck that keeps running derated gets no delay code: what the controller records at T+90 s is the interval DT17 derated — propulsion, VCS, with its cause and start time. Downtime (DLY-DN-01) begins only when DT17 is withdrawn for inspection (section 8.4).

What the derate costs. DT17 needs 195.6 s longer for the rest of R05 and, if it stays derated, 232.8 s longer for its whole remaining loaded trip to C01. DT14 and DT13 lose 173.5 s and 151.4 s on the ramp; once DT17 turns onto R01 they climb the last metres at full speed and accelerate away. Three loaded trucks lose about 8.7 minutes between them on one ramp. Had the derate persisted for a full cycle, DT17's cycle would be 1895.9 s and it would complete 22 loads in 12 h instead of 27.

6.3 Abnormal operation — SCN-PAYLOAD-OVER-EX02

At EX02 the payload system reports 250 t (ch-01-01). On R05 the overloaded truck climbs at 10.62 km/h; a following truck with 220 t closes up on it at 0.82 km/h. The overload is a slow truck without a derate: the platform reports full power, the payload report explains the speed. The controller's check (section 8.2) distinguishes it from SCN-DERATE-DT17 in one look at the payload. What happens to the overloaded truck is a payload-policy decision (ch-13-04, ch-08-05).

6.4 Degraded operation — SCN-GNSS-R05 and an R05 closure

In SCN-GNSS-R05 the ADS on DT17 derates its speed when localisation integrity drops near the highwall at the lower part of R05, and at T+60 s the controller applies a 20 km/h restriction to R05 through the AMS. For loaded trucks the restriction changes nothing — they climb at 11.4 km/h anyway. Empty trucks descend at 20 km/h instead of 40 km/h, and each M-20481 cycle grows by 120.6 s. A loaded truck that is also slow on the climb in this scenario has a second cause.

If R05 is closed for road maintenance or by an exclusion zone (SCN-FIRE-DT17), the pit hauls over R09–R24–R25–R12: M-20481 grows to 1800.1 s and 5865.1 m (ch-01-03), EX02 needs 10.78 trucks to stay busy, and its nine keep it 83.5 % busy. The reroute is a planned degraded state; the controller should still expect slower climbs on R24 than the reference numbers if the reroute's rolling resistance is poor or the trucks are overloaded.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Walk the SCN-DERATE-DT17 timeline and ask what the design must decide in advance.

T+0 s — the derate is a vehicle-side protection. The VCS limits power because a drive-system condition requires it [A]. No central element may lift or override that limit; the ADS and the AMS adapt to it, as central permissions never override vehicle-side safety decisions (ch-01-04 section 4.6, principle 1). Keeping power to keep traffic moving would trade a production loss for a damage or fire risk that the vehicle designer, not the dispatcher, must judge.

T+0 s — report capability, not just a flag. "Drive fault" says something is wrong; a capability report — power fraction or derated Rimpull curve, cause, expected duration if known — lets the ADS plan and the AMS predict. The cost is a richer interface and a rule for how the ADS uses it.

T+2 s — the ADS plans within the capability. If the ADS kept commanding 11.4 km/h, the speed controller would saturate and a tracking monitor might stop the truck on the ramp (ch-07-06). The capability must also reach the intersection and reservation logic: at 6.87 km/h DT17 occupies each block 1.67 times as long as at 11.44 km/h (ch-10-04) and needs longer to clear an intersection after a stop.

T+60 s — followers. Following behaviour (ch-07-04) keeps DT14 and DT13 at a safe gap. Holding trucks at P2 so that they climb later at full speed gains nothing on a ramp without passing: they reach P1 when DT17 lets them.

Model fidelity. The steady-state model is transparent, reproducible and within about 2 % for M-20481; a time-stepped model adds acceleration but needs more assumptions. The handbook's position [H]: plan with the steady-state model calibrated against logged segment times; use time-stepped models where acceleration is the question (intersection crossing, reservation hold times, short segments, restarts on ramps); never mix their numbers without saying which is which.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

  • VCS → ADS: capability. The derate is reported in the platform-state cycle. A delay of seconds is harmless for a climbing truck (its speed falls by itself within 8.1 s) but matters for a truck about to enter an intersection.
  • ADS → AMS: truck state. At T+2 s the AMS receives derated — platform with the power fraction and must keep it distinct from speed derated — localisation (ADS) and from its own restrictions: three causes, each set by a different element (the VCS, the ADS, and the controller through the AMS).
  • AMS → HMI and FMS. The AMS opens a mode interval; the FMS receives the reduced capability so that it can predict the cycle (T+600 s).
  • Followers. DT14 closes up 57.6 s after the derate, DT13 after 112.9 s: an alarm built on bunching arrives about a minute after the cause, the platform report at once. Controllers look at the platform state first (section 8.3).
  • Recording. The mode interval starts at the platform time stamp (T+0 s), not at the acknowledgement (T+90 s).

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

At T+2 s the HMI [H] shows DT17's truck card with derated — platform: propulsion 60 %, drive temperature and a speed of 6.9 km/h against an expected 11.4 km/h for its payload and grade. The restriction list for R05 is empty. At T+60 s two more trucks on R05 show following at the same speed. The ramp view — trucks on R05 by position and speed — shows the bunch moving up the ramp.

8.2 Who decides what

Decision tree for a slow truck on a ramp [H]:

  1. Is a restriction or ADS speed derating active on the segment? (restriction list; truck state speed derated) → yes: the speed is set by a rule. For a loaded climb on R05, a cap above 11.4 km/h cannot explain slowness; look further. For a descent it can.
  2. Does the platform state report a propulsion derate? → yes: vehicle cause. Record the interval with the cause; inform the Maintenance Planner; check the trucks behind.
  3. Is the payload above rated? → yes: mass cause; inform the Shovel Operator (SCN-PAYLOAD-OVER-EX02); payload policy applies.
  4. Are other trucks slow at the same place? → yes: road cause (rolling resistance, soft spot); Pit Supervisor arranges an inspection and grading.
  5. None of these → the truck may be degrading without reporting it: flag it in the ramp-performance view and inform the Maintenance Planner.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — slow loaded truck on R05 (SCN-DERATE-DT17)

The steps follow the shared timeline of section 6.2.

  1. AHS Controller (T+2 s to T+60 s): when a truck card shows derated — platform, or when trucks bunch on a ramp, open the slow truck's card. Compare its speed with the expected speed for its payload and grade (R05 loaded: 11.4 km/h).
  2. AHS Controller (by T+60 s): check the restriction list for the segment and the truck's ADS state. Confirm that no restriction or ADS speed derating explains the speed.
  3. AHS Controller (T+90 s): confirm the platform derate (power fraction and cause). Record the interval derated — propulsion, VCS from the platform time stamp. Do not code it as a delay. Note the following trucks.
  4. AHS Controller (T+90 s): inform the Maintenance Planner: truck, cause, power fraction, position, load state. Inform the Pit Supervisor that R05 traffic is slowed behind DT17.
  5. Maintenance Planner (by T+600 s): decide whether DT17 finishes its load and when it is inspected. If it finishes, tell the controller where it must go after C01 (e.g. PK1 or the workshop).
  6. AHS Controller (T+600 s): have the FMS remove DT17 from EX02's assignments after this load; check the effect on EX02 (eight trucks: shovel about 85 % busy) and tell the Shovel Operator.
  7. AHS Controller (at withdrawal): start DLY-DN-01 for DT17 when it is withdrawn; close the derated-running interval.
  8. AHS Controller (end of shift): check the ramp-performance view: DT17's climb, the followers' climbs and whether other trucks show slow climbs without a recorded cause.

Never do: lift or "reset" a derate from the console to restore speed; apply a speed restriction to "explain" a slow truck; code the followers' slow climbs as a delay.

KPIs: Physical Availability falls by DT17's Downtime; productivity per truck and tonnes per hour fall through longer cycles (a derated cycle of 1895.9 s gives 22 loads per 12 h instead of 27); Operating Efficiency is unaffected by derated running, which is why the mode intervals must be recorded separately. 8.5 Training and competency implications

  • AHS Controllers must read a Rimpull chart well enough to say why loaded trucks climb R05 at 11.4 km/h, to tell a derate from a restriction from an overload, and to record each correctly. Exercise: three slow trucks on R05, one derated, one overloaded, one behind them.
  • Pit Supervisors need the road-maintenance argument in loads per shift (section 4.5) and the cost of an R05 closure in trucks per shovel (section 4.6).
  • Maintenance Planners need the ramp-performance view and the meaning of the platform derate causes; the decision "finish the load or withdraw now" depends on the cause, which the handbook cannot specify [TBV].
  • Engineers must know which model a number comes from, and that the illustrative values of this chapter are not the parameters of any real truck.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-07-01-001 [H] The VCS shall report every propulsion derate to the ADS and, through it, to the AMS as a capability — available power fraction or derated Rimpull curve, cause and start time — and the AMS shall display it per truck, distinct from ADS speed derating and from speed restrictions. Rationale: the speed of a slow truck may be limited by any of three elements (vehicle, ADS, controller); without the capability report the controller cannot tell them apart (sections 4.7, 8.2). Verification: test (LAB-07-01-01 derate scenario as a SIL case; HMI shows derated — platform at T+2 s); inspection of the interface specification.

REQ-07-01-002 [H] The ADS shall plan speed and acceleration within the capability reported by the VCS, and the AMS shall use the truck's reported speed and capability, not nominal values, when it predicts reservation hold times and intersection crossing times. Rationale: a derated truck cannot follow a nominal speed plan, and it holds blocks and intersections longer (section 7.1). Verification: test (SIL: derated truck at 60 % approaching Intersection X; no tracking stop; reservation hold time predicted within the configured tolerance [A]).

REQ-07-01-003 [H] Every cycle-time or travel-time figure produced by the FMS or by engineering analysis shall state the model it comes from (steady-state or time-stepped, with version); steady-state figures shall reproduce the handbook reference values (M-20481 1564.1 s; via R24 1800.1 s) when given the reference inputs. Rationale: the two models differ by up to 41 % on single segments and by 1.8 % on the cycle (section 4.4); mixing them creates false differences. Verification: test (LAB-07-01-02 reference test); inspection of reports.

REQ-07-01-004 [H] The AHS shall record, for every ramp climb, the truck, payload, climb time, speed by grade position, power state and active restrictions, and shall flag climbs slower than the expected time for the payload by more than a configured margin [A]. Rationale: derates, overloads and road condition all show first as slow climbs; without this record they disappear into cycle time (sections 6.1, 8.1). Verification: demonstration (ramp-performance view with the SCN-DERATE-DT17 and SCN-PAYLOAD-OVER-EX02 climbs flagged); inspection.

REQ-07-01-005 [H] Dynamics and cycle-time calculations used for planning or for the ADS shall use the payload reported by the payload monitoring of the truck, not the rated payload, when a valid report exists. Rationale: at 250 t the climb force is 35.3 kN higher and the climb 32.8 s longer than at 220 t (section 4.5). Verification: test (LAB-07-01-02 payload sweep; SIL case with a 250 t report).

REQ-07-01-006 [H] Vehicle dynamics parameters used by the ADS or the FMS (Rimpull and retarding curves, effective mass, axle load shares, acceleration limits) shall come from the vehicle supplier or from site measurement, be held under configuration control with their source, and shall not be taken from illustrative models such as ahs_sim.dynamics. Rationale: every value in this chapter is [A]; using it as data would make planning and control wrong in ways no test of the model would show. Verification: inspection of the configuration records.

REQ-07-01-007 [H] Derated running and restricted running shall be recorded as mode intervals with cause and start time from the platform or AMS time stamp; Downtime shall begin only when the truck is withdrawn from production. Rationale: under the GMG model these intervals carry no delay code (ch-04-06); their cost must still be attributable (section 8.4). Verification: inspection of the exception records after a SIL run of SCN-DERATE-DT17.

REQ-07-01-008 [H] Road-maintenance priority for the ramps shall be supported by an estimate of the cycle-time effect of rolling resistance per segment, derived from the steady-state model and calibrated with logged climbs of trucks at full power and known payload. Rationale: each 0.005 of rolling resistance on R05 costs 17.6 s per cycle (section 4.5); the argument must be made in loads per shift. Verification: analysis (LAB-07-01-02 rolling-resistance sweep); demonstration with logged data.

10. Verification and validation

Model verification. The time-stepped model must reproduce the steady-state model where both apply. The ahs_sim tests check steady_speed_kmh against segment_speed_kmh for three payloads and three grades. LAB-07-01-01's tests check the R05 operating points, the steady-state travel times (804.0 s loaded, 503.1 s empty) and that the derated cycle at fraction 1.0 equals 1564.1 s; LAB-07-01-02's tests reproduce 1564.1 s, 4500.5 m, 1800.1 s, 5865.1 m and the 250 t numbers of ch-01-01, and check that climb time equals (lift + rolling work) / wheel power.

Validation against reality. Nothing here validates the model against a real truck. On a site, logged speed traces are compared with the model: steady climb speeds by payload reveal effective wheel power and rolling resistance; acceleration from standstill at P2 reveals the Rimpull cap and effective mass; segment times show what the model misses [R].

Scenario tests. SCN-DERATE-DT17 is a SIL case (ch-07-08, ch-18-02): inject a 60 % derate on a loaded truck on R05 and check the capability report on the HMI (REQ-07-01-001), no tracking stop and reservation holds that follow the actual speed (REQ-07-01-002), and the mode interval from the platform time stamp (REQ-07-01-007). Tolerances are site and supplier decisions [A].

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Assuming the speed limit sets the climb speed when designing reservation horizons, following gaps or HMI expectations for the loaded climb.
  • Reporting a derate as a fault flag without the remaining capability.
  • Letting central software override vehicle protections to keep traffic moving.
  • Using the steady-state model for segment-level timing where acceleration dominates, or taking the −15.8 s on R05 as a real gain.
  • Taking illustrative curves as data, or planning with rated payload when the truck reports another.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Raising the R05 limit to speed up loaded trucks — nothing changes on the climb, and empty trucks descend faster, which is a stopping-distance question (ch-07-02).
  • Applying a restriction to "explain" a slow truck, hiding the cause and slowing the empty trucks.
  • Coding derated or restricted running as a delay, or not recording it at all.
  • Resetting a derate from the console to restore production.
  • Ignoring the followers — three trucks lose time, not one — and expecting R24 to be faster because it is gentler.

All values [A], computed by LAB-07-01-01 and LAB-07-01-02 with ahs_sim 0.13.0 on mine version 0.3.0. Sections 4.2 and 4.5 already work through the loaded climb (453.0 kN, 11.44 km/h, 421.4 s) and the 250 t overload; the examples below add the steps not shown there.

Example 1 — Starting on R05. Force for 0.6 m/s²: 1.05 × 385 000 × 0.6 = 242.6 kN; plus resistance 453.0 kN = 695.6 kN, below the 900 kN cap. Corner speed 1440 / 900 = 1.6 m/s = 5.76 km/h; above it the force falls as 1440 / v kN. Simulated: 95 % of the steady speed after 8.0 s and 15.9 m; climb from rest 424.6 s (+3.2 s against the steady-state model).

Example 2 — The derate and the followers. At 60 %: 0.6 × 11.44 = 6.87 km/h. Remaining 939.6 m: 295.6 s at full power, 491.2 s derated: +195.6 s. DT14, 170 m behind, closes at 4.58 km/h = 1.27 m/s; reaching the 100 m gap takes about 55 s plus DT17's slowing: T+57.6 s.

Example 3 — A rougher ramp. RR 0.03 → 0.04 on R05: G+RR = 0.12994; v = 1 440 000 / (385 000 × 9.81 × 0.12994) = 2.93 m/s = 10.56 km/h; climb 456.5 s, +35.1 s per cycle; 1599.2 s still gives 27 loads in 12 h, RR 0.05 only 26.

15. Key takeaways

  • A loaded HT220 climbs R05 at 11.4 km/h because it is power-limited: 1440 kW at the wheels against 453.0 kN. The 40 km/h limit never binds on the loaded climb.
  • The time-stepped model agrees with the steady-state model on every steady speed and differs where speed changes: +28.2 s (+1.8 %) on the cycle, up to +41 % on one segment. The steady-state model remains the reference for the handbook's cycle numbers.
  • Each 0.005 of rolling resistance on R05 costs 17.6 s per cycle; a 250 t overload costs 32.8 s and 35.3 kN more drive force on every climb.
  • The R24 reroute is slower at every payload (236.0 s at rated payload); with nine trucks EX02 is then 83.5 % busy.
  • A propulsion derate slows the loaded truck wherever it needs power; an R05 restriction slows the empty descent. Record the cause; neither is a delay code.
  • Vehicle protections are not overridden from the centre; the ADS and the AMS plan with the capability the vehicle reports.
