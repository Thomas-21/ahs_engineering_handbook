1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Day shift, in the battery-electric what-if of the reference mine. DT17, now an HT220-BEV, finishes tipping at C01 and reports 41 % state of charge. Five seconds later the FMS proposes a charging diversion to FB1. The AHS Controller confirms it and tells the Pit Supervisor that EX02 will be a truck short for about twenty-five minutes. The Pit Supervisor has run diesel fleets for years and asks the obvious question: "A diesel truck refuels once a shift. This one has done two loads since it last charged. What is going on?" The next morning R05 is to be closed for windrow work (SCN-CLOSURE-R05), and every ore truck will climb the west ramp R24 instead. The planning engineer wants to know how many cycles a charge buys on the longer route — and whether an overhead line on R05 would change the picture.

The questions:

  • Where does the energy of an M-20481 cycle go, and how much of it can a battery-electric truck get back on the way down?
  • Why does a 1613 kWh pack buy only two cycles between charges, when the energy alone would suggest three?
  • What does the R24 reroute cost in energy, and why is the extra energy not in climbing?
  • What would a hybrid drive or a trolley line on R05 or on R24 change?
  • What changes for the VCS, the ADS, the AMS, the FMS and the people when the energy comes from a battery?

This chapter covers:

  • the energy of one M-20481 cycle for the diesel HT220 and the battery-electric HT220-BEV, separated into the potential term, the rolling term, the auxiliary load and regeneration (FIG-09-01-02);
  • the R05 route against the R24 reroute — the same lift, a longer road (FIG-09-01-03);
  • the four powertrain topologies — diesel-electric, hybrid, trolley-assist and battery-electric — and what each asks of the site (FIG-09-01-01);
  • cycles per charge, the reserve check, and what charging instead of refuelling does to the shift;
  • SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV from both sides, with SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV as a first look at how the energy choice changes the safety response.

The BEV fleet is a what-if. The reference mine's fleet stays diesel (HT220, equipment.yaml). Volume 09 asks what would change if all 40 trucks were HT220-BEV on the same baseline allocation. HT220-BEV masses equal HT220 (165 t empty, 220 t payload) [A], so every comparison here isolates the powertrain. The charging station is at the existing fuel-bay node FB1 with 12 bays of 3000 kW (equipment.yaml, bev_variant, all [A]); in the what-if FB1 is converted to charging only, with no diesel fuelling there. Trolley lines and hybrid drives are not part of the reference mine at all; they are evaluated as what-ifs and would need a mine.yaml change before any lab used them as fact. Every number below is an illustrative assumption [A] computed with ahs_sim 0.19.1 (LAB-09-01-01); no product, manufacturer or cell chemistry is described.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Calculate the energy required per M-20481 cycle for the HT220 diesel baseline and for an assumed BEV variant [A], separating grade, rolling-resistance and auxiliary terms and the energy recoverable on the empty R05 descent.
  2. Compare diesel-electric, hybrid, trolley-assist and battery-electric powertrain topologies in terms of energy storage, peak power on the R05 climb, and site infrastructure.
  3. Identify the changes each powertrain option imposes on VCS power limits, ADS speed planning and AMS mission constraints (e.g. range and charging stops).
  4. Compare energy per cycle and peak traction power for M-20481 via R05 and via the R24 reroute, separating the potential-energy term (the same for both ramps) from the rolling-resistance term (which grows with distance) [A].
  5. Evaluate, as an illustrative what-if, trolley-assist on R05 versus on R24 — line length, energy delivered under the line and effect on climb speed [A].

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Explain to the Pit Supervisor how a BEV fleet changes shift patterns: charging instead of refuelling at FB1, climb speed on R05 and effective availability.
  2. Describe the operational constraints that trolley-assist on R05 would introduce (lane use, connection and disconnection zones, line maintenance closures) as an illustrative option [A].
  3. Explain to the Pit Supervisor how an R05 closure, with traffic rerouted over R24, changes energy per cycle and therefore refuelling or charging frequency.
  4. Identify which operating routines (refuelling at FB1, park-up at PK1, shift-change parking) change under each powertrain option and who owns each change.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The diesel fleet of the reference mine has an energy problem that nobody notices. Each HT220 carries 4000 l of fuel (equipment.yaml), burns about 100 l per M-20481 cycle and refuels at FB1 roughly once every 36 cycles — a little more than a shift's work. Refuelling is a short, planned stop that the FMS schedules and the AHS Controller hardly sees. The energy question is answered by the size of the tank.

A battery-electric truck turns that around. The same climb from EX02 to C01 draws about a quarter of the pack's usable energy, so the energy question is asked after every cycle, and its answer decides where the truck goes next. Three things that a diesel fleet treats as background become operational decisions:

Energy per cycle varies with the route. The R05 closure in SCN-CLOSURE-R05 sends every ore truck over R24. For a diesel truck that is a longer cycle and a little more fuel. For a BEV truck it can be the difference between finishing a cycle and stopping short of the charger, so the reserve check must use the route the truck will actually drive.

Energy stops take production time. In the fleet what-if of LAB-09-01-01, charging takes about a quarter of all truck-hours, against about two per cent for refuelling a diesel truck. The BEV fleet on the baseline allocation hauls about 29 % less than the diesel baseline in the same shift. That figure is a property of the assumed pack, cycle and charger — not a verdict on battery-electric haulage — and ch-09-05, ch-09-06 and ch-09-08 work through what would close it: more bays, more trucks, a different pack, opportunity charging or a trolley line.

The energy source changes the safety case. A diesel truck that catches fire is handled by ch-04-07. A battery that raises a thermal warning (SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV) needs a different detection chain, a high-voltage isolation step and a response whose duration is not yet known; ch-09-04 treats it. This chapter only shows where the energy choice touches the safety architecture.

Before any of those decisions can be argued, the engineer and the Pit Supervisor need a shared, quantitative picture of where the energy goes. That is this chapter's job.

3. Terminology and foundations

Powertrain topologies (FIG-09-01-01):

  • Diesel-electric drive (glossary diesel-electric-drive): a diesel engine drives an alternator; power electronics feed electric wheel motors. Retarding energy goes to a resistor grid. The reference mine's HT220.
  • Hybrid drive (glossary hybrid-drive): a diesel-electric drive with an energy-storage device on the DC link that stores retarding energy and supplies peaks. Not part of the reference mine; not modelled in ahs_sim.
  • Trolley-assist (glossary trolley-assist): an overhead line on selected ramps supplies the electric drive through a pantograph (glossary pantograph) on the truck, so the engine or battery is relieved on the climb. Not part of the reference mine.
  • Battery-electric vehicle (glossary battery-electric-vehicle, BEV): the drive is fed from an onboard traction battery, charged at a charger. The HT220-BEV of equipment.yaml, a what-if.

Source energy, wheel energy. The wheel energy is the work the wheels must do against grade and rolling resistance. The source energy is what the engine (engine output) or the battery (at its terminals) must supply: wheel energy divided by the drivetrain efficiency (0.80 for HT220, 0.85 for HT220-BEV [A]), plus the auxiliary load (cooling, hydraulics, ADS and electronics; 60 kW for the diesel as an engine load and 120 kW for the BEV [A]), minus any energy regenerated.

Potential term and rolling term. Lifting a truck through a height Δh costs mgΔh whatever the road; rolling it a distance L costs mgCrrL, which grows with distance. The split matters for the R24 reroute.

Regenerative braking (glossary regenerative-braking). On a downhill the wheel motors brake as generators (retarding, glossary retarding). A diesel-electric truck turns that energy into heat in the grid; a BEV can return part of it to the battery, limited by the pack's charge acceptance while retarding (1500 kW [A]) and a wheel-to-battery efficiency (0.75 [A]).

State of charge (glossary state-of-charge, SOC) and usable energy. The pack of HT220-BEV is 300 cells in series and 6 in parallel (300s6p) of a generic 3.2 V, 280 Ah cell: 960 V, 1680 Ah, 1612.8 kWh nominal. The operation uses SOC between 20 % and 90 % [A] — an operating window, not a protection limit — so 1129.0 kWh is usable. ch-09-02 explains cells and packs; ch-09-03 how SOC is estimated and why the estimate carries an uncertainty.

Energy per cycle (glossary energy-per-cycle) and cycles per charge (glossary cycles-per-charge). The source energy of one complete haul cycle, and how many complete cycles fit between charges once a reserve is kept.

Reserve check [H]. Before the next cycle is dispatched, the energy above the SOC floor must cover the next cycle, the trip to the charging station from the next decision point and a margin (5 % of nominal pack energy [A]), using the conservative SOC (SOC minus twice its standard deviation [A]). It is the rule ahs_sim.energy.simulate_bev_fleet applies and ch-09-06 develops.

Charging diversion [H]. Sending a truck to a charger bay instead of back to its loading unit. In this chapter it is the operational counterpart of the energy calculation (SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV); the charger bay and its reservation are ch-09-05's subject (glossary charging-infrastructure).

Peak power. The highest source power on the cycle. On the R05 climb both trucks run at their power limit (1800 kW to the drivetrain [A]), so the peak is set by the powertrain, not by the road.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The energy model

ahs_sim.energy.segment_energy is a quasi-static model: the truck runs each segment at the steady speed of ahs_sim.truck (ch-07-01), so acceleration, braking and corners are ignored. With m the gross mass, Δh the rise of the segment and L its length:

Ewheel=mgΔh+mgCrrL

When Ewheel>0 the source supplies Ewheel/η with drivetrain efficiency η. When the segment is net downhill (Ewheel<0) the BEV recovers

Eregen=min(ηr|Ewheel|, Pregen,maxt)

and the diesel recovers nothing. The auxiliary load Paux runs for the segment time t. The battery's net energy on a segment is traction plus auxiliary minus regeneration; the diesel's is the engine output, converted to fuel with a brake-specific fuel consumption of 210 g/kWh and 0.835 kg/l [A], never less than an idle rate of 25 l/h [A]. cycle_energy adds the time spent spotting, loading, queueing and tipping at the auxiliary load. The model is used for every number in this chapter unless it says "time-stepped".

4.2 Where the energy of an M-20481 cycle goes

M-20481 loads at EX02 and hauls ore to C01 over R18, R08, R06, R05, R01, R02 and R21, and returns the same way. LAB-09-01-01 gives, for the BEV via R05:

Leg Segment Length Speed Time Battery energy
loaded R18, R08, R06 1434.4 m 15–40 km/h 173.5 s 53.8 kWh
loaded R05 (climb, 9 %) 1339.6 m 12.2 km/h 396.6 s 210.9 kWh
loaded R01, R02, R21 1726.5 m 15–50 km/h 209.1 s 65.3 kWh
empty R21, R02, R01 1726.5 m 15–50 km/h 209.1 s 27.7 kWh
empty R05 (descent) 1339.6 m 40.0 km/h 120.6 s −22.9 kWh
empty R06, R08, R18 1434.4 m 15–40 km/h 173.5 s 26.4 kWh
idle spot, load, spot, tip — — 257.0 s 8.6 kWh

HT220-BEV, M-20481 via R05, quasi-static [A]; sums of the LAB-09-01-01 segment rows.

The cycle draws 369.7 kWh from the battery: 345.3 kWh of traction and 51.3 kWh of auxiliary load, less 26.9 kWh regenerated. The R05 climb alone takes 210.9 kWh — 57 % of the cycle in 26 % of its time. The climb is power-limited: 1800 kW to the drivetrain at 85 % efficiency moves 385 t up 9 % plus 3 % rolling resistance at 12.2 km/h, and the battery delivers 1915 kW including the auxiliary load. On the empty descent R05 gives the battery back 26.9 kWh of the 35.9 kWh available at the wheel; the cap of 1500 kW is not reached at 40 km/h, so the 0.75 efficiency is the limit.

The diesel HT220 on the same route needs 393.0 kWh of engine output and 99.9 l of fuel per cycle. It climbs R05 at 11.4 km/h (421.4 s), slower than the BEV because its drivetrain efficiency is 0.80 [A]; the whole cycle takes 1564.1 s against 1539.3 s. It regenerates nothing: the 35.9 kWh at the wheel on the descent goes to the grid.

What reaches the wheel. Both trucks do the same wheel work, 293.5 kWh per cycle of positive traction. The BEV's battery supplies 369.7 kWh for it, so 79.4 % of the battery energy reaches the wheel as traction. The diesel's 99.9 l hold about 999 kWh of fuel energy at a lower heating value of 36 MJ/l [A]; 29.4 % of it reaches the wheel. This is the whole energy argument for battery-electric haulage in two numbers — and it leaves out the losses between the grid and the battery, the energy to make and recycle the battery, and the cost of the energy, which ch-09-08 and ch-21-06 take up. In ch-09-08 a charging efficiency of 0.92 from grid to pack [A] is applied.

Waste cycles cost more. EX03 and EX04 haul waste to D01 over longer and steeper routes: 484.1 and 477.9 kWh per BEV cycle against 371.2 and 369.7 kWh for EX01 and EX02. A planner who sizes a BEV fleet on M-20481 alone under-counts the energy of half the fleet.

4.3 R05 against the R24 reroute: the same lift, a longer road

When R05 is closed, ahs_sim routes every ore truck from the pit floor over R09 to W, up the west ramp R24 to P3 and along R25 and R12 to X. R24 is 1425.0 m long at about 6.3 % and rises 90.0 m; with R09 (30.0 m) it lifts the truck the same 120 m as R05. Between EX02-L and C01 the lift is 124.0 m on both routes, because start and end are the same.

Cycle Source energy Potential term Rolling term Auxiliary Regenerated Fuel Cycle time
BEV via R05 369.7 kWh 74.3 kWh 183.3 kWh 51.3 kWh 26.9 kWh — 1539.3 s
BEV via R24 435.7 kWh 74.3 kWh 235.1 kWh 59.1 kWh 22.2 kWh — 1773.1 s
Diesel via R05 393.0 kWh 74.3 kWh 183.3 kWh 26.1 kWh 0 99.9 l 1564.1 s
Diesel via R24 453.7 kWh 74.3 kWh 235.1 kWh 30.0 kWh 0 115.3 l 1800.1 s

M-20481 per cycle; potential and rolling terms at the wheel (net over the loaded and empty legs), the rest at the source [A].

The potential term — 74.3 kWh at the wheel, net of the loaded climb and the empty descent — is identical on both routes. The rolling term grows from 183.3 to 235.1 kWh because the reroute is longer. Climbing more gently does not save energy: the truck still has to rise 124.0 m, and every extra metre of road costs rolling resistance. The BEV pays 66.0 kWh more per cycle (+17.9 %), the diesel 15.4 l more. The reroute also regenerates less: R24's 6.3 % grade gives less retarding energy per metre, and 22.2 kWh comes back instead of 26.9.

Peak power does not change. The climb is power-limited on either ramp: 1918 kW from the battery on R24, 1915 kW on R05. What changes is the speed — 15.6 km/h on R24 against 12.2 km/h on R05 — and the time the truck spends at peak power: 328.1 s on R24 plus 102.7 s on R09, against 396.6 s on R05. The battery sees the same power for longer; ch-09-04 shows what that means for pack temperature.

4.4 A time-stepped check of the power demand

The quasi-static model gives one power per segment. ahs_sim.dynamics.simulate_route (ch-07-01, ch-07-02) drives the same route with acceleration, rimpull and retarding limits, and energy.trace_power turns its forces into source power. FIG-09-01-03 shows both routes.

The peak from the battery is 1920 kW on both ramps: 1800 kW to the drivetrain plus 120 kW auxiliary — the rated limit. The diesel's engine output peaks at 1860 kW. On the empty descent the 20 s average of the BEV's source power reaches −533 kW on R05: the battery is being charged while the truck retards. The diesel never goes below its auxiliary load.

The two models do not agree on energy, and the difference is instructive. Time-stepped, the loaded leg of M-20481 takes 345.5 kWh from the battery against 330.0 kWh quasi-static; the empty leg 51.6 kWh against 31.2 kWh. Acceleration from rest at EX02 and C01, and the speed policy that alternates drive and brake at each speed cap, cost energy that the steady-speed model leaves out. The travel energy is about 10 % higher time-stepped. Volume 09 uses the quasi-static numbers consistently, so absolute energies — and with them cycles per charge — are somewhat optimistic; comparisons between routes and powertrains are less affected. The trial in ch-09-08 measures the real figure.

Qualitative comparison; numbers from LAB-09-01-01 [A]. Hybrid and trolley are not part of the reference mine.

Hybrid. ahs_sim has no hybrid model. LAB-09-01-01 computes only an upper bound: if all the energy the BEV regenerates on the descent, 26.9 kWh per cycle, were stored and replaced engine output one for one, the diesel would save at most 6.8 l per cycle, 6.8 % of its fuel. A real hybrid saves less (storage losses, a store too small for a whole descent) or more (a smaller engine running nearer its best efficiency, peak shaving). Neither effect is modelled; the bound only shows the scale. A hybrid changes nothing in the operating routine: the truck still refuels at FB1.

Trolley-assist on R05 or on R24 (what-if). energy.trolley_assist replaces battery power on one segment by line power through the same drivetrain. With 5000 kW [A] on the line:

Line on Line length Climb speed Time saved per climb Energy from the line Battery energy per cycle Cycles per charge
R05 1339.6 m 12.2 → 33.8 km/h 254 s 202 kWh 158.8 kWh 6.36
R24 1425.0 m 15.6 → 40.0 km/h 200 s 168 kWh 260.9 kWh 3.87

BEV, loaded climb, line 5000 kW [A]; R24 speed capped by its 40 km/h limit. What-if: no line exists in the reference mine.

A line on R05 does two things. It almost triples the climb speed, saving 254 s per cycle, and it takes the climb off the battery: 202 kWh come from the line, the battery supplies only 158.8 kWh per cycle, and cycles per charge rise from 2.73 to 6.36 with the reserve. A line on R24 is longer, takes less energy (the grade is gentler) and is capped by the speed limit, so it saves less time; but R24 carries traffic only when R05 is closed, so a line there would serve the reroute, not the daily cycle. The comparison of the line on the main ramp against the line on the reroute is the planning question; a line on both is a different investment.

The line would bring constraints of its own [A]: one lane of R05 dedicated to trolley trucks under the line; a connection zone at the toe P2 and a disconnection zone at the crest P1 where the pantograph is raised and lowered; no overtaking under the line; line maintenance closures that close the lane (or the ramp) on a schedule; and a fault on the line that sends every truck back to battery power at 12.2 km/h. Electrical standards for overhead lines and their clearances are not verified here [TBV] (SRC-PUB-TROLLEY-LINE-PRACTICE). None of this is in mine.yaml; using a line in a lab as fact would need a mine change.

4.6 Cycles per charge and the reserve

On energy alone the pack's 1129.0 kWh usable covers 1129.0 / 369.7 = 3.05 cycles via R05: three complete cycles. The fleet model does not dispatch on energy alone. Its reserve check needs, after tipping at C01, the next cycle (369.7 kWh), the trip from C01 to FB1 (38.1 kWh including the auxiliary load) and a margin of 5 % of 1612.8 kWh (80.6 kWh): a reserve of 118.7 kWh. With the reserve, 2.73 cycles fit, so the truck charges after two cycles. Via the R24 reroute, 2.59 cycles fit on energy and 2.32 with the reserve — still two, but with a deeper discharge.

n=⌊Eusable−EreserveEcycle⌋

A charge from 20 % to 90 % takes 1408 s at 3000 kW with the taper above 80 % SOC [A], and 1528 s with the 60 s connect and 60 s disconnect of bev_variant. A truck that charges after two cycles starts charging at about 44 % SOC, so its stop is shorter: 1300 s including the 239.4 s detour from C01 via FB1 back to EX02 (1965 m further than going straight back).

LAB-09-01-01 closed form, M-20481 via R05 [A]. The refuelling time is an assumption; it is not in the shared data.

The closed form ignores queueing at the charger and the waste cycles. The discrete-event fleet model energy.simulate_bev_fleet (37 producing trucks on the baseline allocation, 12 bays, threshold policy) gives a whole DAY shift: 310 charges, 115.4 truck-hours at the charger bays coded DLY-OD-09 — 26.0 % of the 444 producing truck-hours — and 620 loads, 136 400 t. The diesel baseline (fms.simulate_fleet, which does not model refuelling) hauls 191 180 t: the BEV what-if hauls 28.7 % less.

This is the number the Pit Supervisor's question was really about. It belongs to the assumed pack (two cycles per charge), charger (3000 kW, 12 bays at one site) and duty (a 9 % ramp on every cycle). ch-09-05 sizes the bays, ch-09-06 the dispatch policy, ch-09-08 the fleet size needed to match the diesel production and its cost. A trolley line on R05 would, in this model, more than double the cycles per charge. This chapter's point is narrower: the energy calculation of a single cycle is enough to predict that charging, not travel, will dominate a BEV shift with this pack.

What else changes in the routine [A]:

  • FB1 becomes a charging station that trucks visit every two cycles instead of once a shift: 12 bays, a queue, an AMS reservation per visit (ch-09-05). In the what-if FB1 is converted to charging only: there is no diesel fuelling there any more (equipment.yaml).
  • PK1 keeps its role for parked trucks; a BEV truck parked with a low SOC must be charged before it can be dispatched, so park-up planning includes the SOC at parking.
  • Shift change (DLY-SO-01) becomes a charging opportunity: trucks that park for the change can be charged, but only 12 at a time.
  • The R05 climb speed of a BEV truck depends on its state of power: at low SOC or low temperature the battery may give less than 1800 kW and the truck climbs slower (ch-09-02, ch-09-03).

Who owns each change: the Pit Supervisor owns the shift plan and the charging priorities at shift change; the FMS owner the charging policy and the reserve parameters; the Maintenance Planner the charger and battery maintenance windows; the AHS Controller the confirmation of each diversion.

4.8 The R05 closure in the fleet

With R05 closed for the whole shift, the BEV what-if fleet needs 469 kWh per delivered load against 438 kWh with R05 open (the fleet average includes the waste cycles and the charging detours). Charging events do not rise — 306 against 310 — because the cycles are longer and fewer: the fleet delivers 133 760 t instead of 136 400 t. What changes is the depth of discharge: the mean SOC at the start of a charge falls from 0.357 to 0.326. In operation that means a fleet running closer to its SOC floor, more trucks for which the reserve check is tight, and a reserve check that must switch to the R24 energy for every truck at once when R05 closes — not truck by truck as each one reaches the junction (REQ-09-01-003).

4.9 What each topology asks of the VCS, the ADS, the AMS and the FMS

  • VCS. For diesel, the power limit is the engine's and changes slowly (temperature derates, SCN-DERATE-DT17). For a BEV the limit is the battery's state of power, which the BMS reports and which falls at low SOC and low temperature (ch-09-03). The VCS applies it and adds the high-voltage isolation sequence to its stop states (ch-09-04). For trolley, the VCS manages the pantograph and the switch between line and onboard power.
  • ADS. Speed planning on R05 must use the power the VCS reports, not the rated power: a BEV at low state of power climbs slower, and the following distance and the reservation times on R05 must follow. Under a trolley line, the ADS also keeps the truck in the line's lane.
  • AMS. Mission constraints gain range: a mission is issued only if the truck can complete it and reach a charger with the reserve (the FMS proposes, the AMS checks the route it will actually issue). The AMS owns charger-bay reservations and exclusion zones (ch-09-05, ch-09-04). Under a line, the AMS also owns the line lane, the connection zones and line closures.
  • FMS. Energy-aware assignment: SOC-based charging recommendations, the reserve check on the current route and charging priorities (ch-09-06).

4.10 Model limits

Quasi-static energy at steady speed (about 10 % optimistic on travel against the time-stepped model); one temperature, no battery ageing (ch-09-07), no charging losses (ch-09-08 adds 0.92); generic cell, no chemistry; identical masses for diesel and BEV [A]; diesel refuelling not modelled in the fleet baseline; hybrid only as a bound; trolley as a segment-level what-if with no line losses, lane or connection time. The fleet model is a separate model from fms.simulate_fleet (it adds charging and SOC), deterministic without a seed.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

In the BEV what-if a truck on M-20481 completes two cycles, is diverted to FB1 after tipping, charges to 90 %, and returns empty to its loading unit. The fleet's charging events spread over the shift; with 12 bays the mean wait for a bay is 125 s (ch-09-05). The AHS Controller confirms diversions as they come, about one every 2.3 minutes for the fleet (310 in 12 h). A diesel truck refuels about once every 36 cycles.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

DT17 (HT220-BEV) on M-20481 finishes tipping at C01 with a reported SOC of 41 % [A]. The timeline is the registry's; LAB-09-01-01 recomputes it.

The energy check at T+5 s is the calculation of section 4.6 with today's numbers. The conservative SOC is 0.41 − 2 × 0.01 = 0.39; above the 20 % floor that leaves 0.19 × 1612.8 = 306.4 kWh. The next cycle needs 369.7 kWh, the trip to FB1 38.1 kWh and the margin 80.6 kWh: 488.4 kWh. DT17 could not even complete the next cycle without the reserve. energy.reserve_need_kwh of ahs_sim 0.19.1 gives the same 488.4 kWh and the reported SOC that would pass the check: 52.3 %. From T+30 s to T+1480 s DT17 is out of production for 1450 s — about one M-20481 cycle.

6.3 Abnormal operation — R05 closed (SCN-CLOSURE-R05)

When R05 closes, every pit truck's next cycle costs the R24 energy: 435.7 kWh instead of 369.7 kWh for M-20481. A truck that tipped at C01 with 2.0 cycles of energy left may now have 1.7. The reserve check at the next tip uses the R24 energy; trucks for which it fails are diverted. In the fleet model, energy per load rises from 438 to 469 kWh and the mean SOC at which charging starts falls from 0.357 to 0.326 (section 4.8). The closure in SCN-CLOSURE-R05 is planned the day before; the FMS can therefore recompute charging priorities before the closure, so that no truck is caught at the crest of R24 with too little energy to reach FB1.

6.4 Abnormal operation — SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV: the energy choice changes the safety response

In the battery variant of the fire scenario, DT17 is climbing R05 loaded when the BMS raises a thermal warning (T+0 s). The VCS begins a controlled stop and a power-isolation sequence at T+5 s, the AMS establishes an exclusion zone at T+10 s with radius and duration per site procedure, and at T+30 s the traction-battery contactors are reported open [A]. The architecture is that of ch-04-07 and ch-08-05. What this chapter adds is only the energy side: the battery that fed the climb is the hazard. Whether a battery event keeps R05 closed longer than a diesel fire is not known (SRC-PUB-BEV-THERMAL-DURATION, [TBV]), and the detection signals, the isolation step, the ERT's approach and the post-event handling are ch-09-04's subject. For energy management one consequence follows at once: the R05 closure that the event causes sends the rest of the fleet onto R24, with the energy effects of section 6.3.

6.5 Degraded operation — energy information stale or missing

  • SOC report stale or invalid. The FMS treats a report older than its age limit (30 s in the fleet model [A]) or flagged invalid as insufficient: the truck is held — at the destination's Queue point (C01-Q or D01-Q), not on the tipping position — until a fresh report arrives, and is never dispatched on a guess (soc_for_dispatch returns no value; ch-09-03, ch-09-06). The AHS Controller sees the truck held and why.
  • State of power stale. The VCS falls back to a conservative power limit, never to the last good value (effective_power_limit_kw). DT17 climbs R05 slower; the ADS plans with the lower limit.
  • Charger bay unavailable. If the reserved bay faults or loses communication, the AMS withdraws the reservation (fail closed) and the FMS re-plans (SCN-CHARGER-OUTAGE-BEV, ch-09-05, ch-09-08).

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Pack size against cycles per charge. With 369.7 kWh per cycle, a 118.7 kWh reserve and a 70 % window, the pack must hold 4 × 369.7 + 118.7 = 1597.5 kWh usable for four cycles — 2282 kWh nominal, 41 % more than 1612.8 kWh. A bigger pack weighs more (this volume keeps masses equal [A], which hides that cost), costs more and still charges at the same power. The alternative is to accept two cycles and charge faster or nearer the route (ch-09-05) — or to take the climb off the battery (trolley).

SOC window. A wider window (say 10–95 %) gives more cycles but ages the pack faster and leaves less margin for estimation error (ch-09-03, ch-09-07). The 20–90 % window is [A].

Regeneration cap. 1500 kW of charge acceptance while retarding is not reached on M-20481 at 40 km/h; on a steeper or faster descent, or at high SOC (a full pack cannot accept charge), the surplus goes to the grid. A BEV therefore keeps a resistor grid.

Equal masses. Keeping HT220-BEV at 165 t empty isolates the powertrain. A real battery and its structure may weigh more or less than engine, fuel and tank; a heavier truck carries less payload or uses more energy per tonne. The assumption is [A] and must be revisited with real data.

Quasi-static energy. Simple, transparent and adequate for comparing routes and powertrains; about 10 % optimistic on travel energy. Dispatch decisions that run close to the floor need the margin to absorb it.

Trolley line. Takes the climb off the battery and nearly triples the climb speed, but needs a dedicated lane, power infrastructure and closures for line maintenance — and its value depends on R05 staying the main route.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

Along the SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV timeline:

  • T+0 s. The BMS publishes SOC, its standard deviation, SOH, SOP and a timestamp (SocReport). The FMS must use a report newer than its age limit; the tip-complete event and a fresh SOC report should be within that limit of each other.
  • T+5 s. The FMS evaluates the reserve check with the route of the next mission as the AMS would issue it now (R05 open or closed) and the energy per cycle of DT17's loading unit. The proposal carries the numbers: conservative SOC, energy above the floor, energy needed.
  • T+10 s. The AMS reserves the bay and prepares the route; a reservation is a permission and fails closed (ch-09-05).
  • T+30 s. The controller's confirmation releases the mission. If the controller rejects it, the truck is held at C01-Q — it is not dispatched to EX02 against a failed reserve check.
  • T+315 s to T+1480 s. Connection, charging and disconnection are reported by the charger and the BMS; DLY-OD-09 runs from DT17's arrival at the bay (T+315 s) to its release (T+1480 s); the detour travel is not coded (section 8.4).

Timing that matters for energy: the reserve check must run before the next mission is issued, at every decision point (after tipping, on arrival at a destination that is down), and again whenever the route changes.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

On the truck panel of DT17: SOC 41 % with its band (39–43 % at 2σ), the time of the report, the energy needed for the next cycle on the current route and the reserve, and the result — charge required. In the FMS proposal list: "DT17 charging diversion to FB1, bay reserved, expected back at EX02 in about 25 min" with Accept and Reject. On the fleet view: FB1 with 12 bays, occupied and reserved, and the queue. On the map, a banner when R05 is closed: "Reserve recomputed for R24 — 6 trucks to charge before next cycle" (an example of the information, [A]).

8.3 Procedure excerpt — charging diversion after a failed reserve check (SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV)

  1. T+5 s — AHS Controller: read the FMS proposal for DT17: SOC and band, energy needed, route (R05 or R24).
  2. Check that the SOC report is fresh. If the panel shows it stale, do not confirm a cycle; the truck is held until a fresh report arrives.
  3. T+30 s — AHS Controller: confirm the diversion. If you reject it, record why; DT17 stays held at C01-Q until a new proposal or a decision by the Pit Supervisor.
  4. Tell the Pit Supervisor by radio: "DT17 to FB1 for charge, back at EX02 in about twenty-five minutes."
  5. T+315 s: check that DT17 arrived at the reserved bay; if the bay faulted, follow the charger procedure (ch-09-05).
  6. T+1480 s: check that DT17 left FB1 with the target SOC and is on its way to EX02.
  7. Record: the time from arrival at the bay to its release (T+315 s to T+1480 s, 1165 s) is coded DLY-OD-09; the travel C01 → FB1 → EX02 is not coded (handbook rule, section 8.4).

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

  • Delay coding (handbook rules, delay-codes.yml coding_rules). Charging and waiting for a charger bay are DLY-OD-09 Fuelling, from arrival at the bay to its release; the extra travel of the detour is not coded. Whether the GMG framework classifies battery charging as Fuelling is [TBV]: the GMG Time Usage Model is verified for its time categories (SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020, [F]), not for battery charging. The GMG guideline for battery electric vehicles in underground mining (SRC-PUB-GMG-BEV-UG-2022, section 9.2.2) treats battery charging or swapping hours as downtime and says time models may need extending [F]; the handbook keeps its rule [A]. Charging while the truck's destination is down is DLY-OD-10 (the cause that started first); charging during a shift change is DLY-SO-01. A truck held because its SOC report went stale through a communication loss is DLY-OD-05; an invalid estimate or a BMS fault is DLY-DN-01.
  • Operating Delay grows: in the fleet what-if, 115.4 truck-hours per shift of DLY-OD-09 against none recorded for the diesel baseline (whose refuelling the fms model does not simulate).
  • Physical Availability is not changed by charging, which is not Downtime; Use of Availability and Effective Utilization fall, and so does production per truck. That is why "effective availability" in a BEV discussion should be stated as a GMG KPI, not as a vague notion.
  • R05 closure. Energy per load +7 % in the fleet (438 → 469 kWh); deeper discharge.

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • AHS Controllers: read SOC with its band and age; know that a stale report means hold; confirm diversions; code DLY-OD-09.
  • Pit Supervisors: plan shifts around charging (every two cycles, 25 minutes), use shift change and planned stops as charging opportunities, and anticipate the extra energy of a closure.
  • Field Technicians who work on the high-voltage system need a high-voltage qualification — a qualification of the Field Technician role (function [A]), not a new role; the applicable work practice is [TBV] (SRC-PUB-HV-WORK-PRACTICE).
  • Engineers: the energy model of section 4.1 and its limits.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-09-01-001 [H] The FMS shall hold the energy per cycle for every loading unit, destination and route in use (including reroutes), and shall use the value for the truck's actual assignment and route in every reserve check. Rationale: waste cycles to D01 need 478–484 kWh against 370 kWh for M-20481, and the R24 reroute 435.7 kWh (sections 4.2, 4.3). Verification: test (LAB-09-01-01: EX03 484.1 kWh, EX04 477.9 kWh, M-20481 via R24 435.7 kWh); inspection of the FMS configuration.

REQ-09-01-002 [H] Before a BEV truck is given its next mission at a decision point, the FMS shall check, using the conservative SOC, that the energy above the SOC floor covers the next cycle, the trip from the next decision point to a charging station and a configurable margin; if it does not, the FMS shall propose a charging diversion and shall not propose the cycle. Rationale: with the reserve, a 1129.0 kWh window covers two M-20481 cycles, not three (section 4.6); SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV. Verification: test (LAB-09-01-01: 306.4 kWh above the floor < 488.4 kWh needed → diversion; cycles per charge 2.73 → 2).

REQ-09-01-003 [H] When a segment is closed or reopened, the FMS shall recompute the reserve check of every BEV truck on the routes the AMS will issue after the change before the next mission of any affected truck is issued. Rationale: an R05 closure raises energy per M-20481 cycle by 17.9 % for every pit truck at once (sections 4.3, 6.3). Verification: test (fleet model with R05 closed: energy per load 438 → 469 kWh, deeper discharge); demonstration.

REQ-09-01-004 [H] The FMS shall treat a SOC report that is older than a configurable age or flagged invalid as insufficient energy: the truck shall be held until a fresh, valid report is received and shall not be dispatched on the last known value. Rationale: fail closed (master-instructions §3); section 6.5. Verification: test (ahs_sim.energy.soc_for_dispatch; fleet model with a stale-report window: no dispatch while stale, ch-09-06); inspection.

REQ-09-01-005 [H] The ADS shall plan speed on every segment within the propulsion power limit currently reported by the VCS, and the AMS shall use the resulting speeds for reservations and mission times; a stale power-limit signal shall be replaced by a conservative fallback, never by the last good value. Rationale: the climb speed on R05 depends on the power available (12.2 km/h at 1800 kW; slower at reduced state of power, ch-09-03); section 4.9. Verification: test (energy.climb_speed_kmh, energy.effective_power_limit_kw); analysis.

REQ-09-01-006 [R] Any decision on trolley-assist should be supported by an analysis of the line on the main ramp R05 and on the reroute R24 with the same assumptions, covering energy from the line, climb speed, cycles per charge and the operating constraints of the line, and should be recorded as a mine.yaml change proposal before any lab or procedure treats the line as part of the mine. Rationale: the line on R05 saves 254 s per climb and lifts cycles per charge to 6.36; on R24 it serves only the reroute (section 4.5). Verification: inspection (analysis record, change proposal).

10. Verification and validation

  • Model checks (LAB-09-01-01 tests). The potential term equals 74.3 kWh on both routes; the rolling term grows from 183.3 to 235.1 kWh; the R05 climb is reproduced by hand (section 12); the pack gives 960 V, 1680 Ah, 1612.8 kWh; the registry times of SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV are reproduced within 3 s.
  • Cross-model check. The time-stepped trace (dynamics.simulate_route) gives 345.5 kWh on the loaded leg against 330.0 kWh quasi-static (section 4.4): the quasi-static model is optimistic on travel by about 10 %. This is recorded, not corrected.
  • Validation needs data. The energy model is not validated against any real truck. A trial (ch-09-08) must measure energy per cycle by segment, regeneration on the descent and climb speed on R05 and R24, and compare them with the model before any decision depends on cycles per charge.
  • Procedure checks. Walk SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV with controllers in a simulator: stale report → hold; rejection → held at C01-Q; DLY-OD-09 applied from arrival at the bay to release.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Sizing the pack or the chargers on M-20481 alone; waste cycles need about 30 % more energy.
  • Treating a gentler ramp as an energy saving: the lift is the same, the road longer.
  • Counting cycles per charge on energy alone and forgetting the reserve to reach the charger.
  • Computing the reserve on the planned route instead of the route the AMS will issue after a closure.
  • Assuming full regeneration at any SOC and removing the resistor grid.
  • Comparing BEV and diesel on energy at the source without the grid-to-battery losses, or on fuel without the engine-output basis.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Confirming a cycle for a truck whose SOC report is stale "because it was fine a minute ago".
  • Rejecting a diversion and sending the truck back to the shovel anyway.
  • Coding charging as a breakdown (DLY-DN-01), which lowers Physical Availability wrongly.
  • Planning an R05 closure without telling the FMS owner, so the reserve check is still computed for R05.
  • Expecting a BEV truck to climb R05 at rated speed late in its discharge.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — the loaded R05 climb by hand [A]. Gross mass m = 385 t. R05 rises 120 m over 1339.6 m; rolling resistance 0.03. Potential: 385 000 × 9.81 × 120 / 3.6 × 10⁶ = 125.9 kWh. Rolling: 385 000 × 9.81 × 0.03 × 1339.6 / 3.6 × 10⁶ = 42.2 kWh. Wheel energy 168.1 kWh; at 0.85, 197.7 kWh from the battery. Speed: 1800 kW × 0.85 = 1530 kW at the wheel against a resisting force of 385 000 × 9.81 × (0.0899 + 0.03) = 452.9 kN gives 3.38 m/s = 12.2 km/h, so the climb takes 396.6 s and the auxiliary load adds 120 × 396.6 / 3600 = 13.2 kWh. Total 210.9 kWh — the lab's figure.

Example 2 — cycles per charge with the reserve. Usable 1612.8 × 0.70 = 1129.0 kWh. Reserve: 38.1 kWh to FB1 + 0.05 × 1612.8 = 80.6 kWh, total 118.7 kWh. (1129.0 − 118.7) / 369.7 = 2.73 → 2 cycles. Via R24: (1129.0 − 118.7) / 435.7 = 2.32 → 2.

Example 3 — diesel fuel. 393.0 kWh of engine output × 210 g/kWh = 82.5 kg = 98.8 l; with the idle fuel at the shovel and the crusher the cycle needs 99.9 l. A 4000 l tank at 90 % lasts 36.0 cycles.

Example 4 — the trolley line's energy. On R05 at 33.8 km/h the climb takes 142.8 s. The line supplies the traction (197.7 kWh, unchanged by speed in the quasi-static model) plus the auxiliary load for 142.8 s (4.8 kWh): 202 kWh. The battery's cycle energy falls from 369.7 to 158.8 kWh; (1129.0 − 118.7) / 158.8 = 6.36 cycles.

15. Key takeaways

  • An M-20481 cycle draws 369.7 kWh from the BEV battery (diesel: 393.0 kWh of engine output, 99.9 l); the R05 climb is 57 % of it.
  • The R24 reroute costs 17.9 % more energy with the same lift: the extra is rolling resistance, not climbing.
  • Peak power is set by the powertrain (1920 kW from the battery), not by the ramp.
  • With the reserve, the pack gives two cycles per charge; charging takes about a quarter of the fleet's truck-hours and the what-if fleet hauls 29 % less — a property of the assumptions that later chapters work on.
  • A trolley line on R05 would more than double cycles per charge and save 254 s per climb; on R24 it serves only the reroute. Neither line exists in the reference mine.
  • The reserve check runs on the route the AMS will issue, fails closed on a stale SOC, and is confirmed by the AHS Controller.
