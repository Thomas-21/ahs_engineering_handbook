1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Volume 09 asks a what-if question: what changes if the reference mine's 40 haul trucks are battery-electric? The baseline fleet stays diesel (HT220). In this volume every truck is an HT220-BEV on the same baseline allocation, with the same masses as the HT220 (165 t empty, 220 t payload) [A], so that only the powertrain differs. In the what-if the fuel bay FB1 is converted to a charging station only: there is no diesel fuelling there.

A cold winter morning, 05:40. DT17 — the BEV variant — has stood at PK1 since the end of the night shift and starts its first M-20481 cycle with its pack at about 0 °C [A]. Its state of charge is low after the night's last cycles. It loads at EX02, crosses R08 and R06 and starts up R05. Its trucks-ahead climbed at a little over 12 km/h yesterday afternoon; DT17 climbs at under 10 km/h. The truck behind closes up. The HMI shows a small indication next to DT17: power limit active. No alarm, no fault code. The Pit Supervisor asks the AHS Controller whether DT17 is broken, whether it should be pulled out, and — because it is the third time this week — whether the batteries are "wearing out".

The questions:

  • Why does a battery truck lose power when its state of charge is low or its pack is cold, when a diesel truck does not?
  • How many M-20481 cycles does a BEV truck complete between charges, and what changes that number?
  • What do voltage, capacity, energy and power mean for a pack of 1,800 cells, and why are they not the same thing?
  • Which battery indications on the HMI are for the controller, which for maintenance, and which for the Emergency Response Team?
  • What is different about a high-voltage battery as a hazard, for the people who isolate, repair or approach the truck?

This chapter covers:

  • the cell, the module and the pack: series and parallel connection, nominal voltage, capacity and energy, and the usable window (FIG-09-02-01);
  • the open-circuit voltage curve and a first-order equivalent-circuit model of a cell, with the voltage sag and heat it predicts on the R05 climb (FIG-09-02-02, FIG-09-02-03);
  • state of power and why available power falls at low SOC and low temperature;
  • cycles per charge — on energy alone, with the operational reserve, and in the cold;
  • lithium-ion chemistry families, compared qualitatively;
  • battery-specific hazards — high voltage, stored energy, thermal runaway — and what they change for isolation and approach, as a foundation for ch-09-04;
  • SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV seen from the battery: why 41 % is not enough for another cycle.

Every number in this chapter is computed by LAB-09-02-01 on ahs_sim.energy (ahs_sim 0.19.1) from the shared data, or is an illustrative assumption [A]. No product, manufacturer, cell datasheet, price, protection limit or safety threshold is described. The battery-management functions are ch-09-03; thermal management and thermal runaway are ch-09-04.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Calculate pack voltage, capacity and energy from cell, module and pack series–parallel configuration [A].
  2. Compare lithium-ion chemistry families qualitatively on energy density, power capability, cycle life and thermal stability, with every quantitative value tagged [A] or [TBV].
  3. Model a cell with an equivalent-circuit model (open-circuit voltage plus resistance and RC branches) and compute voltage sag and heat generation for an R05 climb current profile.
  4. Calculate usable pack energy for an assumed SOC window and compare it with the energy per M-20481 cycle to estimate cycles per charge [A].

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Explain to controllers and maintenance why available power falls at low state of charge or low temperature, and how this appears as slower climbs on R05.
  2. Identify the battery-specific hazards (high voltage, stored energy, thermal runaway) that change isolation and approach procedures for Field Technicians and the Emergency Response Team.
  3. Use the cycles-per-charge estimate to explain to the Pit Supervisor how many M-20481 cycles a BEV truck completes between charges and how low ambient temperature changes that [A].
  4. Assign battery-related HMI indications (power limit active, temperature warning, cell imbalance) to controller action, maintenance referral or ERT escalation.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

A diesel HT220 carries its energy as fuel. Its engine delivers the same net power at the start and at the end of a tank; the fuel gauge tells the controller when to send the truck to FB1, and nobody worries about the gauge's accuracy in the last tenth of a tank. ch-07-01 showed why a loaded HT220 climbs R05 at about 11.4 km/h: the engine's power divided by the force needed to lift 385 t up a 9 % grade and overcome rolling resistance.

The HT220-BEV of equipment.yaml has the same 1,800 kW available to its drivetrain at nominal state of power, and a better drivetrain efficiency from battery to rim (0.85 against the diesel's 0.80 from engine output, both [A]). It climbs R05 at 12.16 km/h — faster than the diesel — as long as the battery can deliver that power. The qualification is the whole point of this chapter. A battery's power depends on its state: on how much charge is left, on its temperature, on how hard it has just been worked, and, over years, on its age. Four consequences follow for the mine.

Speed becomes a function of the battery. A BEV truck may climb more slowly at the end of its charge, after a cold night or with an aged pack. That looks exactly like the propulsion derate of SCN-DERATE-DT17 (ch-07-01) — and it is one: a vehicle-side capability limit, not an ADS restriction and not a fault.

Range replaces the fuel gauge. A diesel truck runs a shift on a tank. A BEV truck runs a few cycles on a charge, and whether it can start another one is a calculation that must be right every time: a truck that runs out of usable energy on R05 blocks the main ramp.

The numbers people quote differ. "1.6 MWh", "1,129 kWh", "three cycles", "two cycles" — all are correct for this pack, and they answer different questions. Planners, controllers and maintenance need the same vocabulary.

The hazard changes. A charged pack holds its energy whether the truck is running or parked, isolated or not. That changes what "isolated" means for a Field Technician (ch-16-04) and what the ERT approaches after an event (SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV, ch-09-04).

3. Terminology and foundations

Cell, module, pack (glossary battery-cell, battery-module, battery-pack). The cell is the electrochemical unit: two electrodes, an electrolyte and a separator in a can or pouch. A module is a mechanical and electrical group of cells with its own sensing. The pack is the assembly of modules with its housing, cooling, contactors, fuses and battery management system (BMS).

Nominal voltage, capacity, energy. A cell's nominal voltage is a representative voltage over its discharge (3.2 V here [A]). Its capacity is the charge it delivers between its full and empty definitions, in ampere-hours (280 Ah [A]). Its energy is approximately nominal voltage × capacity (896 Wh). Energy is what moves the truck; capacity is what SOC counts.

State of charge (SOC) (glossary state-of-charge). The charge still available as a fraction of capacity. The handbook writes SOC in percent; the model works with fractions.

Open-circuit voltage (OCV) (glossary open-circuit-voltage). The voltage of a cell at rest, with no current and its internal transients settled. It is a function of SOC and, more weakly, of temperature.

Equivalent-circuit model (ECM) (glossary equivalent-circuit-model). A circuit of a voltage source and resistors and capacitors that reproduces a cell's terminal voltage under current — a model of behaviour, not of chemistry.

Voltage sag (glossary voltage-sag). The drop of terminal voltage below OCV while current flows.

C-rate (glossary c-rate). Current relative to capacity: 1 C is the current that would empty the nominal capacity in one hour (280 A for this cell).

State of power (SOP). The power the battery can deliver (or accept) over a stated horizon without leaving its allowed voltage, current and temperature range. ch-09-03 defines it as a BMS signal; this chapter explains why it moves.

Usable window (glossary soc-window). The SOC range used in operation: here 20 % to 90 % (soc_min, soc_max in equipment.yaml [A]). It is an operating window chosen for range, life and reserve — not a protection limit.

High voltage (glossary high-voltage). In this handbook, a voltage at which the site's electrical safety rules for high-voltage work apply. Which voltage bands are classed as hazardous, and which work practices apply, are set by standards and site procedures [TBV]; this chapter states no threshold.

Thermal runaway (glossary thermal-runaway). Self-accelerating heat release in a cell that can propagate to neighbouring cells. Its mechanism, detection and response are ch-09-04.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 From cell to pack: series, parallel and energy

FIG-09-02-01 shows the hierarchy of the HT220-BEV pack. equipment.yaml gives the cell (3.2 V, 280 Ah) and the pack (300 cells in series, 6 in parallel: 300s6p), and the module count: 12 modules, which makes each module 25s6p [A] — a common way of breaking a pack into handleable, separately monitored blocks.

Two rules give every number:

  • Series adds voltage. ns cells in series carry the same current; their voltages add: Vpack=ns·Vcell.
  • Parallel adds capacity. np cells in parallel share the current and see the same voltage; their capacities add: Cpack=np·Ccell.

Energy is the product, and it does not care how the cells are arranged:

Epack=Vpack·Cpack=(nsVcell)(npCcell)=nsnp·VcellCcell
Level Configuration Cells Nominal voltage Capacity Energy
Cell — 1 3.2 V 280 Ah 896 Wh
Module 25s6p 150 80 V 1,680 Ah 134.4 kWh
Pack 12 modules in series = 300s6p 1,800 960 V 1,680 Ah 1,612.8 kWh

LAB-09-02-01 part A; all values [A].

The arrangement matters for everything else. The same 1,800 cells as 150s12p would give 480 V and 3,360 Ah: the same energy, half the voltage and twice the current for the same power — and four times the resistive loss in the cables and contactors for the same conductor size, since loss goes with the square of current. High pack voltage is chosen to keep currents and cable sizes manageable at megawatt power. The price is the hazard of section 4.9.

Resistances combine the other way round from capacity. A cell's resistance R becomes R·ns/np at pack level: 0.25 mΩ per cell gives 12.5 mΩ for the pack, and the RC branch's 0.15 mΩ gives 7.5 mΩ (LAB-09-02-01). These pack figures assume identical cells [A]. Real cells differ slightly in capacity and resistance, and in a series string the weakest cell sets the limit for all — the reason for cell balancing (ch-09-03).

4.2 Nominal, usable and deliverable energy

Three energies are easily confused.

  1. Nominal energy, 1,612.8 kWh: nominal voltage × capacity. It is a label, not something a truck can use.
  2. Usable energy, 1,129.0 kWh: nominal energy × (90 % − 20 %), the operating window [A] (usable_energy_kwh). The window keeps a reserve at the bottom, avoids the highest SOC where charging slows and ageing speeds up (ch-09-05, ch-09-07), and keeps the cell away from both steep ends of its voltage curve.
  3. Deliverable energy: what actually reaches the pack terminals between 90 % and 20 % on a given duty and at a given temperature, after the internal losses. On repeated M-20481 cycles at 25 °C it is 1,110.3 kWh, with 39.5 kWh turned into heat inside the cells (section 4.7).

Over a whole pack's life the usable energy also shrinks with the state of health (SOH): an 80 % SOH pack has 80 % of the usable energy of a new one (ch-09-07). The handbook's energy calculations multiply by SOH wherever a real pack is meant [H].

4.3 The open-circuit voltage curve

A cell's OCV is not constant over its charge. FIG-09-02-02 (a) shows the generic curve of ahs_sim.energy [A]: steep at both ends, rising from 2.70 V empty to 3.60 V full, and fairly flat in the middle (3.13 V at 20 %, 3.23 V at 50 %, 3.42 V at 90 %). The shape is generic; real curves depend on chemistry and are measured, not assumed.

Three practical consequences come from the shape:

  • Voltage is a poor fuel gauge in the middle. Between 30 % and 70 % the OCV changes by only 0.14 V per cell; a few millivolts of measurement error or a little polarisation is several percent of SOC. This is why ch-09-03 needs an estimator, not a voltmeter.
  • Voltage falls fast at the bottom. Below 20 % the curve steepens. Any voltage-dependent limit — and the power the pack can deliver — falls quickly there.
  • The same power needs more current at low SOC. Power is voltage × current. When voltage is lower, current must be higher for the same power, which raises the sag and the heat.

4.4 A first-order equivalent circuit

FIG-09-02-03 shows the model used in ahs_sim.energy and in both labs of this chapter and ch-09-03: an OCV source, a series resistance R0 and one RC branch (R1 in parallel with C1, time constant τ1=R1C1).

With current I positive on discharge and CAh the capacity:

dSOCdt=−I3600CAh,dvRCdt=R1I−vRCτ1,V=OCV(SOC)−R0I−vRC

The terms have physical readings. R0 lumps the electronic and ionic resistances that respond at once: a current step produces an instant sag R0I. The RC branch represents slower processes — charge transfer and diffusion — that build up over tens of seconds after a current step and relax after it. The teaching values are R0 = 0.25 mΩ, R1 = 0.15 mΩ and τ1 = 30 s at 25 °C [A]. Both resistances rise when the cell is cold, by an Arrhenius-like factor exp(Ea/R·(1/T−1/Tref)) with a teaching activation parameter [A]; at 0 °C they are about 2.5 times their 25 °C value in this model.

ecm_step integrates the equations exactly for a constant current over each step. The heat generated in the cell is the power lost in the two resistors:

Q˙=I2R0+vRC2R1

At steady state (vRC=R1I) this becomes I2(R0+R1) — the test of LAB-09-02-01 checks it. Real cells add reversible (entropic) heat, hysteresis and further RC branches. A first-order model is the usual starting point for estimation and for explaining sag; it is not a design tool for a real pack.

4.5 Voltage sag and heat on the R05 climb

On the loaded R05 climb of M-20481, DT17 needs 1,914.6 kW from the pack for 396.6 s — close to the 1,800 kW the drivetrain is allowed plus the 120 kW auxiliary load [A] — at a steady 12.16 km/h (LAB-09-02-01, from cycle_energy). That is about 2,000 A at the pack and 332 A per cell, 1.2 C. The climb takes 210.9 kWh — 13 % of the pack's nominal energy — in under seven minutes.

FIG-09-02-02 (a) shows the cell voltage after 60 s at 332 A. At 25 °C the sag is 0.13 V at every SOC: 3.104 V at 50 % against an OCV of 3.230 V. At 0 °C the same current sags 0.32 V, to 2.913 V. The sag at the start of the step is smaller (3.145 V at 50 % after 1 s): the RC branch takes tens of seconds to build up.

On the whole pack, LAB-09-02-01 part D runs the climb at constant power with simulate_pack, which solves for the current at each second and flags any second in which the requested power cannot be delivered without a cell falling below a minimum voltage of 2.9 V — a model input [A], not a protection limit:

Four observations.

Current grows as the climb goes on. The voltage falls (SOC drops and the RC branch charges up), so the current needed for the same power rises: from 2,047 A to 2,114 A from 41 % at 25 °C. Heat grows faster than current — with its square — from 29.1 W to 49.6 W per cell.

Cold triples the loss. At 0 °C the same climb from 90 % turns 23.43 kWh into heat, against 8.12 kWh at 25 °C. That heat warms the pack, which in reality lowers its resistance as the climb goes on; the model holds the temperature fixed [A], so it overstates the cold case. Pack heating and cooling are ch-09-04.

Cold and low SOC together cut power. From 41 % at 0 °C the pack delivers full power for the first 22 s; then the voltage reaches 870 V (300 × 2.9 V) and the current must be reduced to keep it there (FIG-09-02-02 (b)). For 375 of the 397 s the pack gives less than the drivetrain asks. The truck slows. Nothing is broken.

At 25 °C nothing limits the climb in this model, at any SOC in the window. The warm pack has margin; the cold pack does not.

4.6 State of power: why available power falls

The previous section asked whether one particular power can be delivered. The BMS asks the reverse question: what is the largest power the pack can deliver over a horizon without a cell leaving its allowed range? That is the state of power (SOP). state_of_power_kw computes it with the same equivalent circuit for a constant discharge from rest: the largest current I for which the cell voltage at the end of the horizon h stays at or above vmin,

V(h)=OCV(SOCh)−I(R0+R1(1−e−h/τ1))≥vmin,SOP=ncellsIvmin

It falls for two reasons: a lower OCV at low SOC leaves less voltage headroom above vmin, and higher resistance at low temperature uses up that headroom with less current. It also falls with the horizon: a pack can give more for 10 s than for a minute, because the RC branch has not built up yet.

LAB-09-02-01 part E computes the SOP over 60 s [A] and the R05 climb speed the ADS can plan with it (ch-09-03: the auxiliary load is served first, and the drivetrain never receives more than its nominal 1,800 kW):

This table is the answer to the scene in section 1.1. A warm pack gives the drivetrain all it wants down to the bottom of the window. A pack at 0 °C limits power from about half charge: 11.17 km/h at 50 %, 10.19 km/h at 41 %, 7.29 km/h at 20 %. At −10 °C even a full pack limits the climb. A cold pack at low SOC is the worst combination, and it is exactly the situation of a truck starting a shift after a cold night with whatever charge it parked with.

The numbers are model outputs at teaching parameters [A]. Real SOP depends on the cells, on how the BMS defines its limits and on the pack's thermal management, which in practice warms a cold pack before and during work (ch-09-04). The mechanism, though, is general: available power is a function of SOC, temperature, horizon and age.

4.7 Cycles per charge

How many M-20481 cycles does DT17 complete between charges? There are three answers, and each is right for its question (LAB-09-02-01 parts B and F).

On energy alone: 3. One M-20481 cycle via R05 takes 369.7 kWh from the battery in 1,539 s: 345.3 kWh of traction, 51.3 kWh of auxiliary load, minus 26.9 kWh recovered on the empty descent (ch-09-01). That is 22.9 % of the pack per cycle. The usable 1,129.0 kWh holds 3.05 cycles — three complete ones (cycles_per_charge).

With the operational reserve: 2. A truck does not drive its battery to the floor and then look for a charger. The FMS's reserve check (ch-09-06; the same rule in simulate_bev_fleet) asks, after each tip at C01, whether the next cycle plus the trip from C01 to the charging station at FB1 plus a margin of 5 % of the pack's energy [A] fits above the 20 % floor, using a conservative SOC (reported SOC minus twice its uncertainty, with an uncertainty of 1 point [A]):

(SOC−2σ−SOCmin)·Enom ≥ Ecycle+EC01→FB1+0.05Enom

The right-hand side is 369.7 + 38.1 + 80.6 = 488.4 kWh; the trip to FB1 counts its auxiliary load as well (reserve_need_kwh, ahs_sim 0.19.1). A truck that leaves the charger at 90 % is at 67.1 % after its first tip and passes; after the second it is at 44.2 % and fails. It charges after every second cycle. The reported SOC needed to pass is 52.3 %.

In the cold: fewer. Losses inside the cells grow when the pack is cold, so less of the window reaches the terminals. Part F runs repeated cycles from 90 % to 20 % at a fixed pack temperature, with no pre-heating and no self-heating — a pessimistic assumption [A]:

At 25 °C the deliverable energy gives 3.00 cycles — slightly less than the 3.05 of the nominal calculation, because 3.4 % of the energy is lost inside the cells. At 0 °C it gives 2.81 and at −10 °C 2.63. The reserve check is not changed by this table, but the margin it keeps shrinks: a cold pack reaches the floor sooner than the nominal calculation expects, and in the cold the climb is also slower, so each cycle takes longer and burns more auxiliary energy. The practical rule [R]: energy-per-cycle and cycles-per-charge figures used for planning must state the pack temperature they assume, and a site with cold mornings must plan its first-cycle charging with the cold figures.

Lithium-ion is a family of chemistries, named mainly after the cathode (and sometimes the anode) material. The comparison below is qualitative and general; it is a teaching summary that is not treated as verified until its source (SRC-PUB-LIION-CHEMISTRY) is checked [TBV]. No quantitative value is given.

For a mine the trade-off is between energy carried per tonne of battery (fewer charges, or a lighter pack), power at low temperature, life in cycles (ch-09-07), and how violent a thermal event can be (ch-09-04). The handbook's generic cell belongs to no family: its OCV curve, resistances and capacity are teaching values [A], chosen so that the models behave like a lithium-ion cell without describing any product.

4.9 Battery-specific hazards

A battery truck adds three hazards to the diesel truck's list, and each changes an existing procedure.

High voltage. The pack is 960 V nominal and can deliver thousands of amperes. Which voltage bands are classed as hazardous and which work practices — qualification, test-before-touch, insulated tools, approach distances — apply are site and standard matters [TBV] (SRC-PUB-HV-WORK-PRACTICE). In the handbook's roles, high-voltage competency is a qualification of a Field Technician (function [A]), not a new role. The isolation procedure of ch-16-04 gains an electrical isolation point — the pack's main contactors and the service disconnect, if one is fitted — and a verification step [H].

Stored energy that cannot be switched off. Opening the contactors isolates the pack terminals from the rest of the vehicle. It does not discharge the cells: every module inside the pack stays charged, and so does the pack as a whole. A diesel truck with the engine stopped and fuel isolated holds chemical energy that needs an ignition source; a charged battery holds electrical energy that needs only a conducting path. "Isolated" therefore means the vehicle's high-voltage circuits are separated from the pack and verified dead, not the battery is safe [H]. Work inside the pack is a specialist activity under its own procedure.

Thermal runaway. A cell that overheats — from an internal short, mechanical damage, overcharge or an external fire — can reach a point at which its own reactions generate heat faster than it can lose it, and the event can spread to neighbouring cells (glossary thermal-runaway). For the purposes of this chapter, three properties matter: it can develop after the truck has stopped and been isolated; it can release gases as well as heat; and fighting it differs from fighting a diesel fire. The mechanism, the detection signals, the response chain and the post-event handling are ch-09-04, which treats SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV. Duration, agents, tactics and exclusion distances are site-procedure items or [TBV]; none is stated here.

Standards: IEC 62619:2022 is a safety standard for secondary lithium cells and batteries for industrial applications, whose published scope covers stationary and motive applications and excludes road vehicles [F] (SRC-STD-IEC62619; catalogue level only — clause content is not verified, and its applicability to mining haul trucks is an inference to be stated carefully). AS 5062:2022 covers fire prevention and protection for mobile and transportable equipment [F] (SRC-STD-AS5062; catalogue level); how it applies to battery-electric mobile plant is a question for ch-09-04 [TBV].

4.10 What the operator sees, and what it means

Three battery indications reach the AHS Controller's HMI in the handbook's design (ch-09-03 specifies the signals). What each one asks of people is a handbook convention [H]:

Indication What it means physically First response Referral
Power limit active SOP below the drivetrain's demand: low SOC, cold pack, high temperature, ageing or a BMS-reported condition None if the cause is low SOC or a cold pack and the truck is moving; record the cause Maintenance if it recurs at normal SOC and temperature, or the cause is a BMS condition
Battery temperature warning The BMS reports a temperature or temperature trend outside its normal band Follow the thermal-event procedure of ch-09-04; do not reset or clear ERT per ch-09-04; never cleared by a timer
Cell imbalance Cells or modules differ in voltage or SOC beyond the BMS's band None in the cycle; plan a balancing or inspection visit Maintenance referral (work order)

The table separates three kinds of message. A power limit is a capability — it changes what the truck can do; by itself it does not call for a safety response. A temperature warning is a safety indication with its own chain. A cell imbalance is a maintenance indication: it reduces usable energy (section 4.1) and may hide a developing fault, but it does not need the controller during the cycle.

4.11 Model limits

Isothermal pack; identical cells; a generic OCV curve without hysteresis; one RC branch; resistance dependent on temperature but not on SOC or current; SOC and SOP computed on the cell model, not on a real BMS; segment powers at the steady speed of ahs_sim.truck (no acceleration). The models show mechanisms. They do not predict a real pack's voltage, power or range.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

A BEV truck leaves the charger at 90 %, completes two M-20481 cycles, and its reserve check after the second tip sends it to FB1. Its pack is warm; it climbs R05 at 12.16 km/h with no power limit at any SOC in the window. No climb takes the SOC below the window or into the reserve.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

The registered scenario: BEV DT17 on M-20481 finishes tipping at C01 with a reported SOC of 41 % [A]; the energy check finds that the next cycle plus the reserve does not fit; the FMS proposes a charging diversion; DT17 charges at FB1 and returns to EX02. Registry times are marked R, LAB-09-02-01 and LAB-09-03-01 times L. This chapter adds a what-if branch [A]: what would have happened had DT17 gone back to EX02 for one more cycle, at 25 °C and with a pack at 0 °C. What-if times (+670.1 s and so on) are counted from T+0 s, the tip at C01.

The registry's 41 % is a little below the 44.2 % that two ideal cycles leave (section 4.7): queue time draws auxiliary power [A]. The what-if shows why the reserve check is right. One more cycle would end below the floor, with no energy left to reach the charger within the window. In the cold it would also have climbed R05 at 9.57 km/h, power-limited for 381 of the 397 s of the climb, holding up the trucks behind it (LAB-09-02-01 part G).

6.3 Abnormal operation — the cold morning of section 1.1

A pack at 0 °C after a night at PK1 [A] and a SOC near 41 %: the climb runs at about 10 km/h with power limit active. Nothing is faulty. The truck completes its cycle; the reserve check after the tip decides about charging as usual. If the same truck shows a power limit on a warm afternoon at 70 %, the cause is not the battery's state but something that needs maintenance (section 4.10).

6.4 Degraded operation — a battery event (SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV)

When the BMS raises a thermal warning, as in SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV, the stored energy of section 4.9 is the reason the response differs from a diesel fire: isolation separates the pack from the vehicle but does not remove the energy in the cells. The response chain is ch-09-04's; this chapter provides only the vocabulary.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Pack voltage. Higher voltage means lower current and lighter conductors for the same power, and a larger high-voltage hazard. 960 V nominal [A] keeps the climb current near 2,000 A.

Series–parallel split. More cells in parallel per group make the pack tolerant of a weak cell (its neighbours share the current) and raise capacity; more in series raise voltage. The module split (here 25s6p [A]) is a compromise between handleable mass, sensing points and the voltage present on a single module during service.

Window. A wider window gives more cycles per charge and leaves less reserve, faster ageing at the extremes and lower power at the bottom. 20–90 % [A] gives three cycles on energy and two with the reserve.

Power sizing for the cold. If the mine must climb R05 at full speed with a pack at 0 °C and 41 %, the pack needs either more cells (higher SOP), heating (ch-09-04), or an accepted slower climb. The choice is economic as much as technical (ch-09-08).

7.2 Failure modes and mitigations

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

Walk the shared timeline of section 6.2 from the battery's side:

  • T+0 s. The BMS's SOC of 41 % is a charge fraction; the FMS turns it into energy above the floor with the nominal pack energy and, for an aged pack, the SOH.
  • T+5 s. The energy check uses the energy of the next cycle on the current route (369.7 kWh via R05; 435.7 kWh via the R24 reroute when R05 is closed, ch-09-01) and the trip to FB1.
  • T+315 s → T+1,420 s. The charge from about 39 % to 90 %: constant 3,000 kW to 80 % and a taper above (ch-09-05).
  • What-if +843.5 s. At the R05 toe, the SOP decides the climb speed; the BMS reports it, the VCS applies it, the ADS plans within it (ch-09-03). Its horizon must match the use: a 10 s value overstates what the pack can sustain on a 397 s climb.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

The truck tile shows SOC with its uncertainty, the power-limit indication with its cause (low SOC, cold pack, high temperature, BMS condition), a battery temperature state, and a cell-imbalance flag [H]. A truck climbing R05 below its normal speed shows whether the cause is a platform limit (power) or an ADS restriction, as for SCN-DERATE-DT17 (ch-07-01).

8.2 Who decides what

8.3 Procedure excerpt — a slow BEV truck on R05

  1. AHS Controller: read the truck's state. Is power limit active shown, and with which cause? If an ADS restriction is the cause, follow ch-07-01; if a battery temperature warning is shown, follow ch-09-04 at once.
  2. AHS Controller: if the cause is low SOC or a cold pack and the truck is moving, let it continue. Record the interval and cause in the truck log. Do not code a delay: a truck running derated gets none; its cause is recorded (delay-codes.yml coding rules).
  3. AHS Controller: if the trucks behind close up, inform the Pit Supervisor; consider, with them, sending the next cold trucks to charge or to warm up before their first climb [A].
  4. AHS Controller: if the cause is a BMS condition, or a power limit recurs at normal SOC and temperature, raise a maintenance referral to the Maintenance Planner with the truck's SOC, temperature and SOP history.
  5. Pit Supervisor: after a cold night, decide whether the first cycle of trucks parked at low SOC starts with a charge.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Controllers learn the difference between energy, capacity and power; that a power limit at low SOC or in the cold is expected behaviour, not a fault; and which indication goes to whom. Pit Supervisors learn the three cycles-per-charge answers. Field Technicians who work on the high-voltage system need a high-voltage qualification [A] and the isolation and verification steps for the pack; everyone learns that an isolated pack is still a charged pack. The ERT's battery training is part of ch-09-04.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-09-02-001 [H] The AMS shall show a power limit reported by the VCS as a platform capability with its cause (low SOC, pack temperature, BMS condition), distinct from any ADS speed restriction and from any battery temperature warning. Rationale: a power limit at low SOC or low temperature is expected behaviour and must not be mistaken for a fault or a restriction (sections 4.6, 4.10, 6.3). Verification: inspection of the HMI design; demonstration with LAB-09-02-01 cases (41 % at 0 °C).

REQ-09-02-002 [H] Every range, cycles-per-charge and reserve calculation of the FMS shall use usable energy — nominal energy × (upper minus lower SOC of the operating window) × SOH — and never nominal energy. Rationale: nominal energy overstates what a truck can use by the window and the ageing (section 4.2). Verification: test (LAB-09-02-01: 1,129.0 kWh usable, 3 cycles on energy, 2 with the reserve); inspection.

REQ-09-02-003 [H] Energy-per-cycle and cycles-per-charge figures used for planning shall state the pack temperature they assume, and the site's planning shall include a cold-pack case. Rationale: at 0 °C the deliverable energy falls to 1,040.3 kWh and the climb is power-limited (sections 4.6, 4.7). Verification: inspection of planning parameters; test (LAB-09-02-01 part F).

REQ-09-02-004 [H] Battery indications shall be assigned to a response class — controller action, maintenance referral or the thermal-event chain of ch-09-04 — and the assignment shall be part of the controller procedure. Rationale: a power limit, a cell imbalance and a temperature warning ask different things of different people (section 4.10). Verification: inspection of the procedure and the HMI; exercise with SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV and SCN-FIRE-DT17-BEV.

REQ-09-02-005 [H] The isolation procedure for a BEV truck shall include the high-voltage isolation point(s) and a verification that the vehicle's high-voltage circuits are dead, shall state that the pack itself remains charged, and shall be carried out only by Field Technicians holding the site's high-voltage qualification. Rationale: opening the contactors separates the pack from the vehicle but does not discharge it (section 4.9). Verification: inspection of the procedure against ch-16-04; competency records.

REQ-09-02-006 [R] The site should define the power-limit behaviour it accepts on R05 — the lowest climb speed for a cold or low-SOC pack — and choose between pack heating, charging before the first cycle and accepting the slower climb. Rationale: from 35.4 % at 0 °C the climb drops to 9.57 km/h and slows the trucks behind (section 6.2). Verification: inspection of the site's operating rules; analysis with LAB-09-02-01.

10. Verification and validation

Verification (make test CH=ch-09-02, 8 tests): the hierarchy arithmetic; usable energy, energy per cycle, cycles on energy and with the reserve; OCV monotonic and the cell sag values; the R05 climb on the pack at four SOCs and two temperatures (voltages, currents, heat, losses, power-limited seconds); SOP falling with SOC and with temperature and the climb speeds; cycles per charge against temperature; the SCN-SOC-DT17-BEV what-if.

Validation needs real data: a measured OCV curve and resistances over temperature for the chosen cells, pack voltage and current on the R05 climb, SOP as the real BMS reports it, and pack temperatures over a cold night. The model shows mechanisms; it predicts no real pack.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Sizing range with nominal energy, or with the energy of a new pack for its whole life.
  • One SOP number. A 10 s value is not what the pack can sustain for a 397 s climb.
  • Planning the climb at 25 °C only.
  • Ignoring the weakest cell. In a series string, it limits everyone.
  • Treating the module split as a detail. It sets the voltage present during module work and the number of temperature sensors.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Withdrawing a truck for a power limit at low SOC in the cold. It is a capability, not a fault.
  • Ignoring a power limit on a warm truck at high SOC. That one needs maintenance.
  • Quoting "three cycles" when the reserve allows two.
  • Calling an isolated battery truck "safe". The pack is still charged.
  • Clearing a battery temperature warning because it went away. Thermal events follow ch-09-04.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — pack arithmetic. 300 cells of 3.2 V in series: 960 V. Six in parallel of 280 Ah: 1,680 Ah. Energy: 960 V × 1,680 Ah = 1,612.8 kWh. Pack resistance: 0.25 mΩ × 300 / 6 = 12.5 mΩ.

Example 2 — climb current and C-rate. 1,914.6 kW at about 935 V (41 %, 25 °C): about 2,047 A at the start of the climb, 341 A per cell, 1.22 C. Instant sag at 25 °C: 341 A × 0.25 mΩ = 85 mV per cell; after the RC branch builds up a further 0.15 mΩ × 341 A ≈ 51 mV.

Example 3 — cycles per charge. Usable 1,612.8 × 0.70 = 1,129.0 kWh; 1,129.0 / 369.7 = 3.05 cycles. Reserve check after the second tip: SOC 44.2 %, conservative 42.2 %, (0.422 − 0.20) × 1,612.8 = 358 kWh < 488.4 kWh: charge.

Example 4 — the cold climb. From 35.4 % at 0 °C the SOP over 60 s is 1,537 kW. Minus 120 kW auxiliary: 1,417 kW to the drivetrain instead of 1,800 kW. Speed scales with power on a power-limited climb: 12.16 × 1,417 / 1,800 ≈ 9.57 km/h.

15. Key takeaways

  • Series adds voltage, parallel adds capacity; energy is their product: 300s6p of 3.2 V / 280 Ah cells is 960 V, 1,680 Ah, 1,612.8 kWh.
  • Nominal 1,612.8 kWh, usable 1,129.0 kWh, deliverable 1,110.3 kWh at 25 °C: plan with usable × SOH.
  • One M-20481 cycle takes 369.7 kWh, 22.9 % of the pack: three cycles on energy, two with the reserve, fewer in the cold.
  • Voltage sags with current, more when cold; power falls at low SOC and low temperature: 10.19 km/h on R05 from 41 % at 0 °C instead of 12.16 km/h.
  • A power limit is a capability, a temperature warning a safety indication, an imbalance a maintenance item.
  • An isolated pack is still a charged pack; thermal runaway is ch-09-04.
