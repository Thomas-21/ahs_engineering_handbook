1. Overview

Core in five points. Read these first; each links to the section that develops it.

  1. The load–haul cycle has seven components. For DT17 on M-20481 they sum to 1564 s (26.1 min), and travel is 84 % of it (section 4.1, section 4.5).
  2. Loaded up R05 the truck is power-limited at about 11.4 km/h; on flat legs the site speed limit sets the pace. Ask which limit binds before changing one (section 4.3, section 4.4).
  3. A speed restriction above the loaded climbing speed slows only the empty descent and still costs: 20 km/h on R05 adds 121 s per cycle and two loads per truck per shift. Queue time adds one for one (section 6.4, section 6.3).
  4. Each part of the cycle has an owner: the FMS records and interprets it, the AMS restricts and holds, the ADS and VCS drive and report, and people load, supervise and decide; a slow cycle is diagnosed component by component (section 5, section 8.3).
  5. The 1564 s is the answer of one model under stated conditions: one truck, steady speeds, no queues, no losses. The fleet and live models give other numbers for stated reasons (section 4.8; ch-23-01 section 4.10).

Reading order. Core path: sections 1 and 2, 4.1 and 4.3 to 4.5 and 4.8, 5, the shared timeline in 6.1, the restriction table in 6.4, 8.1 and the procedure in 8.3, the calculator in 13, and 15. Detail sections (the route table, the four loading units compared, the Engineering view, verification, mistakes, worked examples) and reference sections (terms, requirements, sources) are folded in the article and open from their summary line or from any link into them.

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is 06:15 on a DAY shift at the reference mine. Haul truck DT17 reverses into the loading area beside shovel EX02 on the pit floor, 120 m below the crest. Nobody is in its cab. The shovel operator starts the four-pass load; two minutes later DT17 pulls away with 220 t of ore, heads for the main ramp R05 and begins the slow climb to the crusher C01. Twenty-six minutes after it first reversed into EX02 it will be back for the next load. Over the shift it will do this about twenty-seven times.

Follow DT17's route in FIG-01-01-04. The numbered markers identify the seven stages of the haul cycle analysed in section 4.1.

Dispatch, traffic coordination, perception, safety functions and control-room procedures all support this cycle. Engineers and operations teams need to know how long each stage takes, which decisions belong to people or systems, and how a change in cycle time affects the tonnes moved over a shift.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Decompose the haul cycle of mission M-20481 into spot, load, loaded travel, queue, spot, dump and empty travel, and state which reference-mine parameter drives each component.
  2. Calculate loaded and empty travel times for M-20481 segment by segment with the steady-state speed model, using the grade and rolling resistance of R05 from mine.yaml.
  3. Explain why loaded travel up R05 is power-limited while empty travel down R05 is capped by the retarding limit or the site speed limit, and show which limit binds with the reference parameters.
  4. Compare the cycle times of ore routes (EX01, EX02 → C01) and waste routes (EX03, EX04 → D01) and identify the segments that dominate each.
  5. Specify the input data a cycle-time model needs (segment lengths, grades, speed limits, truck class, loading, spot and dump times) and trace each value to mine.yaml or equipment.yaml.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Describe the load–haul cycle at the reference mine in the order a Shovel Operator and an AHS Controller experience it, from DT17 spotting at EX02 to tipping at C01.
  2. Identify which cycle components the field can influence during a shift (queueing, spotting, payload, road condition on R05) and which are fixed by the mine plan and the road network.
  3. Estimate the effect of a slower R05 (for example a temporary speed restriction) on the number of loads DT17 completes in a 12 h shift, using the haul-cycle calculator.
  4. Explain to a Shovel Operator how spotting time and the number of bucket passes at EX02 add to DT17's cycle, and which loading-area practices shorten them.
  5. Compare the planned and the observed cycle of DT17 on M-20481 over one shift and identify which cycle component accounts for the difference.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

An open-pit mine earns its living by moving rock. Ore has to reach the primary crusher; waste rock that covers the ore has to be moved to the waste dump so that mining can continue. At the reference mine both flows are carried by one fleet of 40 autonomous haul trucks, loaded by four hydraulic shovels: EX01 and EX02 load ore on the pit floor at RL 300 and send it to crusher C01; EX03 and EX04 load waste on the RL 330 bench and send it to dump D01 [H].

The mine plan states how many tonnes of each material must move in each period. The control room's question is more immediate: this shift, are we moving rock as fast as the plan assumed, and if not, where is the time going? That question can only be answered if everyone agrees on what one cycle is, how long it should take and why.

Consider three conversations that happen at this mine in a typical week:

  • The Pit Supervisor sees that ore trucks are averaging 28 minutes per cycle instead of the planned 26 and asks the AHS Controller whether the trucks are "running slow".
  • The Shovel Operator at EX02 says the trucks keep arriving in bunches, so the shovel sits idle and then a queue forms.
  • Survey has reported soft spots on R05 after rain, and someone proposes a temporary speed restriction of 20 km/h on the ramp. What will it cost?

Each conversation is about the load–haul cycle. Each needs a number, and the numbers have to come from the same model, using the same data, so that engineering and operations are not arguing past each other. This chapter builds that model for the reference mine and uses it to answer all three questions. Losses (delays, standby, breakdowns), queueing between trucks and the formal KPI definitions follow in ch-01-05.

3. Terminology and foundations

ReferenceShow section 3

Open-pit mine. A surface mine developed downward in horizontal steps called benches. Equipment works on the benches and on the pit floor; ramps connect benches with each other and with the surface. At the reference mine the pit floor is at RL 300 and the surface (crest) at RL 420 (RL = reduced level, metres above a local datum) [H].

Haul road and ramp. A haul road is any road used by haul trucks. A ramp is a haul road on a grade. The reference mine has a main ramp, R05 (1.34 km at about 9 %), and a longer secondary west ramp, R24 (about 6.3 %), added in mine version 0.2.0 [H]. R05 is the shortest route for every shovel; R24 matters when R05 is closed (ch-01-03, ch-01-07).

Road grade. Rise over horizontal run, as a percentage. Grade is signed in the direction of travel: R05 is +8.99 % for a truck climbing from the pit and −8.99 % for a truck descending into it.

Loading unit, face and loading area. The shovel digs at the face and loads trucks in its loading area. Each shovel has a queue point (EX02-Q) where trucks wait, and a loading spot (EX02-L) where they are loaded.

Spotting. Manoeuvring the truck into the exact loading or tipping position. In an autonomous system the truck spots itself, but the position is defined by the loading unit or the destination.

Bucket pass. One bucket load. The reference shovel class loads a 220 t truck in four passes of 55 t, with a swing cycle of 33 s, so loading takes 132 s [A].

Payload. Mass carried. The reference truck class HT220 has a rated payload of 220 t and an empty mass of 165 t, so the gross vehicle mass is 385 t [A].

Destination, tipping. C01 is a primary crusher with one tipping position; D01 is a waste dump with three active tip points [A]. Tipping means raising the body to discharge the load.

Rolling resistance. Resistance to motion from tyre and road deformation, expressed as an equivalent grade. A well-maintained haul road might be modelled at 2.5 %; the reference mine uses 3 % on R05 and 4 % in loading and tipping areas [A].

Rimpull and retarding. Rimpull is the tractive force at the rim of the driving wheels. Retarding is the continuous braking used to hold speed on a downhill grade. Uphill, the truck is limited by how much power it can turn into rimpull; downhill, by how much energy it can safely dissipate.

Haul-cycle time. The elapsed time for one complete cycle: from the moment the truck starts spotting at the shovel until it starts spotting at the same shovel again.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The cycle as seven components

The load–haul cycle is a closed loop of seven activities (FIG-01-01-01). For mission M-20481 the values come from ahs_sim with the reference parameters [A].

Model conditions: M1, the steady-state cycle (ahs_sim.haul_cycle.compute_cycle; ahs_sim 0.27.0, mine 0.3.0); one truck (HT220, 220 t), EX02 → C01; one cycle; queues none (0 s entered); acceleration no; disturbances none; deterministic, no seed. The same mine in other models gives other numbers: see the comparison table in ch-23-01 section 4.10.

Queueing can also occur at the shovel (waiting at EX02-Q). In this chapter the baseline has no queue anywhere; ch-01-05 shows how queues form when many trucks share a shovel or a destination.

Two features of this table drive the rest of the chapter. First, travel (components 3 and 7) is 1307 s, or 84 % of the cycle. Second, the fixed activities at the shovel and the crusher (components 1, 2, 5 and 6) add up to 257 s — small per cycle, but repeated on every cycle of every truck.

4.3 The steady-state speed model

On each leg the truck is assumed to travel at a constant, steady-state speed. When the net resistance is positive (climbing, or on the flat where rolling resistance still acts), the speed is set by a power balance. The tractive force available at the wheels at speed v is F=ηP/v; the resisting force is the component of weight along the slope plus rolling resistance, mg(G+RR) for small grades. Setting the two equal and capping by the limits gives

v=min(vlimit, vmax, ηPmg(G+RR))if G+RR>0

where η is drivetrain efficiency, P engine net power, m gross mass, g=9.81 m/s2, G the signed grade and RR the rolling resistance as an equivalent grade. When G+RR≤0 the truck would accelerate without braking, so its speed is capped by the retarding limit instead:

v=min(vlimit, vmax, vretard)if G+RR≤0

with vretard = 30 km/h loaded and 40 km/h empty for HT220 [A]. The model ignores acceleration, braking distances, corners and interaction with other trucks; these are added in Volume 07 (ch-07-01, ch-07-02) and Volume 10 (ch-10-02).

FIG-01-01-02 plots the result along the loaded route: elevation on top, steady-state speed below.

4.4 Which limit binds on R05

Loaded, climbing. With m = 385 000 kg, G+RR=0.0899+0.03=0.1199, ηP = 0.80 × 1.8 MW:

v=0.80×1.8×106385000×9.81×0.1199=3.18 m/s≈11.4 km/h

This is far below the 40 km/h site limit, so the climb is power-limited. The 1339.6 m of R05 takes 421 s loaded — 52 % of loaded travel time, although R05 is only 30 % of the loaded distance.

Empty, descending. Now G+RR=−0.0899+0.03=−0.0599, which is negative: gravity overcomes rolling resistance and the truck must hold its speed with the retarder. The empty retarding cap (40 km/h) and the site speed limit on R05 (40 km/h) are equal, so with the reference parameters both limits bind at the same value and the descent takes 121 s. If the site limit were lowered, the site limit would bind alone; if a heavier truck class had a lower retarding cap, the retarder would bind alone. Asking "which limit binds?" before changing either one is a habit worth forming.

Flat roads. On R01 (flat, 2.5 %) the power-limited speed of a loaded HT220 would be about 55 km/h, so the 50 km/h site limit binds. On most flat legs the site speed limit, not the truck, sets the pace.

4.5 Cycle time, loads per shift and productivity

The cycle time is the sum of the seven components:

tcycle=tspot,L+tload+∑iLivi+tqueue+tspot,D+tdump+∑jLjvj

From it follow the two numbers operations cares about. With no losses, a truck completes n=⌊3600Tshift/tcycle⌋ loads in a shift of Tshift hours, and its productivity is Q=3600mpayload/tcycle tonnes per hour. For M-20481: 27.6 cycles fit in 12 h, so 27 complete loads, and Q = 506 t/h [A]. Real shifts have losses — shift change, refuelling, delays — so these are upper bounds; ch-01-05 introduces the time usage model that turns them into realistic estimates.

4.7 How many trucks does a shovel need?

DetailShow 4.7

A shovel can only load one truck at a time. If every truck arrived exactly when the previous one left, a shovel would be busy for tspot,L+tload = 167 s per truck. The number of trucks needed to keep it continuously busy is therefore

N=tcycletspot,L+tload

For EX02 this is 1564 / 167 = 9.4 trucks; for EX03, 1915 / 167 = 11.5. Summed over the four shovels the reference mine would need about 41.5 trucks to keep every shovel busy with perfectly regular arrivals — close to its 40-truck fleet [A]. In reality arrivals are irregular, so with 40 trucks there will be both shovel idle time and truck queues at the same time. That trade-off — the match factor — is the subject of ch-01-05 and ch-10-02. The steady-state model in this chapter only tells us the ideal ratio.

4.8 Trade-offs and model limits

The model is deliberately simple. Its value is that every number can be traced to a parameter and recomputed when that parameter changes. Its limits are equally clear:

  • No acceleration or braking. Each transition from 15 to 40 km/h or from 50 to 11 km/h takes time and distance that the model ignores. Real cycle times are therefore longer, mainly on routes with many short legs.
  • No interaction. Trucks do not follow, pass, wait at intersections or bunch. All queueing is entered by hand as tqueue.
  • No losses. The truck hauls for the whole shift.
  • No payload limits. As section 6.2 shows, the model rewards overloading. That is a model blind spot, not a recommendation.

Other models of the same mine therefore give other numbers, each under its own conditions: 1592.3 s for the cycle with acceleration (ch-07-01), 184,140 t for a 37-truck shift with queues (the baseline shift log of Volume 22), less again in the live world of the simulator. ch-23-01 section 4.10 lists them in one table with the conditions that explain each difference.

5. Responsibilities and interface boundaries

Who owns each part of the cycle in the handbook's reference architecture (FMS → AMS → ADS → VCS) [H]:

Element Responsibility in the load–haul cycle
FMS Assigns DT17 to EX02 and C01 (assignment), sets the production plan, records cycle components and computes cycle KPIs
AMS Turns the assignment into mission M-20481; reserves road segments and the tipping bay; applies speed restrictions; holds trucks at queue points
ADS Drives each leg within the granted path and speed limits; spots at EX02-L and C01; reports position and state
VCS Executes propulsion, retarding, steering and body raise; reports payload, speeds and faults
Independent safety functions Can stop DT17 at any point in the cycle regardless of AMS or ADS state (Volume 04)
Onboard safety-related systems Payload monitoring reports the load after the last pass; fire and tyre monitoring can end a cycle early (ch-08-05)
People Shovel Operator loads and signals the truck away; AHS Controller supervises cycles and exceptions; Pit Supervisor owns production on the shift; Survey maintains road data

Two boundaries matter for this chapter. First, the cycle components are observed by ADS and VCS but recorded and interpreted by FMS: if the events that mark component boundaries are defined differently in different systems, the control room sees cycles that do not add up (section 7.3). Second, a speed restriction is an AMS action that changes ADS behaviour; FMS must know about it, or its expected cycle times will be wrong for the rest of the shift.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-PAYLOAD-OVER-EX02

DetailShow 6.2

At T+167 s payload monitoring on DT17 reports 250 t instead of 220 t (scenario SCN-PAYLOAD-OVER-EX02). In the steady-state model the heavier truck climbs R05 at 10.6 km/h instead of 11.4 km/h, and the cycle grows by 33 s. Productivity in t/h actually rises in the model (564 t/h instead of 506 t/h), because the model knows nothing about tyre loads, frame stress, brake capacity or the site's payload policy. This is exactly the kind of result that an engineer must not hand to operations without comment: the model is correct about time and silent about consequences. Payload management is covered in ch-13-04 and the onboard reaction in ch-08-05.

6.3 Abnormal operation — SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN (briefly)

DetailShow 6.3

If C01 stops accepting trucks (SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN), ore trucks wait at C01-Q. Queue time adds to the cycle one second for one second: 120 s of queue per cycle turns 27 loads per truck per shift into 25; 300 s turns it into 23. The cycle is only as fast as its destination. What FMS should do — hold, reassign, or change shovel material — is the subject of ch-15-06 and ch-10-07.

6.4 Degraded operation — speed restriction on R05

7. Engineering view

DetailShow section 7 (7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs; 7.2 Failure modes and mitigations; 7.3 Interface and timing considerations)

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Route choice: distance or time? ahs_sim routes by shortest distance. For the ore routes of the reference mine (EX01, EX02 → C01) the shortest route is also the fastest. For the waste routes it is not: from the RL 330 bench the longer west ramp R24 is about 60 s faster loaded and about 20 s slower empty, so a waste cycle via R24 would be about 39 s shorter than the shortest-distance cycle in section 4.6 [A]. A longer route with a gentler grade can be faster loaded and slower empty. Routing on expected travel time requires the speed model to be part of the planner; routing on distance does not. Volume 10 (ch-10-01, ch-10-03) discusses both.

Speed limits. Site speed limits are set for safety and road condition, not for productivity. On flat legs the limit is what sets the cycle, so any proposal to raise a limit must be argued on safety grounds first; on the loaded ramp the limit has no effect at all.

Fixed times. Spot, load and dump times look small but appear on every cycle. Shortening spotting at EX02 by 10 s saves 10 s on every cycle of every truck assigned to EX02. These times depend on loading-area design, shovel–truck coordination and truck manoeuvring logic (Volume 13).

Model granularity. A steady-state model is enough for planning and for explaining cycles; it is not enough for control, for safety analysis or for detailed traffic behaviour. The same parameters feed the more detailed models of Volume 07 — one source of truth, several fidelities.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

The baseline timeline in section 6.1 rests on seven cycle events (the two ramp rows are position reports, not cycle events). Each event must be produced by a defined system, at a defined moment, with a timestamp from a synchronised clock:

  • start spotting at shovel (ADS, when the truck enters the loading spot manoeuvre),
  • loading starts and loading complete (ADS and the loading unit; with payload from VCS at completion),
  • arrive at destination, spotted at destination, tipping complete (ADS and VCS),
  • arrive back at the loading unit.

FMS computes component durations as differences between consecutive events. If any event is missing, late or duplicated, the component durations are wrong even though the trucks are fine. This is why cycle analysis is an interface topic before it is an analytics topic: the definitions belong in the interface control document between ADS/VCS and FMS (ch-02-06). The timing tolerance needed depends on the use — seconds for cycle analysis, much tighter for traffic coordination (Volume 10).

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

On the reference mine's control-room display [H] the controller sees, for each truck, its mission, current cycle component and elapsed time, and a comparison with the expected value. For DT17 at T+600 s the useful view is "loaded travel, on R05, 433 s into loaded travel, expected 804 s". A cycle that is running long shows which component is long. Shift-level views show cycle-time distributions by loading unit, loads per truck and tonnes per hour against plan. By handbook convention [H], slow cycles do not raise alarms by themselves; they are an operational signal, not a fault.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — investigating a slow cycle on M-20481

  1. AHS Controller: open the cycle breakdown for M-20481 for the last hour; compare each component with the expected value (baseline 35 / 132 / 804 / 0 / 40 / 50 / 503 s).
  2. AHS Controller: if loaded or empty travel is long, check active speed restrictions and truck derates on R05 first, then the other legs.
  3. AHS Controller: if queue is long, check the destination state (C01-Q) and the number of trucks assigned to C01; inform the Pit Supervisor.
  4. Shovel Operator: if spot or load is long at EX02, report face condition, bucket fill and floor condition to the controller.
  5. Pit Supervisor: decide on corrective action (road maintenance, reassignment, loading practice) and record the decision in the shift log.
  6. AHS Controller: after the action, confirm on the next three cycles that the component has returned to its expected value; remove any temporary restriction that is no longer needed.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

DetailShow 8.4

The cycle feeds three KPIs used at the reference mine: cycle time by loading unit and destination, loads per truck per shift, and tonnes per hour (per truck and per fleet). Queue time and speed restrictions show up as longer components rather than as separate events; how time is classified into the categories of the GMG time usage model (such as Productive Time, Operating Delay and Standby) — and the delay codes used for it — is defined in ch-01-05.

8.5 Training and competency implications

DetailShow 8.5

AHS Controllers need to read a cycle breakdown and know the expected value of each component for the main missions — not to the second, but well enough to recognise which component is out of line. Shovel Operators need to understand that their spotting and loading seconds multiply across every truck. Supervisors need the habit of asking "what does this cost per cycle and per shift?" before approving restrictions or practices. The haul-cycle calculator (WGT-01-01-01) is suitable for that training.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

ReferenceShow section 9

REQ-01-01-001 [H] The FMS shall record, for every completed haul cycle, a timestamp for each of the following events: start of spotting at the loading unit, start of loading, end of loading, arrival at the destination, end of spotting at the destination, end of tipping, and arrival back at the loading unit. Rationale: component durations (section 4.1) are differences between these events; a missing event makes the breakdown impossible. Verification: inspection of the event schema; test with the SCN-BASELINE-SHIFT timeline.

REQ-01-01-002 [H] The cycle-time model used for planning shall take all geometry from the version-controlled reference configuration and shall record that configuration version with every result. Rationale: hand-copied lengths and grades drift from the AHS map (section 7.2). Verification: inspection; test (LAB-01-01-01 reports values computed from mine.yaml).

REQ-01-01-003 [H] When the AMS applies or removes a speed restriction on a road segment, it shall notify the FMS, and the FMS shall recompute the expected cycle times of the missions that use that segment. Rationale: otherwise expected and observed cycles diverge for the rest of the shift (section 7.2). Verification: test (LAB-01-01-01 experiment 2 gives the expected values for R05 restrictions); demonstration.

REQ-01-01-004 [H] The control-room display shall show, for each truck on a mission, the current cycle component, its elapsed time and its expected duration. Rationale: controllers diagnose slow cycles by component (section 8.3). Verification: demonstration.

REQ-01-01-005 [H] Expected cycle times presented to controllers shall be computed by the same model and configuration version as those used for planning, and shall not be entered manually. Rationale: a single source of truth for expected values (section 7.1). Verification: inspection; test.

10. Verification and validation

DetailShow section 10

Verification asks whether the model computes what it claims to compute. For this chapter:

  • the power-limited speed on R05 is checked against a hand calculation (section 4.4, 11.4 km/h);
  • ahs_sim unit tests check derived geometry, routing, speed limits and the cycle breakdown;
  • the lab tests check the reference values in section 4.6 and the monotonic effect of restrictions;
  • the browser calculator in section 13 is checked against ahs_sim on seven reference cases with a relative tolerance of 10−9 (golden-value test).

Validation asks whether the model is good enough for its purpose at a real mine. That requires observed cycle data — event timestamps per REQ-01-01-001 — compared with model predictions by component. A steady-state model typically underestimates travel time because it ignores acceleration and braking; at a real site the calibration would be documented as part of the planning model's evidence. The reference mine has no real data, so in this handbook validation is limited to comparisons between models of different fidelity (Volume 23).

11. Common mistakes

DetailShow section 11 (11.1 Design mistakes; 11.2 Operational mistakes)

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Averaging speeds instead of times. The arithmetic mean of the seven loaded-leg speeds of M-20481 is 28.8 km/h, which would predict 563 s for the 4.50 km loaded haul. The correct value is 804 s, because the truck spends most of its time on the slow leg. Always sum Li/vi.
  • Ignoring the sign of the grade. The same road is uphill one way and downhill the other; using one grade for both directions gives wrong empty times.
  • Treating the speed limit as the speed. On R05 the loaded truck runs at 11.4 km/h against a 40 km/h limit.
  • Horizontal instead of slope length. For M-20481 the difference is small (4495 m against 4500 m), but it is a symptom of geometry being handled by hand.
  • Presenting model blind spots as results. The model says overloading raises t/h. Report what the model cannot see together with what it computes.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Blaming the flat roads. A slow ore cycle is far more likely to come from R05 or from a queue at C01.
  • Leaving a restriction in place. A 20 km/h restriction on R05 costs two loads per truck per shift; it must be removed as soon as the road is fit again (step 6 of the procedure).
  • Ignoring queue time. A 2-minute queue at C01 costs as much as a 20 km/h restriction on R05.
  • Comparing cycles across shovels. EX03 cycles are six minutes longer than EX02 cycles because of the route, not because EX03 is slower.

12. Worked examples

DetailShow section 12

All values [A], computed with ahs_sim for HT220 on mine version 0.3.0, with model M1 (steady-state cycle, one truck, no queue unless stated, no acceleration, no seed; ch-23-01 section 4.10).

Example 1 — R05 climbing speed. v=0.80×1.8×106/(385000×9.81×0.1199) = 3.18 m/s = 11.4 km/h; time on R05 = 1339.6 m / 3.18 m/s = 421 s.

Example 2 — full cycle. 35 + 132 + 804 + 0 + 40 + 50 + 503 = 1564 s = 26.1 min.

Example 3 — loads per shift. 12 × 3600 / 1564 = 27.6, so 27 complete loads; 27 × 220 t = 5940 t per truck per shift with no losses.

Example 4 — cost of a 20 km/h restriction on R05. The empty descent slows from 40 to 20 km/h: 1339.6 m at 20 km/h takes 241 s instead of 121 s. The cycle becomes 1685 s; loads per shift fall from 27 to 25. Across the 19 or so trucks that serve the two ore shovels, that is about 38 loads, or roughly 8400 t of ore, per shift.

Example 5 — trucks to keep EX02 busy. N = 1564 / (35 + 132) = 9.4 trucks.

Example 6 — the averaging trap. Mean of leg speeds = (15 + 30 + 40 + 11.4 + 50 + 40 + 15) / 7 = 28.8 km/h; 4500.5 m / (28.8 / 3.6) m/s = 563 s, which underestimates the loaded haul by 241 s (30 %).

15. Key takeaways

  • The load–haul cycle has seven components; for M-20481 they sum to 1564 s (26.1 min), and travel is 84 % of it.
  • The loaded climb of R05 is power-limited at about 11.4 km/h and takes 52 % of loaded travel time.
  • On flat legs the site speed limit sets the pace; on the loaded ramp it has no effect.
  • Restrictions above the loaded climbing speed slow only the empty descent, but they are not free: on R05 a 20 km/h restriction costs 121 s per cycle and two loads per truck per shift.
  • Queue time adds one-for-one: fixing the destination is often worth more than fixing the road.
  • Every number must be traceable to the reference configuration; average times, never speeds.
