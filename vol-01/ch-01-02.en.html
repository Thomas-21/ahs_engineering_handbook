1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is 13:20 on a DAY shift. DT17, running empty along the dump haul road R03, has a drivetrain fault and stops in its lane [A].

A driver could report the truck's location and symptoms by radio. In this case, nobody is in the cab. The AHS Controller sees the stop on screen five seconds later. The Field Technician is in the workshop, 2.8 km away by road, and can enter the autonomous area once the required permissions are granted and confirmed. DT17 returns to work 44.7 minutes after the stop.

The drivetrain fault illustrates how autonomy redistributes the driver's tasks. Systems detect and report the stop. People outside the truck coordinate access, diagnose the fault and organise recovery.

This chapter answers three questions:

  • Which of the driver's tasks go to which system or role?
  • What does the move cost, and gain, in hours spent hauling?
  • Which new procedures and skills does it require?

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Explain which functions of a human haul-truck driver are reallocated to ADS, AMS, independent safety functions and onboard safety-related systems, and which remain with people.
  2. Analyse why an autonomous truck that stops is not "safe and finished": identify the system states and permissions that must persist until DT17 is recovered.
  3. Explain why manned vehicles in an autonomous area need position reporting units and digital access permissions, using LV07 entering AA-North as the example.
  4. Identify the failure modes that autonomy brings to the reference mine (localisation degradation on R05, loss of wireless link, nuisance stops) that a human driver would previously have absorbed.
  5. Describe how rules a driver applied by judgement (giving way at Intersection X, slowing on a wet R05) become explicit system rules, reservations and map attributes.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Compare a manned and an autonomous shift at the reference mine and list the changes to time usage (shift change, crib breaks, whether an operator is present) that affect productivity.
  2. Describe how entering AA-North changes for LV07's driver once trucks are autonomous: positive communication, access permission and radio protocol.
  3. Explain who responds, and how, when there is no driver in the cab — for a stopped truck (DT17 on R03) and for a fire on DT17.
  4. Identify the tasks that remain manual after autonomy (dozing at EX02 with DZ03, watering with WT02, recovering stopped trucks, inspections) and how each now interacts with autonomous traffic.
  5. Describe the new competencies that controllers and field crews need compared with a manned operation (positive communication, permission handling, remote supervision).

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine is a brownfield site: the same pit, roads and much of the same workforce once ran a manned truck fleet, and autonomy was introduced into that operation [A]. Three conversations from one week show what the change means.

  • The Pit Supervisor has heard that autonomous trucks "do not take crib breaks". She asks the AHS Controller why EX02 is not loading noticeably more trucks per shift than in the manned days.
  • The driver of LV07, a pit patrol vehicle, used to call "LV07 entering the pit via the main ramp" and drive down. Now the driver waits at access point AP-02 until a permission appears on the field terminal, while ore trucks take the longer west ramp. Why does a short job now need a procedure?
  • The AHS Controller has DT17 stopped on R03. The driver used to handle the first ten minutes. Who goes now, how do they get there, and when can the truck move again?

The questions are about time, access and response, and each has a quantitative side and a side about who decides. Both come from one fact: the driver carried many tasks, most never written down, and autonomy forces every one of them to be reassigned explicitly. This chapter makes the reassignment visible, quantifies it with ahs_sim, and introduces procedures developed later (access in ch-10-06 and ch-16-03, recovery in ch-16-05, fire in ch-04-07).

Productivity gains from autonomy at real operations are not quantified here. Every number comes from the illustrative reference-mine model [A]; the point is the mechanism, not a claim about any site.

3. Terminology and foundations

Manned and autonomous operation. In a manned operation each haul truck has a driver on board. In an autonomous operation the trucks run without one, under the handbook's reference architecture FMS → AMS → ADS → VCS (ch-01-04) [H]. The reference mine is a mixed fleet: shovels, dozers, water trucks and light vehicles remain manned and share roads and loading areas with the autonomous trucks.

Function allocation. The assignment of each task in an operation to a system element or a human role. The move to autonomy is a re-allocation: every task the driver performed must end up somewhere.

Autonomous area and access point. An area in which autonomous machines operate and entry is controlled: AA-North (the pit), AA-Central (main roads, X, C01) and AA-South (D01). People and manned vehicles request entry at access points AP-01 (MIA gate G01), AP-02 (pit crest P1), AP-03 (Intersection Y) and AP-04 (west ramp crest P3) [H].

Positive communication and digital access permission. Positive communication is the human protocol of request, permission, read-back and confirmation before entering a controlled area, carried by radio and field terminal. The digital access permission is its machine side: a record held by AMS that authorises a named person or vehicle to occupy a defined area for a defined time, and that keeps autonomous traffic out of that area while it is active [H].

Fail closed. A permission that is lost, stale, duplicated, ambiguous or unacknowledged counts as not granted (master-instructions §3). A truck without a valid permission does not move; a person without one does not enter.

Position reporting unit (PRU) and field terminal. Every manned vehicle entering an autonomous area carries a PRU, which reports its position to the system, a two-way radio and a field terminal for requests and acknowledgements (equipment.yaml).

Controlled stop, isolation and stand-off point. A controlled stop brakes the truck to standstill along its path. Isolation secures a machine against unexpected movement or energy release before a person works on it. A stand-off point is where an attending person stops, short of a stopped truck, to confirm isolation before approaching on foot (50 m at the reference mine [A]).

Exclusion zone and work zone. An exclusion zone is closed to everyone while a hazard persists (a truck on fire); a work zone is reserved for manned work, with autonomous traffic excluded (DZ03 at EX02).

Remote supervision. Directing the fleet from the control room through the HMI, without seeing the trucks.

Time usage terms. The handbook follows the GMG Time Usage Model (2020) for its time categories and KPIs [F]; the delay codes and the reference-mine examples are the handbook's own [H] (delay-codes.yml, ahs_sim.kpi, ch-01-05). Scheduled time is split into downtime and available time, and available time into standby (available but not operating) and operating time. Standby is either operating standby, by management decision (shift change, operator breaks), or external standby, outside management's control (site-wide weather). Operating time is operating delay (operating, but stopped or prevented from working: fuelling, waiting while the loading unit moves or is down) plus working time. Working time is non-productive time (travel empty, queues at a working loading unit or crusher) plus productive time (spot, load, haul loaded, dump) [F]. An operator break (crib break, DLY-SO-02) exists only for manned equipment. A hot-seat change hands a machine to the next operator without stopping it. Use of availability is operating time over available time, operating efficiency working time over operating time, production effectiveness productive time over operating time, and effective utilisation working time over scheduled time [F]. In this chapter in-cycle time is the time a truck spends cycling over a shift: productive time plus travel empty, which is the time not covered by any delay event [H].

Nuisance stop. A stop triggered by a detection that, on review, was not a genuine hazard.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 What a driver actually does

Ask drivers what the job is and they say "drive the truck". Write down what they do over a shift and the list is longer, and much of it is in no procedure. FIG-01-02-01 lists fourteen such tasks and shows where each goes in the autonomous operation of the reference mine [H].

Three patterns run through the table. Sensing and acting move to ADS, VCS and the safety functions. Coordination moves to AMS and becomes explicit: a reservation, a permission, a rule. Judgement and response stay with people who are no longer in the truck — in CR1, at an access point, in the workshop — and who reach the truck only through the system.

4.2 From judgement to explicit rules

A driver applies dozens of unwritten rules every shift and adapts each to circumstances. The autonomous system cannot apply a rule it has not been given, and it applies the rules it has as written. Every rule the site still needs must be written down, given an owner and put where the system can read it [H]:

Driver's judgement (manned) Explicit system rule (autonomous) Where it lives Owner
"Loaded trucks go first at X" Intersection reservation at X with a priority rule (loaded before empty [A]) AMS traffic configuration Engineering, through change management
"R05 is wet — take it slow" Temporary speed restriction on R05, both directions AMS road state, published to FMS (REQ-01-01-003) Controller applies; Pit Supervisor accountable
"The windrow on the lower ramp is low — keep inside" Map attribute or lane restriction AHS map (Volume 12) Survey
"The dozer is at EX02 — wait until the operator waves me in" Work zone at EX02; trucks held at EX02-Q (SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02) AMS work zones Shovel Operator requests; controller grants
"The crusher light is red — hold" Tipping-bay reservation withheld while C01 is not ready AMS, from the plant signal Plant and controller
"GPS is playing up, but I can see the road" Localisation integrity check; derate or stop ADS Engineering (Volume 05)

The rule is consistent: applied the same way at 03:00 as at 13:00, by every truck, testable and auditable. It is also rigid: it does what it says even when a driver would have seen that the situation needed something else. A driver who noticed a low windrow simply kept further from the edge, and nobody else ever knew. An autonomous truck keeps to its mapped path until someone updates the map — so the low windrow must now be noticed, reported and turned into a map change by named people. The hazard did not change; the organisation that handles it did.

4.3 A stopped truck is not finished

A stopped manned truck has its driver with it: the driver knows why it stopped, will not drive off while someone is underneath, and tells everyone else. When an autonomous truck stops, its safety is not yet established: that takes its stop position being held, the traffic around it being controlled and its state being known. Even then it is not "safe and finished": until it is recovered, the system must hold, on the absent driver's behalf, the information the driver used to keep and pass on. For DT17 on R03 [H]:

State Held by Why it must persist
Stopped, with position and heading ADS reports; AMS holds Other trucks are planned around it
Stop reason (drivetrain fault code) VCS reports; AMS and FMS record Triage, delay coding, decision to send someone
Road space occupied (its lane on R03) AMS reservation No path may be granted through a stationary truck
No path granted to DT17 AMS DT17 must not move until an authorised role releases it from recovery (recovery state released)
Withdrawn from the EX02 assignment FMS FMS must not count on DT17's loads
Field Technician's permission (route, work area) AMS, field terminal, PRU Keeps autonomous traffic away from the person
DT17 isolated while a person is at it Truck and AMS state DT17 must not move even if a path were granted by mistake
Delay record (DLY-DN-01) FMS The shift report must show where the time went

Two principles govern the list (master-instructions §3). Permissions fail closed: if AMS loses DT17's state, after a restart for example, DT17 is treated as stopped and without a path until the state is re-established (SCN-AMS-RESTART, ch-02-08). And central permissions never override vehicle-side safety decisions: AMS can withhold a path, but it cannot release a stop the truck itself has decided on.

Persistence also has an end, and the order of ending matters: fault cleared, person clear of the truck, isolation removed, the person's permission released, DT17's state reset, and only then a new path (section 6.2). Granting DT17 a path while the technician's permission is still active is the most direct way to put a person and a moving truck in the same place.

4.4 Manned vehicles in an autonomous area

In the manned pit LV07's safety rested on three things: drivers could see it, hear it on the radio and negotiate with it ("LV07, I'm behind you, pull in at the bay"). An autonomous truck does none of these in the same way. It does not hear voice radio and it cannot negotiate. Its perception detects objects in its path and stops, which protects against surprises but does not organise traffic: a truck that stops whenever it meets LV07 is unproductive, and a sign that separation was left to chance [H].

The autonomous operation therefore separates manned vehicles from trucks by plan [H]. The PRU tells the system where LV07 is. The digital access permission tells AMS which area LV07 may occupy, so that AMS clears trucks out of it before LV07 enters. Positive communication makes the driver, the controller and the system agree on the same area at the same time. The devices also check each other: the permission says where LV07 should be, the PRU where it is. If the PRU stops reporting inside AA-North, the system can no longer confirm LV07 is in its area and the procedure restores awareness by radio (SCN-PRU-FAIL-LV07). A permission that is lost or late is not granted (SCN-POSCOM-STALE).

The price is time. In SCN-POSCOM-LV07 the permitted area covers R05 and R06 [A], so from T+25 s, when AMS locks it, to T+1320 s, when release is confirmed, the main ramp is closed to autonomous trucks: 1295 s, or 21.6 minutes. Trucks from EX01 and EX02 take the west ramp R24; the M-20481 cycle grows from 1564.1 s to 1800.1 s (+236 s) and the loaded haul from 4.50 km to 5.87 km (waste trucks from EX03 and EX04 are barely affected). In the manned pit the same inspection cost no truck time — and relied entirely on everyone hearing one radio call.

4.5 What the driver used to absorb

A driver silently absorbs a class of disturbances — poor satellite positioning near the highwall, a patchy radio on the lower ramp, dust behind the water truck — by driving on sight. In an autonomous operation each becomes a system condition with a defined reaction [H]:

Condition at the reference mine Driver (manned) Autonomous truck Delay code
Localisation integrity degraded on R05 (SCN-GNSS-R05) Not noticed ADS derates or stops DLY-OD-06
Wireless link lost on lower R05 or the RL 330 bench (SCN-COMMS-R05) Calls in later Stops within its granted path; AMS freezes reservations DLY-OD-05
Dust or spray behind WT02 on R03 (SCN-WATER-WT02) Slows, drives through May stop; controller reviews, authorises resume DLY-OD-03
Light vehicle entering the pit (SCN-POSCOM-LV07) Gives way on sight Held or rerouted while the area is permitted DLY-OD-04

These conditions are not spread evenly. mine.yaml records wireless coverage and GNSS multipath risk for every segment, and LAB-01-02-01 lists the exposed ones: marginal coverage on R04, R05, R09, R10, R11, R19, R20 and R24, poor coverage on the D01 tip link R22, high multipath risk on R05 and medium risk on R07, R08, R17, R18 and R24. R05 is the only segment with both marginal coverage and high multipath risk — and it is the main ramp, the segment that dominates the cycle (ch-01-01). Each stop is short; together they form a delay category the manned fleet did not have, and section 4.6 shows that it decides whether autonomy gains any hauling time at all.

4.6 Time usage: manned versus autonomous

In the GMG time usage model [F] a truck's travel empty is non-productive time, so productive time is not the time the truck spends cycling. What counts for loads is the in-cycle time [H], productive time plus travel empty, which for one truck in one shift is

Thc=Tsched−Tdown−TSB−TOD−TQ

with standby TSB, operating delay TOD and queues TQ (non-productive time spent waiting at a working shovel or crusher). Complete loads are n=⌊Thc/tcycle⌋, with tcycle = 1564.1 s for M-20481 (ch-01-01), of which travel empty tempty takes 503.1 s; productive time is Tprod=Thc(1−tempty/tcycle) [H]. With operating time Top=Tsched−Tdown−TSB, use of availability U, operating efficiency E and production effectiveness P are [F]

U=TopTsched−Tdown,E=Top−TODTop,P=TprodTop

LAB-01-02-01 builds an event log for one truck on M-20481 over the 12 h DAY shift and classifies it with ahs_sim.kpi [A]. The manned truck stops for a driver changeover at each end of the shift (2 × 15 min, DLY-SO-01) and two crib breaks (2 × 30 min, DLY-SO-02), all operating standby. The autonomous truck has neither, but EX02 is still manned: its operator changeover leaves the truck waiting 10 min for a loading unit that is not loading (DLY-OD-11, an operating delay), and with unchanged shovel practice the shovel also stops for two crib breaks, which the truck waits through in the same way (2 × 30 min, DLY-OD-11; treating the shovel operator's break like a shovel that is down is the handbook's extension [H]). In the hot-seat case a relief operator keeps EX02 loading through them. Every case stops 15 min for fuelling (DLY-OD-09, an operating delay) and queues for 20 min at the working shovel and crusher (DLY-NP-01, DLY-NP-02, non-productive time). The autonomous cases add 30 min of autonomy-specific stops, all operating delay: four 3-min nuisance stops, two 5-min holds for access permissions, one 5-min stop on link loss and one 3-min stop on localisation degradation.

FIG-01-02-02 shows the three shifts on a clock, with a fourth row for the hot-seated case on the afternoon of SCN-RECOVER-DT17. The light background of each row is in-cycle time; the figure at the right of each row gives the hours in the cycle and the complete loads.

Four results matter.

  1. Removing the driver removes all of the truck's operating standby, but not the shovel's breaks. The autonomous truck has no shift change and no crib breaks, so its operating standby falls from 90 min to zero. With unchanged shovel practice, however, the shovel's changeover and crib breaks come back as the truck's operating delay: it waits 70 min at EX02 for a loading unit that is not loading (DLY-OD-11). The net saving is only 20 min.
  2. Autonomy adds a delay category. The 30 min of autonomy-specific stops more than cancel that saving: the autonomous truck has 10 min less in-cycle time than the manned one (9.75 h against 9.92 h). The complete-load count hides this — both show 22 — so compare cycles, not complete loads, when differences are small.
  3. The gain comes from the operating model, not the truck. Hot-seating the four shovels removes another 60 min of operating delay: 10.75 h of in-cycle time, 50 min and two loads more than the manned truck. In the manned fleet the same effect would have needed relief drivers for 40 trucks; here it needs relief operators for four shovels.
  4. KPIs move in different directions. Use of availability rises from 87.5 % to 100 %, because the autonomous truck has no operating standby, while operating efficiency falls (97.6 % to 84.0 % or 92.4 %), because what the truck still loses it now loses as operating delay. GMG notes that in an autonomous operation operating delay is "within the control of the operating system" [F], and it is there that the autonomous truck's losses collect. Production effectiveness follows operating efficiency, lowered by the travel-empty share of every cycle. Either of the first two alone misleads; effective utilisation (working time over scheduled time: 85.4 % against 84.0 % or 92.4 %) and in-cycle time tell the story.

The break-even follows from the first equation. With equal downtime and equal queues, the autonomous truck is ahead only while its autonomy-specific delay stays below the standby and other operating delay it saves:

TOD,aut*=(TSB+TOD)manned−(TSB+TOD)otheraut

where the second term counts the autonomous truck's standby and operating delay other than the autonomy-specific stops. Here TOD,aut* is 20 min per truck per shift with unchanged shovel breaks and 80 min with hot-seated shovels. Every nuisance stop, permission hold and link-loss stop is paid from that margin, which is why access-area design (section 7.1), nuisance-stop rates (Volume 06) and coverage on R05 (Volume 11) are productivity topics as well as engineering ones.

4.7 Trade-offs and model limits

  • One truck, no interaction. The model ignores bunching behind a stopped shovel, trucks delayed behind a held truck, and FMS reassignment during shovel breaks.
  • Fixed events. Breaks and delays have fixed times and lengths; ahs_sim.kpi.synthetic_shift_log generates seeded variable logs (ch-01-05).
  • No downtime. Both fleets are assumed to have no downtime (physical availability 100 %); autonomy also changes maintenance and physical availability (Volume 18), which this chapter does not quantify.
  • Travel empty as a fixed share. The lab does not record each trip; it splits in-cycle time into productive time and travel empty by the travel-empty share of the M-20481 cycle [H].
  • Manned practice is one choice. A manned operation can hot-seat its trucks; an autonomous one can still stop for shovel breaks. The model shows the mechanism, not the result at any site.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

The shared timeline for sections 6, 7 and 8 is SCN-RECOVER-DT17 (section 6.2). Section 6.1 first shows the most common new routine of an autonomous shift, the entry of a manned vehicle.

6.1 Normal operation — SCN-POSCOM-LV07

At 09:00 the Pit Supervisor asks LV07's driver to inspect a damaged windrow on R06. In the manned pit the driver would have called once and driven down R05. Now the entry follows positive communication [H], with the registered times [A]:

T+20 s Area selected on the HMI; permission request submitted AMS AHS Controller
T+25 s Trucks in the area stopped clear or rerouted via R24; area locked AMS; ADS; FMS re-plans Controller watches trucks leave
T+90 s Area confirmed clear; permission granted, pending acknowledgement AMS —
T+95 s Permission given by radio; read back; acknowledged on the field terminal AMS, field terminal Controller and driver

The protocol costs 100 s before LV07 moves; the area costs far more. A permission over R05 and R06 closes the main route out of the pit for 21.6 minutes (section 4.4). Closing R06 alone would do the same, because R06 is the only link between the pit floor and the foot of R05. Only an area smaller than a segment (the damaged section of R06 and its lane, with trucks passing slowly in the other lane), or an inspection timed to a planned closure, keeps the route open — worth 236 s on every ore cycle that would otherwise take the west ramp.

R03 is the only route between X and D01 (ch-01-03): at the reference mine [A] trucks may pass a stopped truck in the opposite lane at reduced speed while nobody is at it, but the technician's work area covers both lanes from T+747 s to T+2457 s, so for those 28.5 minutes waste haulage to D01 is held (DLY-OD-04 on each truck affected).

6.3 Emergency — SCN-FIRE-DT17 (introduced; detail in ch-04-07)

On another day DT17 reports fire detection while climbing R05 loaded. In the manned mine the driver was the first detector, the first responder and the first person at risk. Now nobody in the cab is at risk — and nobody is there to act early. The registered key events [A]:

Time Event System actor People
T+0 s Fire detection alarm on DT17 Onboard detection and suppression —
T+5 s Controlled stop begins; stop location evaluated ADS, VCS —
T+10 s Exclusion zone around DT17; R05 traffic held AMS —
T+15 s Alarm acknowledged; ERT and Pit Supervisor called — AHS Controller

Detection and suppression are automatic because every autonomous truck at the reference mine is fitted with them (master-instructions §4). The first person arrives at T+600 s at the earliest, and only into an area the controller has opened. Stop location, zone size and ERT approach are developed in ch-04-07 and ch-16-06.

6.4 Degraded operation — conditions the driver used to absorb

The conditions of section 4.5 — integrity degradation on lower R05 (SCN-GNSS-R05), a link loss when trailer CT3 fails (SCN-COMMS-R05), nuisance stops behind WT02 (SCN-WATER-WT02) — do not stop the mine. Each stops trucks for minutes, is coded as an operating delay and needs a controller decision: restrict the ramp and call Survey, wait for CT3 and reconcile, or review the snapshot and authorise a resume. In the lab they total 30 minutes per truck per shift [A], enough by section 4.6 to decide whether the autonomous shift beats the manned one.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Stop, derate or continue. A driver chose continuously; an autonomous truck needs a default. The handbook default [H] is to stop on any condition the truck cannot resolve itself, because a stopped truck is a known state. The price is the operating delay of section 4.6, so every condition where a derate would suffice is worth engineering: it turns a stop into a slower cycle.

Where to stop. Stopping in lane is quick but blocks R03, the only route to D01; reaching a bay helps traffic but means moving on with a fault. Safety comes first (ch-04-07).

Size of an access area. A large area (all of R05 and R06) is simple and leaves no gap in the separation of LV07 from the trucks, but closes the main route for 21.6 minutes. A small area (one lane of the damaged section of R06) keeps the route open but needs a transit permission or escort down R05 and a rule for trucks passing in the other lane: more requests, more radio traffic, more chances of error. The break-even of section 4.6 is the budget against which this trade-off is made.

Which copy of the state is authoritative. DT17's stopped state exists in the truck, AMS and FMS. The design must say which is authoritative for each item — the truck for "stopped and isolated", AMS for "no path granted", FMS for "not assigned" — and how they are reconciled after a link loss or a server restart (ch-02-08).

Remote reset or field attendance. A remote reset saves most of the 2684 s of downtime; a wrong one puts a damaged truck back in traffic. Which faults may be reset from CR1, by whom and after which checks is a design decision with a safety argument.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

The SCN-RECOVER-DT17 timeline rests on a few messages and state changes, each with a producer, a consumer and a deadline:

  • T+0 s to T+5 s, fault and stop. VCS reports the fault; ADS reports "stopped" with position and reason. The five seconds are [A]; what matters is that the truck confirms the stopped state rather than AMS inferring it from a missing heartbeat.
  • T+5 s, FMS update. FMS must learn the same state from AMS, or it keeps counting on DT17's next load.
  • T+481 s to T+581 s, permission. Request, area lock, confirmation, grant, read-back, acknowledgement. Each step has a time-out; a step that times out leaves the permission not granted.
  • T+747 s to T+2457 s, presence. The PRU reports the technician's position throughout. A stale position is a reason to hold, never to release.
  • T+2457 s to T+2684 s, release order. Technician clear, isolation removed, permission released and confirmed, state reset, mission, path. AMS must enforce the order rather than rely on people: releasing DT17's path checks that no permission covering DT17 is active (REQ-01-02-002).

The driver did all of this by voice. Now each message needs an interface control document entry (ch-02-06) with states, time-outs and failure behaviour, because no driver fills the gaps.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

At T+5 s the AHS Controller sees an alarm: "DT17 stopped — drivetrain fault — R03, X-bound lane, 600 m from X", with the time since stop counting up. By handbook convention [H] the display shows the truck's recovery state (awaiting triage, attendance dispatched, attended and isolated, released, in service) and the permissions around it (REQ-01-02-006). At T+747 s it shows the technician's PRU at the stand-off point inside the work area; at T+897 s DT17 isolated; at T+2654 s the permission released. Trucks held on R03 appear as held, with the reason.

In the manned mine the controller heard the fleet; now the controller watches it, and what the HMI does not show the controller does not know. Recovery state and permissions therefore belong on the display, not in memory.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — attending a stopped autonomous truck (SCN-RECOVER-DT17)

  1. AHS Controller (T+5 s): acknowledge the alarm; confirm that DT17 is stopped, has no path and is withdrawn from its assignment; note the fault code.
  2. AHS Controller (by T+60 s): triage the fault data. If a remote reset is permitted for this fault, follow that procedure. Otherwise dispatch the Field Technician, inform the Pit Supervisor and code the stop (DLY-DN-01).
  3. Field Technician (T+481 s): at AP-01, request entry by radio and field terminal: who, route and work area, why, how long. Do not enter until the permission is read back and acknowledged.
  4. Field Technician (T+747 s): stop at the stand-off point. Confirm with the controller that DT17 is isolated and has no path; read back. Apply personal isolation before working on the truck.
  5. Field Technician (T+2397 s to T+2457 s): when the fault is cleared, remove personal isolation, return to the light vehicle and report "clear of DT17".
  6. Field Technician (T+2624 s): leave the area; release the permission on the field terminal.
  7. AHS Controller (T+2654 s): confirm the technician's PRU is outside; confirm the release; only then reset DT17 and let FMS assign it. Watch DT17 move off.
  8. Maintenance Planner (after the shift): review the fault; decide on workshop follow-up.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Autonomy does not reduce the competence an operation needs; it moves it.

  • AHS Controllers supervise forty trucks they cannot see: fluent positive communication, permission handling (grant, read back, release, re-issue), fault triage from data, recognising patterns across trucks (three nuisance stops at one place are a road problem), and coding every stop as it happens.
  • Field Technicians and light-vehicle drivers need the access protocol, the stand-off and isolation routine, and the habit of treating PRU, radio and field terminal as safety equipment. Without a permission they are, to the system, not there.
  • Shovel Operators become the main manned interface of every truck: they signal trucks away, request work zones for DZ03 and decide with the Pit Supervisor how their breaks are covered.
  • Pit Supervisors need to read time usage as in section 4.6; use of availability alone looks better than the shift was.
  • Former drivers hold the unwritten rules that table 4.2 needs; capturing them before the transition is part of the work (ch-17-06, ch-19-05).

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-01-02-001 [H] When an autonomous truck stops because of a fault, the AMS shall keep the truck's stopped state, position, stop reason and occupied road space, and shall not grant the truck a new path until an authorised role has released it from recovery. Rationale: a stopped truck is not finished; the system must remember on behalf of the absent driver (section 4.3). Verification: test (LAB-01-02-01 SCN-RECOVER-DT17 timeline; AMS restart during the stop); inspection.

REQ-01-02-002 [H] The AMS shall not grant a path to a truck, or a path through an area, while a digital access permission covering that truck or area is active. A permission shall be released only after the permitted person or vehicle is confirmed outside the area by position report and by radio read-back. Rationale: release order is where recovery can put a person and a moving truck in one place (sections 4.3, 7.3). Verification: test (fault injection: path request with an active permission); demonstration.

REQ-01-02-003 [H] The AMS shall grant a manned vehicle entry to an autonomous area only when the vehicle's position reporting unit is reporting, the requested area has been confirmed clear of autonomous traffic, and the grant has been read back and acknowledged on the field terminal. A grant not acknowledged within its time-out shall be treated as not granted. Rationale: manned vehicles and autonomous trucks are separated by plan, failing closed (section 4.4). Verification: test (SCN-POSCOM-LV07, SCN-POSCOM-STALE); inspection.

REQ-01-02-004 [H] Every traffic rule that drivers applied by judgement in the manned operation and that the site still requires shall be recorded in a rule register with its system representation (AMS rule, speed restriction or map attribute), its owner and its configuration version. Rationale: an unwritten rule disappears with the driver (section 4.2). Verification: inspection (rule register against table 4.2 and the former manned procedures).

REQ-01-02-005 [H] The FMS shall classify truck time with the handbook time usage model, recording autonomy-specific stops (DLY-OD-03 to DLY-OD-06) and waiting for a loading unit that is not loading (DLY-OD-11) under their own codes, so that manned and autonomous shifts can be compared on the same basis. Rationale: the gain from autonomy is the operating standby removed minus the operating delay added (section 4.6). Verification: test (LAB-01-02-01 experiments 1 and 2); inspection of the delay code set.

REQ-01-02-006 [H] The control-room display shall show, for each stopped autonomous truck, the stop reason, the time since the stop, the recovery state (awaiting triage, attendance dispatched, attended and isolated, released, in service) and the access permissions active around it. Rationale: the controller knows only what the display shows (section 8.1). Verification: demonstration with the SCN-RECOVER-DT17 timeline.

10. Verification and validation

Verification of this chapter's numbers:

  • time usage is computed by ahs_sim.kpi.classify and kpis, which have their own unit tests;
  • the lab tests check the reference values of section 4.6, the break-even values (20 and 80 min), that autonomy raises use of availability and lowers operating efficiency, that DLY-SO-02 never appears on an autonomous truck, and that every event log is valid;
  • the SCN-RECOVER-DT17 timeline is checked against the registered skeleton (dispatch at T+60 s, isolation by about T+900 s, fault cleared at about T+2400 s) and its travel times against the road network;
  • the LV07 reroute (+236 s for EX01 and EX02) is computed by compute_cycle with R05 and R06 closed.

On a real system some requirements need fault injection: REQ-01-02-002 must be tested by deliberately requesting a path while a permission is active, not by observing that it never happens.

Validation of the time usage comparison needs real shift data from a manned and an autonomous operation, coded with the same delay codes — which is what REQ-01-02-005 asks for. The reference mine has no such data, and this chapter makes no claim about the size of the gain at any real site.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Treating "stopped" as "safe and finished". The stop starts a recovery that needs persistent state, permissions and a release order.
  • Relying on perception to separate people from trucks. Separation is planned with PRUs and permissions.
  • Leaving rules implicit. Every rule the drivers carried in their heads needs a register entry and an owner.
  • Designing access areas without counting held trucks. An area over R05 costs 236 s on every ore cycle that must take the west ramp.
  • Letting the release order depend on people alone. The system must refuse a path through an active permission.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Expecting the drivers' break time to appear automatically. The shovels still stop.
  • Reading only use of availability. It rises to 100 % while operating efficiency falls; effective utilisation and in-cycle time tell the story.
  • Leaving autonomy-specific stops uncoded. Thirty minutes of uncoded stops turn a gain into a loss unseen.
  • Driving into an autonomous area "just for a minute". Without a permission the system does not know you are there.
  • Releasing a permission before the person is confirmed outside. It makes the rest of the procedure pointless.

12. Worked examples

All values [A], computed by LAB-01-02-01 with ahs_sim on mine version 0.3.0.

Example 1 — manned. In-cycle time 720 − (30 + 60) − 15 − 20 = 595 min = 9.92 h (operating standby, operating delay, queues); 595 × 60 / 1564.1 = 22.8 cycles, 22 complete loads. Productive time 9.92 × (1 − 503.1 / 1564.1) = 6.73 h. Use of availability 630 / 720 = 87.5 %; operating efficiency (630 − 15) / 630 = 97.6 %.

Example 2 — autonomous, shovel breaks unchanged. 720 − (10 + 60 + 15 + 30) − 20 = 585 min = 9.75 h; 22.4 cycles, 22 complete loads; 10 minutes less than manned. Use of availability 720 / 720 = 100 %; operating efficiency (720 − 115) / 720 = 84.0 %.

Example 3 — autonomous, shovels hot-seated. 720 − (10 + 15 + 30) − 20 = 645 min = 10.75 h; 24.7 cycles, 24 complete loads; 50 minutes and two loads more than manned. Operating efficiency (720 − 55) / 720 = 92.4 %.

Example 4 — break-even. Hot-seated: (90 + 15) − (10 + 15) = 80 min of autonomy-specific delay per truck per shift. Unchanged shovel breaks: (90 + 15) − (70 + 15) = 20 min.

Example 5 — first eyes on DT17. Dispatch at T+60 s, preparation 300 s, travel to AP-01 121 s, permission 100 s, travel to the stand-off point 167 s: T+747 s. Travel alone is 287 s for 2.80 km at site speed limits.

Example 6 — cost of the recovery. 2684 s = 44.7 min down, or 2684 / 1564.1 = 1.72 cycles of M-20481. In-cycle time falls from 10.75 h to 10.00 h, complete loads from 24 to 23, physical availability to 93.8 %.

Example 7 — cost of LV07's permission. R05 is locked for 1295 s; by the west ramp M-20481 takes 1800.1 s instead of 1564.1 s (+236 s per ore cycle).

15. Key takeaways

  • Autonomy does not remove the driver's tasks; it moves them — sensing and acting to ADS, VCS and safety functions, coordination to AMS as explicit rules, judgement and response to people outside the truck.
  • A stopped autonomous truck is not "safe and finished": its state, the permissions around it and a strict release order must persist until recovery is complete. In the walkthrough DT17 is down 44.7 minutes, 25 of them for repair.
  • Manned vehicles are separated from trucks by plan — PRU, digital access permission, positive communication — not by perception. The price is held or rerouted trucks: 236 s per ore cycle while R05 is locked.
  • Autonomy adds a delay category that drivers used to absorb, concentrated where coverage and positioning are weak, above all on R05.
  • The gain in in-cycle time is the operating standby removed minus the operating delay added. With unchanged shovel breaks it can be negative; with hot-seated shovels it is 50 minutes per truck per shift in the reference model, with a break-even of 80 minutes of autonomy-specific delay.
  • Read use of availability, operating efficiency and effective utilisation together; use of availability alone flatters autonomy.
