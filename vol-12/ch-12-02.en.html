1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

The planning meeting for the R05 rework is nearly over when the Pit Supervisor has an idea. "Survey has a drone. Fly R05 when the dozer is done instead of walking six strings down a 1.3 km ramp. Fifteen minutes in the air, nobody on the road, and we get the whole surface, not just lines." Survey likes the idea and does not like it. The AHS Controller asks whether the drone will be over trucks, and who holds a permission for an aircraft. The network team asks which radio band the drone's links use. The map team asks the question that decides it: "Will a drone surface show us a windrow that is 0.40 m low over 60 m — and will we have it before you want to reopen?"

The same meeting has a second item: EX02 moves to a new face, and the new loading area must be in the map before the AMS activates it. Here a drone looks even better — the face is a jumble of material a ground crew cannot safely walk.

The questions this meeting raises are the subject of the chapter:

  • How does a set of overlapping images become a surface in REFMINE-GRID, and what do ground control points contribute?
  • How do the camera, the flight height and the overlaps set the ground sampling distance, the flight time and the coverage — over a pit 120 m deep?
  • Can a drone survey resolve R05's windrow well enough for a map change, and can it be delivered in time?
  • What does flying over an autonomous area mean for the AHS: radio, masts, dust, and the permission discipline?
  • How often should the EX02 face and the dump D01 be flown?

This chapter covers:

  • the structure-from-motion workflow from images to surface and the role of ground control points and check points (FIG-12-02-01);
  • flight planning — ground sampling distance, overlap, coverage, flight time — and the effect of the pit's depth, computed in LAB-12-02-01 (FIG-12-02-02);
  • whether a drone survey can replace a ground survey for R05's windrow in SCN-MAP-R05, on accuracy and on timing;
  • drones and the AHS: radio spectrum, masts, dust and lighting, lost links, and the drone flight permission;
  • flight frequency over EX02 and D01 from production rates, and the quality report that accompanies every photogrammetric product.

The control chain, the local grid REFMINE-GRID, error propagation and the survey hand-off package are ch-12-01; this chapter adds what is specific to images. LiDAR is ch-12-03, terrain models and volumes ch-12-04. Every camera, height, overlap, accuracy and time in this chapter is an illustrative assumption [A] and not a recommendation. Drone (remotely piloted aircraft) regulation in Australia is a topic only [TBV]; no requirement is stated.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Explain the structure-from-motion photogrammetry workflow and the role of ground control points in accuracy.
  2. Calculate ground sampling distance, image overlap and flight time for a survey of AA-North [A].
  3. Assess interactions between drone operations and the AHS (radio spectrum, airspace near comms sites, dust and lighting) as engineering constraints.
  4. Evaluate whether drone photogrammetry can resolve windrow geometry on R05 well enough for a map change, compared with ground survey [A].
  5. Specify the metadata and quality report that must accompany a photogrammetric product delivered to the map team.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Plan a drone survey flight over an active autonomous area: timing, permissions, communication with the AHS Controller and stand-off from operating equipment.
  2. Decide when drone data is sufficient for a map change and when a ground survey is required.
  3. Coordinate a flight with the network team and the AHS Controller so that it does not interfere with radio communication or field crews.
  4. Set the flight frequency over the EX02 face and D01 according to how fast production changes the surface.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Ground survey measures points and lines where a person or a vehicle can stand. That is precise and slow, and it puts a person on the road. A drone with a camera captures every square metre of a block from the air in minutes, with nobody on the ground below. For a mine whose surface changes every shift, the attraction is obvious. So are the questions: how good is the surface, how long until it exists as data, and what does an aircraft over an autonomous area mean for the trucks?

The reference mine makes the questions concrete. AA-North is a pit 120 m deep: the crest and the surface are at RL 420, the bench at RL 330, the floor at RL 300 (mine.yaml). R05 descends 120 m over 1334.2 m of plan length from P1 to P2. The AHS map's AA-North zone contains 12 segments with 13 end nodes; their bounding box with a 100 m buffer [A] is a block 2400 m by 1700 m. CT2's crest tower stands next to P1, 54 m from it (ch-01-03); the trailer CT3 stands on the pit floor. The pit walls cast radio shadows (ch-11-04) and, at low sun, shadows on the floor.

Two registered events need survey of the kind a drone might give:

  • SCN-MAP-R05. The registry has the as-built submitted at T+0 s, the map change validated at T+1800 s and the road reopened at T+2400 s, with R05 closed from T−3600 s. The windrow and edge rework must be in the map before trucks return.
  • SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE. EX02 is set to relocating at T+0 s and its new loading area is activated by the AMS at T+3600 s. The new floor and face toe must be in the map by then. SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02 prepares such floors.

LAB-12-02-01 answers the planning questions with a calculator built on ahs_sim.mapping (the AA-North zone of the AHS map, deviation statistics along R05) and ahs_sim.fms (production rates). Its answers are less flattering to the drone than the Pit Supervisor's idea, and more useful.

3. Terminology and foundations

Drone (remotely piloted aircraft). An uncrewed aircraft flown by a remote pilot or under supervised automation. The handbook uses "drone" for the multirotor survey aircraft of this chapter (glossary drone).

Photogrammetry and structure from motion. Photogrammetry derives 3D geometry from overlapping images (glossary photogrammetry). Structure from motion (SfM) is the workflow that matches features across many overlapping images, solves for the camera positions and orientations and the 3D positions of the matched points together, and then builds a dense surface.

Tie point and bundle adjustment. A tie point is a feature recognised in several images. Bundle adjustment is the least-squares solution that adjusts camera positions, orientations and calibration and the tie points' 3D positions so that every ray through every image agrees.

Ground control point (GCP) and check point. A GCP is a target on the ground whose REFMINE-GRID coordinates Survey has measured from the control register (ch-12-01); it ties the photogrammetric block to the grid. A check point (ch-12-01) is measured the same way but kept out of the solution and used to test it.

Ground sampling distance (GSD). The distance on the ground between the centres of two adjacent pixels of a nadir image: GSD=Hp/f for height above ground H, pixel pitch p and focal length f. GSD sets what can be seen; it is not the accuracy of the surface.

Footprint, forward overlap and side overlap. The footprint is the ground area of one image. Forward overlap is the share of a footprint that the next image along the line repeats; side overlap is the share that the next line repeats. Together they decide how many images see each point.

Constant-altitude and terrain-following flight. A constant-altitude flight keeps one altitude above the take-off point; over a pit its height above ground grows as the ground falls away. A terrain-following flight keeps a constant height above the ground below and needs a terrain model to do so.

Dense point cloud, surface model and orthomosaic. The dense cloud is the set of 3D points produced after bundle adjustment (glossary point-cloud); a surface model or DTM is built from it (ch-12-04); an orthomosaic is a single image of the block corrected for relief and camera tilt, used for outlines and inspection.

Quality report. The record that accompanies a photogrammetric product: what was flown, how it was solved, how it was checked (section 4.11) [H].

Drone flight permission. The handbook's digital access permission for a drone flight over an autonomous area (section 4.9) [H].

4. Technical explanation

4.1 From images to surface

FIG-12-02-01 shows the workflow.

A flight plan fixes the block, the height and the overlaps. After the permission is granted the aircraft flies the lines and captures images with their times and approximate camera positions. A first image check — coverage, motion blur, dust, shadow — decides whether the flight is usable or must be repeated. Software then finds tie points across overlapping images and runs the bundle adjustment, solving camera positions, orientations and calibration together. Ground control points tie the solution to REFMINE-GRID; independent check points, which the solution never saw, test it. Only then is the dense point cloud built, filtered (dust plumes, moving machines, noise) and turned into a surface model and an orthomosaic. A quality report goes with the product to the map team, which decides whether it is sufficient for the map change in hand or whether ground survey is needed.

Three points in the figure carry most of the risk. The image check is where dust and shadow are caught before hours of processing are spent on them. The check-point gate is the only place where the product's accuracy is measured rather than assumed. And the sufficiency decision is where the map team, not the pilot, decides what the product can be used for (section 4.7).

4.2 Ground control and check points

A photogrammetric block without ground control floats: bundle adjustment fixes the shape up to a scale, a rotation and a shift, and even the shape can bend over a long, narrow block [TBV] (SRC-PUB-PHOTOGRAMMETRY-ACCURACY). GCPs remove that freedom. Camera positions from the aircraft's own GNSS can take over part of the role ("direct georeferencing") [TBV]; the handbook treats them as an input to the solution, not as a substitute for check points [H].

The geometry rule of ch-12-01 carries over unchanged. Control must surround the block, in plan and in height, because errors grow with distance from it (ch-12-01 section 4.4). For R05 that means GCPs at the crest, at the toe and between, spanning the 120 m of height; GCPs only at the crest would leave the lower ramp extrapolated. For the AA-North block it means GCPs on the rim and on the bench and the floor. GCPs and check points are measured from the control register, in REFMINE-GRID, with the register version recorded — otherwise the drone product lands in a different frame from the ground survey it is compared with (ch-12-01 section 4.1).

Check points are the evidence. They must be independent of the solution, spread over the block including its lowest and highest parts, and numerous enough to give a meaningful figure [A]. A product whose quality report has no independent check points has an assumed accuracy, not a measured one (REQ-12-02-002).

4.3 Camera geometry: GSD, footprint and overlap

For a nadir image the ground sampling distance and the footprint follow from similar triangles:

GSD=Hpf,W=NacrossGSD,L=NalongGSD

where N are the pixel counts across and along the flight line. Exposures along a line are spaced L(1−of) apart and lines W(1−os) apart for forward overlap of and side overlap os. The interval between exposures, L(1−of)/v at speed v, must not be shorter than the camera's minimum interval; if it is, the aircraft must fly slower.

LAB-12-02-01 uses two generic cameras [A] — numbers chosen for teaching, not taken from any product:

Camera A Camera B
Pixel pitch 2.4 µm 4.0 µm
Image (across × along) 5400 × 3600 px 8000 × 5300 px
Focal length 8.8 mm 35 mm
GSD at 100 m 2.73 cm 1.14 cm
Footprint at 100 m (across × along) 147.3 m × 98.2 m —

GSD grows linearly with height: camera A gives 2.18 cm at 80 m, 4.64 cm at 170 m, 5.45 cm at 200 m and 6.00 cm at 220 m. The flight parameters are also assumptions [A]: 10 m/s, forward overlap 0.80, side overlap 0.70, 15 s per line change, 20 min of usable flight per battery after the reserve, a minimum interval of 2.0 s between exposures and an exposure of 1/1000 s.

4.4 Flying over a pit

A survey block over AA-North spans 120 m of relief. Flown at a constant altitude above the take-off point at the crest P1 (AP-02), the aircraft is 120 m higher above the pit floor than above the crest. FIG-12-02-02 shows what that does.

With camera A at 100 m above the crest [A], the height above ground is 100 m at the crest and 220 m at the floor; the GSD is 2.73 cm and 6.00 cm. If line and exposure spacing are set for the crest, the overlaps on the floor rise to 0.864 side and 0.909 forward — more than needed. If instead the spacing is set for the floor, the overlaps at the crest fall to 0.34 side and 0.56 forward, and the solution will be weak or broken along the rim [A]. At 80 m above the crest the pattern is the same: 2.18 cm at the crest, 4.64 cm on the bench (170 m), 5.45 cm on the floor (200 m); planned for the floor, the crest overlaps drop to 0.25 and 0.50.

Two rules follow [H]. Plan overlap at the highest ground and GSD at the lowest: spacing is set where footprints are smallest, the GSD that matters for a requirement is the worst one. And if the lowest ground needs a finer GSD than a constant altitude gives, fly terrain-following or in steps — at the price of flying lower into the pit, closer to walls, masts and equipment, and further below the pilot's line of sight (section 4.8).

R05 itself is the clearest case. Along the ramp the road falls 120 m. A constant-altitude flight 80 m above P1 has a GSD of 2.18 cm at the crest and 5.45 cm at the toe, rising linearly (FIG-12-02-02, panel b); a flight that keeps 80 m above the road has 2.18 cm everywhere.

4.5 Flight time and coverage

plan_block turns a block and a height into lines, images, distance, speed, time and batteries. The block of AA-North comes from the AHS map (build_map): the end nodes of the 12 segments in the AA-North zone, RL 300 to 420, plus the buffer.

Block and plan Lines Images Distance Speed Flight time Batteries Motion blur
AA-North, camera A, 100 m 56 4928 97.63 km 9.82 m/s 179.5 min 9 0.36 px
AA-North, camera B, 100 m 89 12 638 153.7 km 6.06 m/s 445.0 min 23 0.53 px
R05 corridor 120 m × 1334.2 m, camera A, 80 m 5 430 6.81 km 7.85 m/s 15.5 min 1 0.36 px
EX02 block 300 m × 200 m, camera A, 80 m 10 140 2.32 km 7.85 m/s 7.2 min 1 0.36 px
D01 block 400 m × 300 m, camera A, 80 m 13 273 4.32 km 7.85 m/s 12.2 min 1 0.36 px

Three readings matter.

  1. The camera, not the aircraft, sets the speed. In every plan the exposure spacing divided by 10 m/s would be shorter than the 2.0 s minimum interval, so the calculator slows the aircraft: to 9.82 m/s for camera A at 100 m, to 7.85 m/s at 80 m, to 6.06 m/s for camera B. A plan that ignores the interval either loses overlap or images.
  2. Finer GSD is paid for in hours. Camera B's 1.14 cm GSD costs 445.0 min and 23 batteries for AA-North, against 179.5 min and 9 for camera A. Neither is a single sortie; the whole pit is a day's flying [A].
  3. Fly what changes. R05, the EX02 face and D01 are each one battery and under 16 minutes. The sensible plan is a small block over each surface that changes, at the frequency it changes (section 4.10), and a whole-pit flight only when the purpose needs it.

4.6 Can a drone resolve the R05 windrow?

The map change of SCN-MAP-R05 rests on the windrow and edge geometry. LAB-12-02-01 asks whether a drone surface would show a defect that matters. It sets up a teaching case [A]: the rework leaves the windrow 0.40 m below its design height between 700 m and 760 m from P1; heights are compared with the design at 267 stations every 5 m; a station is flagged when the measured deviation exceeds 0.20 m. These are teaching values, not windrow dimensions or tolerances — design guidelines for windrows are topics only [TBV] (ch-12-05).

The vertical error of a photogrammetric surface is modelled as σz=kzGSD with kz=2 — a lab assumption, not a published relation [A] [TBV]. The ground survey has σz=0.05 m on R05 (high multipath risk; the value LAB-12-01-01 uses). Three methods are compared with the same seeded noise:

Method Largest σ_z Low section 700–760 m found False flagged spans Pixels across a 1 m crest (fewest)
Ground survey 0.05 m yes 0 —
Drone, 80 m above the road 0.0436 m yes 0 45.8
Drone, constant altitude 80 m above P1 0.1089 m yes 5 18.4

The drone that keeps its height above the road performs as well as the ground survey in this model; the constant-altitude drone finds the defect but adds five false single-station spans on the lower ramp, where its GSD reaches 5.45 cm. Resolution is not the limit — even the worst case has 18.4 pixels across a 1 m crest. Height noise is.

What the model cannot show matters as much [A]:

  • The accuracy model is assumed. Whether σz is 2 GSD or something else on this ramp is known only from independent check points along the whole ramp, including the toe.
  • A surface is not a string. The map needs windrow toe and crest lines (ch-12-01 section 4.7). Extracting them from a surface is an editing step with its own errors.
  • Dust and shadow. On a closed R05 there is no traffic dust, but low sun leaves the toe of the eastern wall in shadow.
  • Systematic error. A long, narrow corridor block with weak control can bend [TBV]; random-noise models do not show it, check points do.

The engineering answer is therefore conditional: a terrain-following drone survey with ground control spanning the ramp's height and independent check points can resolve the windrow defect in this model; a constant-altitude survey cannot be trusted on lower R05. Whether it can be used for the map change also depends on time.

4.7 Timing: when does the drone product exist?

Images are not a surface. LAB-12-02-01 adds the times from mobilisation to delivery [A]: 20 min to mobilise and obtain the permission, 15 min to set up and check GCPs and the aircraft, the flight (15.5 min for R05), 120 min of processing and 30 min of quality assurance and report. In total 200.5 min.

In SCN-MAP-R05, if the drone crew starts when DZ03 leaves R05 at T−1200 s [A] (ch-12-01's timeline), the product is ready at T+10 827 s — three hours after the as-built is due at T+0 s, after validation at T+1800 s and after the planned reopening at T+2400 s. Turned the other way: to deliver by T+0 s, the flight would have to end by T−9000 s — before the closure at T−3600 s, before the rework exists. Within the registered timeline the drone cannot be the as-built for reopening R05; the ground survey of ch-12-01 is.

The same calculation for SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE: to deliver by the AMS activation at T+3600 s, the flight must end by T−5400 s — ninety minutes before EX02 starts to relocate. The drone captures the new loading area before the move: the prepared floor (SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02), processed in time for the map change to be validated and deployed before T+3600 s. A capture after the move starts is too late for the activation, though not for the next update.

This is not an argument against drones. It assigns them the work they are good at [R]:

  • Ahead of a change: the detour R24, P3, R25 and V days before an R05 closure (ch-12-01 section 4.9), the prepared floor of a new loading area.
  • After a change, off the critical path: a full-surface record of reworked R05 for the terrain model (ch-12-04) and for comparison with the ground strings and the deployed map; a difference beyond the site's value [A] is raised as a map discrepancy (ch-12-09).
  • Surfaces that change continuously: the EX02 face and D01 at a set frequency (section 4.10).

A site whose processing is much faster changes these numbers; the lab makes every time a parameter. The rule does not change: compute the latest flight end from the deadline before deciding that a drone will deliver.

4.8 Drones and the AHS: engineering constraints

A drone over an autonomous area meets the AHS in five places. Each is an engineering constraint to be assessed for the site; magnitudes are [TBV] (SRC-PUB-DRONE-MINE-OPERATIONS).

  • Radio spectrum. The drone's control and video links use radio bands that may be shared with the site network or with other site radio (ch-11-02). ch-11-04's estimate used 2400 MHz as an illustration [A]; whatever the site's bands, a drone link in the same band near a comms site can raise the noise the AHS link sees. The network team checks the drone's bands against the site's spectrum plan before the first flight; which arrangements apply is itself a regulatory topic [TBV] (SRC-REG-AU-SPECTRUM-LICENSING).
  • Masts and airspace near comms sites. CT2's tower stands 54 m from P1, the obvious launch point for the pit (AP-02); CT3 has a mast on the pit floor. Flight lines and climb-outs keep a stand-off from masts, guy wires and antennas [A], which the flight plan shows. Exposure near transmitting antennas is a topic [TBV].
  • The pilot's link into the pit. A pilot on the crest controls an aircraft that descends below the rim. The pit wall that casts radio shadows for the AHS network (ch-11-04) can cast one for the drone's link. The aircraft's behaviour on a lost link is a property of the product [TBV]; the plan states it and keeps the return path clear of haul roads.
  • Dust and lighting. Haul traffic raises dust that hides the surface and creates false points; low sun leaves pit walls and floors in shadow. Both degrade the product without stopping the flight. A closed road has no traffic dust; a flight near midday has the shortest shadows [A].
  • Objects on the road. A drone that lands, drops a part or falls on a haul road becomes an object on the road. A truck's perception may detect it and stop — a nuisance stop if it is small — or may not detect it (Volume 06) [TBV]. Either way, someone must retrieve it, which is a manned entry.

None of these is visible to the AMS on its own: a drone has no position-reporting unit (ch-04-08) and no granted path. The permission makes it visible.

4.9 The drone flight permission

Drones over autonomous areas follow the same permission discipline as manned entry (ch-10-06, ch-16-04). The handbook defines a drone flight permission [H]:

Field Content
Requester and pilot Named remote pilot, radio call sign, Survey contact
Pilot position At an access point outside the boundary, or inside under the pilot's own manned-entry permission
Flight area Polygon on the AHS map, with the altitude band above ground [A]
Validity window Start and end time; extension by request only
Launch, landing and lost-link return points Off haul roads; shown on the HMI
Stand-off From operating equipment, people, masts [A]
Traffic effect Overflight only, or with traffic exclusion (below)
Coordination Network team informed of bands; field crews in the area informed

It moves through the same states as any permission: requested, granted, acknowledged, active, released, release confirmed. It fails closed [H]: an expired or unacknowledged drone permission is not a permission, and the pilot lands at the agreed point; the AHS Controller can revoke it and the pilot returns at once.

Two classes cover the cases [H]:

  1. Overflight. The drone stays within its altitude band and keeps its stand-off; no traffic exclusion is set. Where possible the flight lines avoid active haul roads, or the flight is timed when the road is closed or quiet. Whether overflight of operating trucks is acceptable at all is a site risk-assessment and regulatory matter [TBV]; the handbook does not assume it is.
  2. Low flight, landing or recovery inside the area. Any planned flight below the band, any landing and any recovery of a downed aircraft is a manned-entry permission with an area lock (ch-16-04), because a person or an object will be on or near the road.

A drone that comes down inside an autonomous area is handled as an object on a haul road: the AHS Controller locks an area around it before anyone approaches, and the retrieval is a manned entry with its own permission. On the RL 330 bench this is costly: a retrieval on R10 via AP-04 locks R09, R10, R11 and R24 and holds EX03 and EX04 (ch-10-06).

4.10 Flight frequency over EX02 and D01

How often to fly a surface depends on how fast production changes it. LAB-12-02-01 takes the rates from the deterministic shift of ahs_sim.fms and adds the geometry [A]:

EX02 face D01
Material per 12 h shift 49 060 t of ore (223 loads) 88 000 t of waste
Volume 18 869 bcm (2.6 t/bcm) 47 667 lcm (2.4 t/bcm, swell 1.3 [A])
Geometry [A] Face 150 m wide, 15 m high Paddock layers 4 m thick
Change per shift Face advances 8.4 m 11 917 m² covered
Re-capture trigger [A] Face advanced 20 m beyond the mapped loading area 25 % of the 400 m × 300 m block changed
Interval every 2 shifts every 2 shifts
Flight 7.2 min, 140 images 12.2 min, 273 images

The trigger is the decision, not the arithmetic. A loading area in the map must contain the area where trucks spot; if the face advances 8.4 m per shift and the map's loading-area zone has a 20 m margin [A], the zone is out of date after the second shift. D01's paddock dumping changes tip points every load; the map's dump zone needs re-capture when a substantial share of it has changed [A]. Both rates come from one deterministic model; a site measures its own from surface differences (ch-12-04) and sets triggers with the Pit Supervisor [R].

4.11 Metadata and quality report

Every photogrammetric product travels in the survey hand-off package of ch-12-01 (coordinate system, epoch, method, control used, accuracy statement, feature list, conditions, map version compared, author and checker). The quality report adds the fields that are specific to images [H]:

Field Content Why the map team needs it
Flight record Block, date and time of each flight, aircraft, pilot, permission ID Epoch and traceability to the access record
Camera Camera ID, calibration used or solved, exposure Explains blur and lens effects
Heights and GSD Height above ground range, GSD range (best and worst) Shows where the product is coarse
Overlap achieved Forward and side overlap, lowest values and where Weak areas of the solution
Images Number captured, used, rejected (blur, dust, shadow) Coverage gaps
Control GCP IDs, coordinates and control-register version; residuals at GCPs Frame and fit
Check points IDs, differences, RMS horizontal and vertical — independent of the solution The measured accuracy

Exclusions Areas masked or unreliable (dust, moving machines, shadow, occlusion) What not to use
Product list Dense cloud, surface model, orthomosaic, extracted lines — with formats What was delivered

A product without independent check points is labelled "accuracy not measured" and may be used for planning and inspection, not for a map change (REQ-12-02-002).

4.12 Drone regulation: a topic, not a requirement

Flying a drone for commercial survey in Australia is regulated [F] (SRC-REG-AU-CASR-PART101, Civil Aviation Safety Regulations 1998, Part 101); what the regulation requires is [TBV] (SRC-REG-AU-RPA-OPERATIONS). The handbook states no requirement and no number. A site's drone procedure has to establish, from the regulator's own instruments and guidance, at least: who may operate and pilot; height limits and how they are referenced (to the take-off point or the ground below — over a 120 m pit the difference is the whole pit depth, section 4.4); operations near people and over moving vehicles; operations beyond visual line of sight, which a pilot on the crest may reach as the aircraft descends; and airspace near aerodromes and other aircraft. The site's own rules — mine-site risk assessment, the access-permission discipline of section 4.9 — come in addition and are site matters.

4.13 Trade-offs and model limits

  • The cameras, overlaps, speeds, endurance, intervals and processing times are generic assumptions [A].
  • GSD and footprint assume a nadir camera over locally flat ground; sloping walls and oblique views are not modelled.
  • σz=2GSD is a lab assumption; real accuracy depends on control, texture, light and processing [TBV].
  • The windrow is a 1D height profile with Gaussian noise; occlusion, break-line extraction and systematic deformation are not modelled.
  • Production rates come from one deterministic shift; face and dump geometry are assumed.
  • ahs_sim.mapping provides the AA-North block and the deviation statistics; the reference mine has no terrain surface data, so heights come from the node RLs.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

Small blocks over EX02 and D01 are flown every two shifts [A] under drone flight permissions, at times agreed with the AHS Controller, with launch and landing at access points. GCPs and check points stay in place between flights where they survive production [A]. Products go to the map team with the hand-off package and the quality report.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-MAP-R05: the drone beside the ground survey (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

6.4 Degraded operation — lost link and a drone down on the bench

During a block flight over the RL 330 bench the pilot's link drops as the aircraft descends below the rim [A]. The aircraft executes its lost-link behaviour [TBV] and, in the worst case, lands on R10. The pilot reports at once; the AHS Controller locks an area around it before any truck reaches it. Retrieval is a manned entry: via AP-04 to R10 the lock covers R09, R10, R11 and R24 and holds EX03 and EX04 (ch-10-06); the held trucks are coded DLY-OD-04 [H]. The incident is reviewed with the flight plan: was the return path clear of roads, was the descent below the pilot's line of sight planned, did the network team know the bands?

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along SCN-MAP-R05

  • T−5 d. The detour flight is an overflight permission in the AMS. R24 has no traffic while R05 is open, and no production circuit crosses V (LAB-12-01-01). The network team confirms the drone's bands.
  • T−1200 s. Two permissions coexist on R05: the ground crew's and the drone's. The drone's plan keeps its stand-off from the crew; the AHS Controller sees both.
  • T+0 s. The as-built for the map change is the ground survey. The drone flight has not yet started; the map change does not wait for it.
  • T+1827 s. The drone permission must be released and confirmed before the reopening at T+2400 s; an active drone permission over a road being reopened is a blocking item for the controller [H].
  • T+10 827 s. The drone product arrives with the map version it is compared against (the version deployed at T+2400 s). Differences become a change request, never a direct edit.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

A drone flight permission appears on the HMI like any permission: its area as a polygon on the map, the altitude band, the pilot's name and call sign, the validity window, the launch, landing and lost-link points, and its state. An active drone permission over a road with traffic carries a distinct marker so that the controller knows an aircraft is overhead. Expiry and revocation are shown; a drone permission that is neither released nor confirmed at the end of its window raises an alarm [H].

8.3 Procedure excerpts

A. A drone flight over an active autonomous area.

  1. Survey: prepare the flight plan — block, height mode, camera, lines, launch, landing and lost-link points, stand-off — and the latest flight end if a deadline applies.
  2. Network team: confirm that the drone's bands do not conflict with the site's spectrum plan; note any comms-site stand-off.
  3. Survey (pilot) → AHS Controller: request the drone flight permission at the access point; give area, altitude band, window and class (overflight or with exclusion).
  4. AHS Controller: check traffic in the area; if exclusion is needed, follow ch-16-04 and get the Pit Supervisor's approval for any held circuit; inform field crews in the area.
  5. AHS Controller: grant; the pilot reads back area, band, window and landing point.
  6. Pilot: fly the plan; report any lost link or deviation at once; land at the agreed point before the window ends.
  7. Pilot → AHS Controller: report landed and clear; the controller confirms the release.

B. Is drone data sufficient for this map change? (decision tree, [H])

  1. Does the product meet the accuracy the map change needs, measured on independent check points across the features (not assumed)? If not → ground survey.
  2. Are the features visible — no dust, shadow or occlusion over them (quality report exclusions)? If not → ground survey of those features.
  3. Does the product exist before the deadline (latest flight end achievable)? If not → ground survey for the deadline; drone product as a later record.
  4. Is the package complete (ch-12-01 section 4.8 plus section 4.11)? If not → returned.
  5. All yes → the map team may use it for the change.

C. Setting flight frequency. Survey proposes, the Pit Supervisor approves [H]: the re-capture trigger per surface (face advance beyond the loading-area margin, share of a dump changed) from current production rates; reviewed when the mine plan or the fleet allocation changes.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

An overflight with no traffic exclusion holds no truck and costs nothing in GMG terms. A flight that needs exclusion, a landing or a retrieval holds trucks and loading units; they are coded DLY-OD-04 [H] (coding_rules). Flights timed inside a closure that exists anyway (SCN-MAP-R05) add nothing. Useful non-GMG indicators are the age of each changing surface in the map (hours since the EX02 loading area and D01 were last captured) and the share of products delivered with independent check points.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Remote pilots learn the access-permission discipline as manned-entry workers do, and the site's drone procedure (its regulatory content [TBV]). Surveyors learn GSD and overlap planning over relief, GCP and check-point layout, and the quality report. AHS Controllers learn the drone permission, its two classes and the response to a downed aircraft. Map teams learn the sufficiency decision. Network teams learn to check drone bands. Pit Supervisors learn that "fifteen minutes in the air" is not "fifteen minutes to a map change".

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-12-02-001 [H] A flight plan over relief shall set image spacing for the required overlaps at the highest ground of the block and shall report the GSD range from the highest to the lowest ground. Rationale: spacing set for the pit floor leaves 0.25–0.34 side overlap at the crest; a constant altitude doubles or more the GSD on the floor (section 4.4). Verification: inspection of flight plans; analysis (LAB-12-02-01)

REQ-12-02-002 [H] A photogrammetric product shall be used for an AHS map change only if its quality report gives horizontal and vertical differences at independent check points spread over the features to be mapped, including the lowest and highest parts of the block; otherwise it shall be labelled "accuracy not measured". Rationale: the accuracy model is an assumption; only check points measure it (sections 4.2, 4.6). Verification: inspection of quality reports

REQ-12-02-003 [H] Ground control points and check points shall be measured from the control register in REFMINE-GRID and RL, and the register version shall be recorded in the quality report. Rationale: a product in another frame cannot be compared with the ground survey or the map (ch-12-01). Verification: inspection

REQ-12-02-004 [H] Windrow and road-edge geometry for a map change shall not be taken from a constant-altitude flight over a ramp unless the check points on the lower ramp meet the site's values. Rationale: in the lab a constant-altitude flight 80 m above P1 raises σ_z to 0.1089 m at the toe and produces five false flagged spans (section 4.6). Verification: analysis (LAB-12-02-01); inspection

REQ-12-02-005 [H] A drone product shall be placed on the critical path of a reopening or an activation only if the latest flight end computed from the deadline, processing and quality assurance is achievable. Rationale: for SCN-MAP-R05 the latest flight end is T−9000 s, before the rework exists; for SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE it is T−5400 s (section 4.7). Verification: inspection of closure and relocation plans

REQ-12-02-006 [H] Every drone flight over an autonomous area shall hold a drone flight permission (section 4.9) that fails closed; any low flight, landing or recovery inside the area shall be a manned-entry permission with an area lock. Rationale: the AMS cannot see a drone; the permission makes it visible and bounded, and a downed drone is an object on a road (sections 4.8, 4.9). Verification: inspection of permission records; drill

REQ-12-02-007 [H] Before a drone type is first flown at the site, the network team shall check its control and video link bands against the site's spectrum plan, and flight plans shall keep a stated stand-off from comms-site masts. Rationale: a drone link in the site network's band near a comms site can disturb AHS communication (section 4.8). Verification: inspection of the network team's record

REQ-12-02-008 [H] Every photogrammetric product delivered to the map team shall carry the survey hand-off package of ch-12-01 and the quality report fields of section 4.11. Rationale: images add failure modes (overlap, blur, dust, shadow, processing) that the map team must see. Verification: inspection

10. Verification and validation

  • The lab verifies its arithmetic. Eight tests in LAB-12-02-01 check GSD and footprint, the AA-North block from the AHS map, overlaps under constant altitude, the plans for AA-North, R05, EX02 and D01, the windrow comparison, the timing and latest flight ends, and the production-based frequencies.
  • The lab does not validate photogrammetric accuracy. σz=2 GSD is assumed. A site validates its products with independent check points on every block and, periodically, with a block flown twice under the same plan.
  • Comparison with ground survey. The registered timeline gives a natural experiment: the ground as-built of R05 and the drone surface of the same epoch, compared after reopening. Systematic differences along the ramp are the ones to look for.
  • Procedural validation. Drills for a revoked drone permission (pilot lands within the agreed time), an expired one (fails closed), and a downed drone on a road (area locked before approach; retrieval by manned entry).

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Planning overlap at the pit floor and losing the rim, or quoting the crest GSD for the whole pit.
  • Treating GSD as accuracy.
  • Placing GCPs only on the crest of a 120 m deep pit or ramp.
  • Using a drone product without independent check points for a map change.
  • Flying the whole pit every time instead of the blocks that change.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Promising a map change "fifteen minutes after the flight".
  • Launching at P1 without accounting for CT2's tower next to it.
  • Flying over an active road without a permission because "it's only a drone".
  • Sending someone to pick up a downed drone on R10 without a permission.
  • Letting EX02's loading area age past the face advance because the flight is "not urgent".

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — GSD and footprint. Camera A at 100 m: GSD=100×2.4×10−6/8.8×10−3=0.0273 m = 2.73 cm. Footprint 5400 × 0.0273 = 147.3 m across, 3600 × 0.0273 = 98.2 m along [A]. Line spacing at side overlap 0.70: 44.2 m; exposure spacing at forward overlap 0.80: 19.6 m. At 10 m/s the interval would be 1.96 s, below the 2.0 s minimum; the speed falls to 9.82 m/s.

Example 2 — Overlap at the crest when planned for the floor. At 80 m above the crest, the floor is 200 m below the camera. Planned for the floor, exposure spacing is 0.2×3600×0.0545=39.3 m. At the crest the along-track footprint is 3600×0.0218=78.5 m, so forward overlap is 1−39.3/78.5=0.50 — not the 0.80 planned [A].

Example 3 — Latest flight end. Processing 120 min and QA 30 min [A]: 150 min = 9000 s. For an as-built due at T+0 s the flight must end by T−9000 s; for EX02's activation at T+3600 s, by T−5400 s. In SCN-MAP-R05 the rework does not start before T−3300 s, so the drone cannot be the as-built for the reopening.

15. Key takeaways

  • A drone captures a surface in minutes; the product exists hours later. Compute the latest flight end before promising a map change.
  • GSD is set by height, pixel pitch and focal length; over a 120 m pit a constant-altitude flight has more than twice the GSD on the floor than on the crest.
  • Plan overlap at the highest ground and check GSD at the lowest.
  • The camera's minimum interval, not the aircraft, often sets the speed.
  • In the lab a terrain-following drone matches ground survey for a windrow defect on R05; a constant-altitude drone adds false flags on the lower ramp. Only independent check points tell which applies on site.
  • Drones over autonomous areas hold a drone flight permission that fails closed; a downed drone is an object on a road.
  • Fly the surfaces that change, at the rate production changes them — every two shifts for EX02 and D01 in the lab.
