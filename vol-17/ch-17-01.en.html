1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Thursday afternoon, the planning meeting for next week. The weekly plan on the screen asks for 1,980,000 t: 560,000 t from EX01, 520,000 t from EX02, 450,000 t each from EX03 and EX04. C01 is down for planned maintenance on Tuesday's DAY shift. Two items are new. Survey wants R05 closed for a few hours to rework its windrow and edge, and the Shovel Operator on EX02 says the face will be finished by Tuesday and the shovel must move 200 m north.

The superintendent asks the obvious question: "Can we do both on Tuesday, while the crusher is down anyway?" The Pit Supervisor likes the idea — no ore is lost if the ore circuits are not running. Survey shakes their head: "The face move, yes. The R05 map change, not from a request I receive today. The map change takes a week through the gates." The AHS Controller adds a second objection: "If R05 rework slips into Thursday instead, every truck climbs the west ramp. Last time that needed two more trucks than we have."

Nobody in the room is wrong. They are talking about different planning horizons. The face move is a weekly-plan item; the AHS map change behind the R05 rework belongs to the monthly plan; the truck count is a shift-plan check; and whether R05 can ever be closed without stopping the pit was decided long ago, in the life-of-mine plan, when R24 was built as the second exit. This chapter asks:

  • Which decisions belong to which horizon, and which AHS prerequisites must be committed in each?
  • How does a weekly plan become shift targets, FMS assignments and AMS map and area definitions — and where does each translation fail?
  • How many trucks does the plan need, how sensitive is that to R05, and what changes when R05 is closed and the pit climbs R24?
  • How are planned and actual tonnes at C01 and D01 reconciled at the end of the week?

It covers:

  • the horizons from life-of-mine to shift and the AHS decisions in each (FIG-17-01-01);
  • the information flow from the mine plan to FMS assignments and to AMS maps and areas, and the checks it needs (FIG-17-01-02);
  • the plan-to-shift translation with the truck, shovel, ramp and destination checks, including two models of ramp capacity, and the sensitivity to R05 travel time (FIG-17-01-03);
  • the lead times of survey, map changes, area definitions and road hand-back, placed into the plan with SCN-MAP-R05 and SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE;
  • plan-against-actual reconciliation for a week, with the variance attributed to events.

Planning structures, checks and procedures are handbook conventions [H]; no mine-planning software or data format is described. Every tonnage, factor, lead time and capacity is an illustrative assumption [A] computed with ahs_sim 0.21.1 (planning_ops) in LAB-17-01-01.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Explain how planning outputs — dig blocks, material destinations, road layouts — become FMS assignments and AMS map and area definitions, and where each translation can fail.
  2. Calculate the truck requirement for a weekly plan across EX01–EX04 with the reference-mine haul cycle and queueing models, and show its sensitivity to R05 travel time.
  3. Specify the data the mine plan must hand to the AHS (dig-block outlines, material type, destination, road designs) and the validation checks it needs before use.
  4. Analyse how the west ramp R24 changes the haulage capacity available to a weekly plan in which R05 is closed for rework.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Distinguish the life-of-mine, annual, monthly, weekly and shift horizons and state which AHS-related decisions belong to each.
  2. Identify the lead times of AHS-specific prerequisites (survey, map update, area definition, road handover) and place them in the weekly plan, using the EX02 face change as an example.
  3. Reconcile planned and actual tonnes at C01 and D01 for a week and attribute the variance to causes.
  4. Translate a weekly plan into shift targets per loading unit and destination that CR1 and the Pit Supervisor can track.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

In a manned mine many planning gaps are absorbed by people. A truck driver who finds a new face follows the dozer's tracks; a road that was rebuilt last night is driven carefully the next morning; a shovel that moved is found by radio. An autonomous fleet absorbs none of this. An autonomous truck goes only where the AMS has a route on the map version it holds, loads only at a loading area whose definition is active, and climbs a ramp only if the ramp is in its map and open. Each of these is a prerequisite with an owner and a lead time. When the plan does not know about them, the shift discovers them — usually as trucks queueing behind a closed road or a shovel waiting at a face that does not yet exist in the AMS.

Three facts about the reference mine make planning horizons concrete:

  • The pit has two exits. R05 is the main ramp (P2 to P1) and the shortest route from every shovel. The west ramp R24 (W to P3) is the alternative. Closing R05 reroutes pit traffic over R09, R24, R25 and R12; closing both isolates the pit (ch-01-03, ch-10-03). The second exit was a life-of-mine decision; how to use it is a weekly and shift decision.
  • C01 is the bottleneck of the ore system. With R05 open, C01 accepts 40 loads per hour and the ore trucks could bring 43.6 (ch-10-01). Any hour C01 is down is ore lost for that shift; any hour it is down by plan is an opportunity for work that would otherwise cost ore.
  • AHS prerequisites take time. The map change for the R05 rework runs through the gates of ch-12-09: registered seven days ahead [A], detour checked two days ahead [A], and 2,400 s from as-built survey to activation [A]. The EX02 face change needs a face survey and a new loading-area definition, activated 3,600 s after the move starts (SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE).

The problem of this chapter is the meeting of section 1.1: a plan that is correct in tonnes but cannot be executed by the FMS and AMS, or can be executed only at a cost nobody priced. The tools are simple — a translation from tonnes to trucks, a lead-time check, a reconciliation — but they only work if each horizon hands the next one what it needs.

3. Terminology and foundations

Mine plan. The planned sequence of mining by location and time over a given horizon (glossary mine-plan). In this chapter "the plan" is the family of plans at different horizons; "the shift plan" is the production plan of ch-10-01 owned by the Pit Supervisor.

Planning horizon. The period a plan covers and the detail it carries. The handbook uses six horizons [H]: life-of-mine (decades to years), annual, quarterly, monthly, weekly and shift. The quarter is included because the autonomous-area change, the longest AHS prerequisite of the model, is committed there (planning_ops uses the same six). Each horizon fixes what the next shorter one may assume.

Life-of-mine plan. The plan for the whole life of the deposit: pit shells, pushbacks, ramps and exits, waste dumps, infrastructure corridors. For an autonomous mine it also fixes which areas can ever be autonomous.

Dig block. The unit of the short-term plan: an outline on a bench with a material type, a tonnage and a destination, assigned to a loading unit for a period. The handbook treats a dig block as generic plan data [H]; no block-model or file format is described.

Shift target. The tonnes per loading unit and destination that a shift is expected to deliver. The hourly plan of short-interval control (ch-17-03) is derived from it.

Plan attainment. Actual tonnes divided by planned tonnes for a period and a material or destination. It is a handbook KPI [H], not a GMG KPI.

Lead time. The time between requesting an AHS prerequisite and having it in place. planning_ops.AHS_LEAD_TIMES gives five [A]: face survey 1 d, loading-area definition 2 d, AHS map change 7 d, autonomous-area change 90 d, road hand-back 0.5 d. Each is tagged with the horizon in which it must be committed.

Plan factors. The plan converts calendar hours into hauling hours with an availability (0.90 [A]) and a utilisation (0.85 [A]), and shovel hours with a shovel availability (0.90 [A]). They play the roles of Physical Availability and Use of Availability in the GMG Time Usage Model (SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020) [F] for the category names; the values and the mapping are this handbook's [A]/[H].

Variance attribution. Splitting the difference between plan and actual into the contributions of recorded events and a residual. It is not the production reconciliation of ch-12-04, which compares independent measurements of the same tonnes (survey, payload, plant).

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The horizons and the AHS decisions that belong to them

FIG-17-01-01 places the AHS decisions on the six horizons. The left column is what the plan decides; the right column is what the AHS needs committed at that horizon so that the shorter horizons can execute.

Life-of-mine. Pit shells, ramps and exits, destinations and corridors. For the reference mine the decision that matters most here is that the pit has two exits. Without R24 every R05 rework stops the pit; with it, R05 can be closed at a price. Ramp width and grade also decide whether autonomous trucks can pass each other and how fast they climb: R05 is steeper than R24, so a loaded truck climbs R05 at 11.4 km/h and R24 at 14.7 km/h (planning_ops.climb_speed_kmh). These are not shift-plan choices; they can only be used or not.

Annual. Fleet, loading units and destinations by period, major road builds, the crusher's shutdown calendar, and the budget for area activations, communications coverage and Survey's capacity. An annual plan that adds a bench ramp without the survey and map capacity to commission it has scheduled a road that autonomous trucks cannot use.

Quarterly. Changes of autonomous-area boundaries. In the model this takes 90 days [A], because a new boundary needs a map change, new access points, procedures, training and an area activation (ch-18-04). A face outside the current autonomous area cannot be planned for next week, whatever its tonnes.

Monthly. The dig sequence by bench and the windows for road rework. The AHS map change behind a road rework is committed here: seven days [A] through the gates of ch-12-09, with the detour checked against the as-built two days ahead.

Weekly. Dig blocks and tonnes per loading unit, face changes, planned downtime windows and closures. The face survey (1 d [A]) and the new loading-area definition (2 d [A]) for a face change are committed here, as are closures with their detour and fleet effect.

Shift. Targets per loading unit and destination, the truck allocation, work zones and permissions, and the hand-back of roads after work (0.5 d [A] in the model: inspection, as-built survey and the map gate of ch-14-07 §4.5). Within the shift, short-interval control compares actual with the plan every hour (ch-17-03).

Two properties of the figure matter for operations. First, lead times grow with the horizon. A prerequisite with a lead time longer than a horizon cannot be decided in that horizon; it must already be in the longer one. Second, each horizon fixes what the next may assume. A weekly plan that assumes R05 is closed on Tuesday assumes that a monthly plan registered the map change; if it did not, the weekly plan is wrong, not late.

4.2 From plan data to FMS assignments and AMS definitions

FIG-17-01-02 shows the information flow. The mine plan and the shift plan are people's decisions. The FMS turns them into assignments. The AMS owns the map version in use, the loading-area and autonomous-area definitions, the routes, closures and permissions. Survey owns surfaces.

The plan hands the AHS five kinds of data [H]:

Plan data Used by What it becomes Validation before use
Dig-block outline, bench, period Survey, AMS Face survey; loading-area definition (spots, queue point) Inside an autonomous area; surveyed surface exists; loading-area definition planned with lead time
Material type and destination FMS Assignment fields material, destination (ICD-FMS-AMS 1.0, ch-02-06) Ore → C01, waste → D01; destination exists and accepts the material
Tonnes per loading unit and period FMS, Pit Supervisor Shift targets, truck allocation, hourly plan Plan-to-shift checks (section 4.3) feasible, binding constraint named

Planned downtime windows (C01, loading units) FMS, plant Unavailable windows; reduced targets Targets of the feeding units set to zero in the window

Each translation can fail in a characteristic way:

  • Dig block → loading area. The face is mined out early or late; the outline is on a surface Survey has not yet measured; the block lies partly outside the autonomous area. The AMS then has no active loading area, and the FMS assigns trucks to a shovel they cannot reach (ch-13-05). The check is that every planned face change carries its face survey and loading-area definition with their lead times.
  • Material → destination. A block labelled ore with destination D01, or a waste block sent to C01, is executed exactly as written: the FMS has no grade knowledge to second-guess it. The check is a consistency rule between material and destination per loading unit [H].
  • Tonnes → trucks. Tonnes that need more trucks, shovel hours, ramp passes or tipping time than exist are not a plan but a wish. The check is section 4.3.
  • Road design → map version. A road that exists in the plan but not in the map version in use does not exist for an autonomous truck. A road that is reworked without a map change is worse: the trucks drive a map that no longer matches the road (SCN-MAP-R05, ch-12-09). The check is a registered map change with its lead time for every planned road change.

The architecture sets who may change what. The FMS never invents a route; it sends ASSIGNMENT requests and accepts ROUTE_UNAVAILABLE and DESTINATION_NOT_READY rejections from the AMS (ch-10-01). The AMS never accepts a map version that has not passed validation, and never routes a truck over a reopened road before that truck confirms the new version (REQ-16-07-008). Nothing in the planning chain overrides a vehicle-side safety decision; the plan can only make fewer of them necessary.

4.3 Plan to shift: tonnes, trucks and the four checks

planning_ops.weekly_to_shifts spreads the weekly tonnes per loading unit over the 14 shifts of the week. A planned window on a destination stops the units that feed it; the weekly target is divided by each unit's available hours and each shift receives the rate times its available hours. For the week of section 1.1 with C01 down from 24 h to 36 h:

The ore units carry their weekly tonnes over 13 shifts, the waste units over 14. The plan already absorbs the shutdown; it does not hide it.

planning_ops.plan_to_shift translates one shift's targets into resources. Per loading unit i with target Ti, payload p = 220 t and free cycle tc,i from compute_cycle:

ni=Ti/p3600HAUtc,i

where H = 12 h is the shift, A = 0.90 the availability and U = 0.85 the utilisation [A]. The fractional ni is rounded up per unit for a fixed allocation. Four checks follow, each a ratio of demand to capacity:

  • trucks: ∑ini against the producing trucks of the baseline allocation (37);
  • shovel: Ti against 3600HAsUp/ts with shovel availability As = 0.90 [A] and service ts = 167 s (spot and load);
  • ramp: loaded trucks per hauling hour on the pit ramp that the loaded routes use, against the ramp capacity (section 4.4);
  • destination: loads against tipping positions times hauling seconds over spot-and-dump time.

Three readings matter. With R05 open, the plan fits: the whole-truck allocation 10 / 9 / 9 / 9 is exactly the baseline allocation of equipment.yaml, and the tightest resource is EX01 at 0.989 of its shovel hours — a plan with no slack on EX01. With R05 closed, the ore cycles grow by 236 s and the waste cycles shrink by 39.4 s (ch-10-01, ch-16-07); the plan needs 37.06 trucks, 0.2 % more than exist, and 39 with a fixed allocation. The binding constraint moves from a shovel to the trucks. And the ramp does not bind on either exit, for a reason section 4.4 explains.

The whole and fractional counts answer different questions. The fractional sum assumes trucks can be pooled across units, as dynamic dispatch partly does; the whole count per unit assumes a fixed allocation, as the reference mine's production plan does (ch-10-01). A plan that is feasible only on the fractional sum depends on dispatch to share trucks — worth knowing before the shift starts.

4.4 Two models of ramp capacity

The ramp check compares the loaded flow with a capacity. The flow is set by the plan: 65.7 loaded trucks per hauling hour for shift 0, all of which climb R05 when it is open (the waste routes to D01 also use it). Close R05 and the same 65.7 trucks per hour climb R24. The flow follows the tonnes, not the ramp.

The capacity is less certain, and the model offers two versions [A]:

  • Time headway. A minimum time headway of 30 s [A] between loaded trucks entering the ramp gives 120 trucks per hour on either ramp. Utilisation is 0.547 on both. This is planning_ops.RAMP_HEADWAY_S; the AMS model of Volume 10 has no headway value to reuse.
  • AMS blocks. If the AMS lets a follower enter a block only when the leader has left it (ams.route_blocks), the headway is the block length plus the truck length (13.0 m) divided by the climb speed. With 200 m blocks [A]: R05 passes 56.0 trucks per hour (utilisation 1.173), R24 77.0 (utilisation 0.853). R24 is longer but gentler, so trucks climb it faster and follow closer in time.

The two models disagree by a factor of two on R05, and the block model says the baseline itself is infeasible. Neither is validated. The break-even block length at which the block model just passes the planned flow on R05 is 161.3 m; the real value depends on the following rules of a real AMS, which the handbook does not know [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AHS-RAMP-FOLLOWING). What the planner should take from this is not a number but a habit: the ramp check is only as good as its capacity model, and when the two models straddle 1 the plan needs a test on the real system (Volume 23), not a decision by spreadsheet. The fleet model used in Volumes 10 and 16 (fms.simulate_fleet) has no interaction between trucks at all, so it cannot settle the question either.

For the R05 closure the conclusion is robust: in both models R24 has more capacity per hour than R05, and the flow does not change. With R05 closed it is the trucks that bind, not the ramp.

Down to 11.4 km/h a restriction slows only the empty descent, because the loaded climb is already slower than the limit; below it, both directions slow. Each 10 s added to the R05 round trip costs about one fifth of a truck for this plan. The restriction at 20 km/h used in SCN-GNSS-R05 (ch-04-02, ch-16-07) needs slightly more trucks than the closure via R24: 37.16 against 37.06. In truck terms, a 20 km/h R05 and a closed R05 are the same problem. In production terms they are not: ch-16-07 showed that the restriction costs the fleet more and the ore circuits less. A planner who learns that R05 will be restricted for a week should therefore plan as for a closure — fewer tonnes or more trucks — and leave the choice between restriction and reroute to the playbook of ch-16-07.

4.7 Plan against actual: reconciling the week

At the end of the week the planner compares planned and actual tonnes at C01 and D01 and explains the difference. LAB-17-01-01 builds a synthetic week [A]: each shift's actual is its target times a seeded random factor (mean 1, coefficient of variation 2 %), minus the losses of three recorded events computed with the fleet models — the R05 rework done on Thursday's DAY shift instead of in the shutdown (866.2 t of ore lost, 320.8 t of waste gained), the 45-minute unplanned C01 stop of SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN on Friday (6,380 t of ore lost under hold, ch-15-06) and an unplanned EX02 face change on Saturday (3,740 t of ore). These are not data from any mine.

The three events explain 10,986.2 t of the 24,705.9 t ore variance. The residual of 13,719.6 t is about 2.3 standard deviations of the variation the synthetic week was built with: in a real reconciliation a residual of that size is not "noise" but a question. It is exactly the kind of loss that no single event records — a longer exchange here, a slow ramp there — which ch-17-03 teaches CR1 to catch within the shift (SCN-SHORTFALL-C01).

The attribution rule is the same as for lost tonnes (ch-01-07): an event's loss is the difference between runs of the same model with and without it. Two consequences follow. Losses of events computed on different models are not exactly additive; and an event that was in the plan (the C01 shutdown) contributes nothing to the variance, because the plan already carried it. Plan the known, and the variance shows the unknown.

4.8 Trade-offs and model limits

  • Steady state. plan_to_shift uses free cycles and constant factors; queueing at C01 appears only in the loop tonnes (queueing.deterministic_loop), not in the truck count. ch-10-01 shows the queue that C01's bottleneck creates.
  • Ramp capacity has two unvalidated models (section 4.4); a stated limit of the model.
  • Lead times are single numbers [A]; real ones depend on Survey's workload and on the map gates (ch-12-09) and have a spread. ch-17-04 models survey capacity and map gates over a week.
  • Start of the map-change lead time. The 7 d map-change lead time is read here as the time from request to the start of the rework window (the closure). ch-12-09 places the request 7 d before the as-built submission (T+0 s); counted from the closure at T−3600 s it is one hour short of 7 d, so the model flags it one hour late. This is also a stated limit of the model.
  • The week is spread evenly over available hours; there is no stockpile, no grade blending and no carry-over between shifts.
  • Event losses are computed at full fleet rate (100 % utilisation) on the fleet model and applied to a plan built with factors; they are upper bounds [A].
  • Synthetic actuals illustrate the method of variance attribution; they say nothing about real mines.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

In a normal week the plans nest without surprises: the monthly plan registered next month's road changes, the weekly plan's checks are feasible with a named binding constraint, the shift plan's whole-truck allocation matches the producing trucks, and the hourly reviews of ch-17-03 find the shift inside its band. The weekly reconciliation shows a small residual and events that were either planned or recorded with their cost.

6.3 Abnormal operation — the rework lands in a normal shift

The same rework requested only with the weekly plan is two days late for Tuesday (section 4.6). The planner moves it to Thursday's DAY shift. Nothing about the AMS procedure changes; the price does: 866.2 t of ore lost and 320.8 t of waste gained over the 1.75 h closure, net 545.4 t (ch-16-07). If the rework needed R05 closed for the whole shift, the shift plan would be infeasible on trucks (37.06 against 37); the Pit Supervisor would lower the ore target or move a truck from EX03 to EX02, which ch-10-01 shows recovers most of the ore at about one tonne of waste per tonne of ore.

6.4 Abnormal operation — SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE in the plan

The EX02 face change has a short lead time and fits in the weekly plan. Placed in the shutdown it costs no ore; placed in a normal shift it costs 3,740 t of ore for the hour EX02 does not load (ch-13-05). Unplanned — the face finishing early, as in ch-13-05's walkthrough — it costs the same tonnes and appears in the reconciliation as an event, not as plan.

6.5 Degraded operation — a prerequisite is late

If the map change is late (validation fails, Survey's capacity is taken by another job), the hand-back is blocked and R05 stays closed into the NIGHT shift. The shift plan built for R05 open is then infeasible on trucks (1.002); the binding constraint has moved, and the plan must be re-run with R05 closed before the shift starts, not discovered at the first hourly review. A late prerequisite is a planning event: it is recorded with its cause and its cost.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

  • Translate before issuing. Running the plan-to-shift checks for every shift of the week, and for the variant with each planned closure, costs seconds and catches the infeasible shift before it reaches CR1. The checks belong where the plan is made, not in the FMS at shift start [R].
  • Fractional versus whole trucks. Reporting both shows whether a plan relies on pooling. A plan feasible only on the fractional sum (R05 at 25 km/h: 36.18 pooled, 38 fixed) is a dispatch assumption, not a fleet fact.
  • Ramp capacity model. A time headway is simple and conservative for R05 at 30 s; a block model ties capacity to the AMS's real following rule and makes the climb speed visible. Which one is right is a property of the AMS, to be measured; the plan should show both while they disagree.
  • Lead times as data. Storing lead times with their horizon and owner turns "Survey said no" into a check that runs every time the plan changes.
  • Planned windows as resources. Treating a planned destination outage as capacity for AHS work, rather than as lost time, is a scheduling choice that costs nothing but discipline.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along SCN-MAP-R05

  • T−18 d to T−4 d. The planning chain is offline: plans, change records and checks. The only system interface is the AMS change record created at T−7 d (ch-12-09), whose ID every later record cites.
  • T−1 d. The shift plan enters the FMS: targets per loading unit and destination, the allocation, C01's unavailable window. The FMS must hold C01 unavailable for the whole window so that no ore assignment is issued.
  • T−3600 s to T+2700 s. System time: closure, replanning, permission, validation, deployment, per-truck confirmation and route return, as in ch-12-09. The plan's role is over; its assumptions are now checked by the gates.
  • T+36,000 s. The ore circuits restart on a map version the waste trucks have used for nine hours. Any ore truck that did not confirm the new version (one in maintenance, for example) keeps its route around R05 until it does (REQ-16-07-008).

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

CR1 sees the plan at the shift-plan horizon: targets per loading unit and destination, the allocation, planned windows (C01 unavailable 06:00–18:00 Tuesday), planned closures with their change-record ID and planned hand-back time, and planned face changes with their loading-area activation. During SCN-MAP-R05 the AHS Controller sees the closure, the permission, the validation and deployment records and the per-truck confirmation list of ch-12-09. A planned closure without a change-record ID, or a face change without an activation time, is a planning gap visible on the HMI before it becomes a production loss.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — putting R05 rework into the plan

  1. Mine planner (T−18 d, monthly plan): place the rework window; prefer a planned destination outage on the circuits it would stop; record the window with its map-change lead time and the detour.
  2. Survey (by T−7 d): register the map change request; list the segments and the detour; agree the window with the Pit Supervisor.
  3. Mine planner (T−5 d, weekly plan): spread the weekly tonnes over the shifts with the planned windows; run the plan-to-shift checks for each shift and for the variant with R05 closed; run the lead-time check for every AHS item.
  4. Mine planner and Pit Supervisor (T−4 d): issue only a plan whose checks are feasible and whose prerequisites are on time; for each late item, move it or record that it is out of the plan.
  5. Survey (T−2 d): check the detour against the road and record the result.
  6. Pit Supervisor (T−1 d): build the shift plan; schedule ore-truck maintenance into the C01 window with the Maintenance Planner; confirm closure and hand-back times with Survey and the AHS Controller.
  7. AHS Controller (T−3600 s to T+2700 s): apply the closure, grant the permission and deploy the validated map as in ch-12-09; record the hand-back time against the plan.
  8. Mine planner (end of week): reconcile plan and actual at C01 and D01; attribute the variance to recorded events; question a residual larger than the expected variation.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Mine planners need to know the AHS prerequisites, their owners and lead times, and the two exits — enough to run the checks and read a binding constraint. Pit Supervisors need to read a shift plan's checks and know when to re-run them (a closure that extends, a late hand-back). AHS Controllers need to recognise a planning gap on the HMI: a closure without a change record, a face change without an activation. Survey needs a shared calendar with the plan. The planning meeting itself is the training ground: the meeting of section 1.1, held with the lab's tables on the screen.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-17-01-001 [H] The mine plan shall hand the AHS, for every dig block of the weekly horizon, its outline and bench, the surface it was designed on, the material type, the destination, the loading unit and the period; and, for every planned road change, the design, the window and the map change request ID. Rationale: each item becomes a loading-area definition, an assignment field or a map change; a missing item becomes a gap the shift discovers (section 4.2). Verification: inspection of a weekly plan against this list.

REQ-17-01-002 [H] Before a weekly plan is issued, its data shall be validated: every dig block lies inside an autonomous area and on a surveyed surface, every loading unit's material–destination pair is consistent (ore to C01, waste to D01), and every road the plan uses exists in the map version in use or has a registered map change. Rationale: the FMS and AMS execute what they receive; the planning chain is the last place where a wrong destination or an unmapped road is cheap to fix (sections 4.2, 7.2). Verification: test with a plan containing each defect; inspection.

REQ-17-01-003 [H] For each shift of a weekly plan, and for each variant with a planned closure, the plan-to-shift translation shall report trucks (fractional and whole), shovel, ramp and destination ratios, state whether the plan is feasible and name the binding constraint; a plan with any ratio above 1 shall not be issued as firm. Rationale: R05 closed moves the binding constraint from shovel EX01 (0.989) to the trucks (1.002) (section 4.3). Verification: test (LAB-17-01-01: R05 open 37 trucks, feasible, binding shovel EX01; R05 closed 39, infeasible, binding trucks).

REQ-17-01-004 [H] Every plan item that needs an AHS prerequisite shall carry the prerequisite, its owner, its lead time and its request time; the plan shall flag items whose prerequisites cannot be ready by the item's start, and a flagged item shall be moved or removed before the plan is issued. Rationale: an R05 rework requested with the weekly plan is two days late; a face outside AA-North is 85 days late (section 4.6). Verification: test (LAB-17-01-01: two late items, slack −2.00 d and −85.00 d); inspection of issued plans.

REQ-17-01-005 [H] A planned road change that alters the geometry of a road used by autonomous trucks shall have its AHS map change registered no later than its lead time before the work, and that registration shall be committed in the monthly plan. Rationale: the map change takes seven days [A] through the gates of ch-12-09; the weekly plan is too late to start it (section 4.6). Verification: inspection of change records against the monthly plan.

REQ-17-01-006 [H] A planned closure of a pit exit shall be checked against every other closure in the same window; a plan that closes R05 and R24 together shall be issued only with a recorded decision to stop production in AA-North. Rationale: closing both exits isolates the pit (ch-01-03, ch-10-03). Verification: test of the closure check with overlapping windows; inspection.

REQ-17-01-007 [H] The shift plan published to CR1 and the FMS shall give targets per loading unit and destination, the allocation, the planned unavailable windows of destinations and loading units, the planned closures with their change-record IDs and planned hand-back times, and the planned loading-area activations. Rationale: CR1 can only track what it receives, and a planning gap is cheapest when it is visible before the shift (section 8.1). Verification: inspection of the FMS shift plan; demonstration at shift start.

REQ-17-01-008 [H] The weekly plan-against-actual comparison shall give plan, actual, variance and attainment per material and destination, attribute the variance to recorded events computed as the difference between model runs with and without each event, and report the residual together with the variation expected from the plan's own spread. Rationale: events explain 10,986.2 t of a 24,705.9 t ore variance in the synthetic week; the residual of 13,719.6 t is about 2.3 standard deviations and is a question, not noise (section 4.7). Verification: test (LAB-17-01-01 part E); inspection of the weekly report.

REQ-17-01-009 [H] The FMS shall not issue assignments to a loading unit whose loading-area definition is not active in the AMS, nor to a destination inside a planned unavailable window. Rationale: a plan that runs ahead of its prerequisites must stop at the system boundary, not at the shovel (sections 4.2, 6.4). Verification: test on the FMS with an inactive loading area and a planned C01 window.

10. Verification and validation

Verification of the model. The lab's tests check that the translation equals a hand computation from compute_cycle (EX02: 40,000 t / 220 t loads over 12 × 0.90 × 0.85 × 3600 / 1564.1 s per truck), that the shift targets conserve the weekly tonnes, that the R05-closed cycles and the closure cost reproduce ch-10-01 and ch-16-07 (1800.1 s, 495.0 t/h, 545.4 t), that the hour of the EX02 move reproduces ch-13-05 (3,740 t) and that the C01 stop reproduces ch-15-06 (6,380 t). The lead-time results follow from AHS_LEAD_TIMES; the registered times of SCN-MAP-R05 are checked against the registry.

Verification of the requirements. REQ-17-01-003, -004 and -008 have test cases in the lab; REQ-17-01-002, -006 and -009 need tests on a real planning tool, FMS and AMS; -001, -005 and -007 are verified by inspection.

Validation. Whether the plan factors, ramp capacity and lead times describe a real mine is a question for that mine's data: plan factors against its time-usage records, ramp capacity against observed ramp flows with the real AMS, lead times against its change records. How mine-planning practice structures horizons and reconciles plans is not verified by this handbook [TBV] (SRC-PUB-MINE-PLANNING-HORIZONS; SRC-PUB-MINE-RECONCILIATION-PRACTICE).

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Treating the ramp as the bottleneck of a closure. The flow does not change with the ramp; with R05 closed the trucks bind.
  • Building the plan-to-shift translation on a fleet model without factors and comparing it with plans built with factors: the fleet model produces about 191,000 t per shift (ch-10-01) against a plan of about 147,000 t.
  • Storing lead times in people's heads rather than as data with an owner and a horizon.
  • Letting the FMS accept assignments to a loading area that is not yet active "because the plan says so".

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Planning a road rework into a production shift when a planned crusher outage the same week stops the circuits it would cost.
  • Asking for a map change in the weekly meeting for work in the same week.
  • Issuing a plan that is feasible only on pooled trucks without telling CR1 that it depends on dynamic dispatch.
  • Explaining a weekly variance with the two biggest events and calling the rest noise.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — Trucks for EX02 on shift 0. Target 40,000 t → 181.8 loads. Hauling seconds per truck: 12 × 0.90 × 0.85 × 3600 = 33,048 s; cycle 1564.1 s → 21.13 loads per truck. Trucks: 181.8 / 21.13 = 8.60 → 9. With R05 closed the cycle is 1800.1 s, 18.36 loads per truck, 9.90 → 10 trucks.

Example 2 — The R05 ramp check. Loads in shift 0: 147,363 t / 220 t = 669.8 loads over 12 × 0.85 = 10.2 hauling hours = 65.7 loaded trucks per hour, all climbing R05. At a 30 s headway the capacity is 120 per hour: 0.547. With 200 m blocks ams.route_blocks cuts the 1,339.6 m of R05 into seven equal blocks of 191.4 m. With a 13.0 m truck at 11.4 km/h (3.18 m/s) the headway is (191.4 + 13.0) / 3.18 = 64.3 s → 56.0 per hour, utilisation 1.173. On R24, at 14.7 km/h, 77.0 per hour and 0.853.

Example 3 — Is the R05 rework late? Requested with the weekly plan at T−4 d; lead 7 d → ready at +3 d; needed at +1 d (Tuesday 06:00): two days late. Requested with the monthly plan at T−18 d → ready at −11 d: twelve days of slack.

Example 4 — Where to put the rework. In Thursday's DAY shift: 1.75 h × 495.0 t/h = 866.2 t of ore lost and 1.75 h × 183.3 t/h = 320.8 t of waste gained, net 545.4 t. In Tuesday's shutdown shift: no ore lost, 320.8 t of waste gained.

Example 5 — Reading the reconciliation. Ore variance 24,705.9 t; events 866.2 + 6,380.0 + 3,740.0 = 10,986.2 t; residual 13,719.6 t. Expected spread: √(Σ (0.02 × 83,077)²) over 13 ore shifts = 0.02 × 83,077 × √13 = 5,990.8 t. The residual is 2.3 times that.

15. Key takeaways

  • Each horizon fixes what the next may assume; an AHS prerequisite belongs to the horizon at least as long as its lead time.
  • The plan becomes FMS assignments and AMS map and area definitions only through prerequisites with owners.
  • For the reference plan, R05 open is feasible with EX01 binding (0.989); R05 closed needs 39 trucks and binds on trucks (1.002).
  • The ramp flow follows the tonnes; the ramp capacity depends on a model that must be measured on the real AMS.
  • A 20 km/h R05 costs as many trucks as the R24 reroute.
  • A planned destination outage is the cheapest window for AHS work on the circuits it stops.
  • Reconcile the week against events, and treat a large residual as a question.
