1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is 12:45 at the reference mine. Fifteen minutes ago the pit-floor comms trailer CT3 lost power (SCN-COMMS-R05), and the trucks on lower R05, the RL 330 bench and the loading links stopped within their granted paths. CT3 is back now. Within one second the AHS Controller's alarm list fills with stop entries stamped 12:30:03, and the telemetry tiles of the same trucks jump from "no data since 12:30:00" to fresh values. The Pit Supervisor, watching over the controller's shoulder, asks whether the trucks have just stopped again. Down the corridor, the Maintenance Planner's tyre-temperature trend for one of those trucks has a fifteen-minute hole that is filling in, point by point, while they watch.

Nothing is wrong with the trucks. The room is watching the onboard telemetry buffers empty: every record written while the link was down arrives a quarter of an hour late, stop records first because they have priority. Whether this is read correctly depends on design decisions made long before: what each truck sends and how often, how much it keeps when the link is gone, what goes first when it returns, and which clock stamped each record.

The questions this chapter addresses are:

  • Which DT17 signals are sent periodically, which only when they change, and which as discrete events — and what does each choice cost in bandwidth and in how late the site sees a change?
  • How much data does the fleet produce, how big must each truck's buffer be to ride through a CT3 outage, and how long does the backlog take to drain?
  • When the backlog arrives, how does the site tell history from live data, and avoid acting twice on the same event?
  • What happens to the record when a truck's clock drifts (SCN-CLOCK-DT17), and how is that detected and corrected without touching the AMS's own records?
  • What does a "no data" state on the HMI or a dashboard imply about the truck, and what does it not?

This chapter covers:

  • signal telemetry and event telemetry, with sampling rate, deadband and heartbeat for five DT17 signals (FIG-22-02-01, tyre temperature from SCN-TYRE-DT17 as the worked case);
  • the telemetry budget of one truck and of the fleet, and its place below AMS control traffic on the radio network;
  • the four times of a record — event, device, send and receive — and the common event schema;
  • store-and-forward through the CT3 outage, prioritisation and buffer overflow (FIG-22-02-02);
  • the site side of back-fill: live or historical, duplicates, ordering;
  • clock-offset detection and correction in the analytics path;
  • the telemetry path from VCS and ADS signals to site ingest (FIG-22-02-03), kept apart from the AMS control path.

All rates, sizes, deadbands, margins and durations are illustrative assumptions [A]; no product, protocol or published data rate is implied. The numbers come from LAB-22-02-01 and LAB-22-02-02. Telemetry here is for monitoring and analytics; safety-related signal handling on the truck belongs to ch-08-05 and ch-04-04 and is not changed here.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader can:

  1. Distinguish periodic signal telemetry from discrete event telemetry and select sampling rate, deadband and payload for at least five DT17 signals (speed, payload, tyre temperature, localisation integrity, fire-system status), with each value tagged [A].
  2. Calculate the telemetry bandwidth and onboard buffer size needed for 40 trucks to ride through a CT3 outage of given duration using store-and-forward, and the backlog drain time after restoration.
  3. Design timestamping and clock-synchronisation rules (event time, send time, receive time) and analyse how an onboard clock offset corrupts event ordering.
  4. Design telemetry prioritisation so that high-priority events (fire-system status, remote-stop acknowledgement, fault events) are sent ahead of bulk signal data when bandwidth on the marginal-coverage ramps R05 and R24 is constrained.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader can:

  1. Explain what a "no data" state for a truck on the HMI or dashboard does and does not imply about the truck's real state, and which procedure applies.
  2. Describe the post-outage symptoms an AHS Controller sees when buffered telemetry back-fills (sudden bursts, out-of-order alarms) and how to avoid acting twice on the same historical event.
  3. Report a suspected telemetry fault on DT17 (missing signal, frozen value, implausible payload) to the Field Technician with the evidence needed for diagnosis.
  4. Explain why the AHS Controller's HMI and the Maintenance Planner's tools may show the same DT17 signal at different resolutions and delays, and which one each role should rely on.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine has 40 autonomous trucks, four loading units, crusher C01 and dump D01. The main ramp R05 and the west ramp R24 carry most traffic out of the pit, and coverage on both is marginal in places. Lower R05, the RL 330 bench roads R09 to R11 and the loading links R19 and R20 are served by CT3 alone (ch-11-07). Most of what the trucks produce is never looked at in real time. It is read later — on a maintenance trend, in the shift report, in an incident review — and is then expected to be complete, correctly ordered and correctly timed.

Four problems recur at sites like this.

Too much, or not enough. Sent at the rate the ECUs produce it, most telemetry is redundant and competes with AMS control traffic on marginal links. Sent too sparsely, it hides the rise the Maintenance Planner needed to see.

No data is not a state. When a truck stops reporting, a dashboard tile goes grey or freezes. People read that as "stopped", "fine" or "broken" depending on habit. It is none of these. It means only that the site has not received anything since a certain time. The truck may be driving normally outside coverage, may have stopped within its granted path, or may have a failed telemetry unit.

History that looks live. After a coverage outage the trucks send what they buffered. If the site treats every arriving record as new, the controller sees a wave of alarms that are fifteen minutes old, and may act on them a second time. A shift report that counts records by arrival time puts the stop in the wrong quarter-hour, or in the wrong shift.

Clocks that disagree. Every record says when it happened, according to the clock of the unit that wrote it. If DT17's onboard clock drifts (SCN-CLOCK-DT17), DT17's records sort in the wrong order against the AMS's and the FMS's records, and an investigator reading the merged log sees effects before causes.

The shared walkthrough is SCN-COMMS-R05; the numbers come from synthetic logs of the registered scenarios.

3. Terminology and foundations

Telemetry. Automatic transmission of measured data from machines to central systems (glossary). In this chapter telemetry means monitoring and analytics data. It is not the AMS's control exchange (grants, extensions, acknowledgements), which has its own class on the network (ch-11-03) and its own timing rules (ch-02-07).

Signal telemetry and event telemetry. Signal telemetry samples a quantity that always has a value — speed, payload, tyre temperature, localisation integrity, position. Event telemetry reports that something happened — a state change, a stop, an alarm, a reservation granted. A missing signal sample is usually recoverable from its neighbours; a missing event is not.

Sampling rate, deadband and heartbeat. A periodic policy sends a sample every 1/f seconds. A deadband policy checks the signal at its periodic rate and sends only when it has moved by at least the deadband δ since the last sent value. A heartbeat (glossary) re-sends the current value at least every H seconds even when nothing changed, so that the receiver can tell "unchanged" from "silent". An event policy sends every change.

Four times of a record. The event time is when the event happened, as stamped by the producing unit's clock — the device time of ahs_sim.telemetry. A synthetic log also knows the true event time; a real system does not. The send time is when the record left the unit, the receive time when site ingest received it, on the site clock.

Sequence number. A number set by the sender per stream that increases with every record (glossary). Here a stream is one source on one unit (for example ADS on DT17).

Store-and-forward. The onboard behaviour of keeping records in a buffer while the link is down and sending them when it returns. Back-fill is what the site sees as a result: records arriving long after their event time, filling a gap in the site's record.

Clock offset and drift. Clock offset is a node's clock reading minus the reference time; at the reference mine the active AMS instance is the reference (glossary, ch-02-07). Drift is the rate at which the offset grows. Clock synchronisation (glossary) keeps offsets within a tolerance; when a unit loses its time source, its clock free-runs.

Staleness and idempotency. A value is stale when it is too old to be trusted for the decision at hand (glossary). A consumer is idempotent when receiving the same record twice has the same effect as receiving it once (glossary).

4. Technical explanation

4.1 What DT17 sends

Table 1 lists the monitoring signals of the telemetry model with their periodic rate, deadband, size on the wire and priority. All values are [A]; they are chosen to make the arithmetic visible, not taken from any truck.

Signal Source Periodic rate Deadband Bytes per sample Share kept by deadband on a typical cycle Priority
Speed VCS 1 Hz 1 km/h 16 0.35 bulk
Payload VCS (payload system) 0.2 Hz 2 t 16 0.10 bulk
Tyre temperature (hottest tyre) VCS (tyre monitoring) 0.1 Hz 1 °C 24 0.30 bulk
Localisation integrity ADS 1 Hz 0.1 m 16 0.25 high
Fire-system status onboard safety-related system on change, heartbeat 60 s — 16 1.0 high
Position ADS 1 Hz 0.5 m 24 0.90 bulk

Position is added to the five signals of the learning objectives because every analytics product needs it. Each rate follows how fast the quantity changes in a way that matters to its monitoring consumer [A]: speed and localisation integrity within seconds, payload in steps, tyre temperature over minutes. The fire-system status is a state with few values, so it is sent on change with a 60 s heartbeat. Localisation integrity and fire-system status are high priority not because the site decides anything with them — the truck makes those decisions itself (ch-05-06, ch-08-05) — but because after an outage they are what a controller and an investigator need first.

Discrete events are the other half: cycle-state changes, segment traversals, stops, resumes, derates, alarms and fault records from the VCS and the ADS, plus the AMS and FMS records about DT17 written on the site. The model counts 600 event records per truck-hour at 160 bytes each [A]. FIG-22-02-01 shows one haul cycle of DT17 on mission M-20481, from EX02 to C01, in the seed-0 baseline log.

The cycle takes 2022 s. At 1 Hz, speed produces 2022 samples; with a 1 km/h deadband and a 60 s heartbeat, 40 — one per segment boundary and one per heartbeat on the long loaded climb of R05. Tyre temperature gives 203 samples at 0.1 Hz and 58 with a 1 °C deadband. The log holds 24 discrete event records for the cycle (including the previous cycle's dump completion at the boundary) plus six VCS summary samples every 300 s [A]. Events are few, irregular and each one matters; signal samples are many, regular and individually expendable.

4.2 Choosing the rate and the deadband: tyre temperature

SCN-TYRE-DT17 begins with a tyre-temperature alarm raised on the truck, followed by a speed restriction at T+5 s and DT17's withdrawal to PK1 at T+600 s. The alarm is an onboard event that telemetry carries to the site as a high-priority record; nothing in the site's sampled trend raises or clears it. Tyre limits and alarm criteria are outside this chapter (SRC-PUB-TYRE-MONITORING-LIMITS [TBV]). The telemetry design decides how well the Maintenance Planner sees the trend that led up to the alarm, and at what cost.

LAB-22-02-01 uses a synthetic hottest-tyre trace [A] of one hour at 1 s resolution: a base of about 70 °C, a ±6 °C swing with the haul cycle, measurement noise of 0.6 °C standard deviation and, from 1800 s, a rise of 30 °C per hour. The noise-free trend rises by 5 °C in 419 s after the fault starts — sooner than 600 s, because the haul-cycle swing is rising at the same time. The lab asks how long after the trend has risen 5 °C the value held at the site (the last sample received) shows the rise too. The 5 °C is a teaching device for "a rise the planner should see", not a limit.

Policy Samples per hour Bytes per second Rise seen after
Periodic 1 Hz 3601 24.01 3 s
Periodic 0.1 Hz 361 2.41 11 s
Periodic 1/60 Hz 61 0.41 61 s
Deadband 0.5 °C (checked at 0.1 Hz) 210 1.40 11 s
Deadband 1 °C 111 0.74 31 s
Deadband 2 °C 66 0.44 51 s

Three things follow. Sending every second costs ten times more than every ten seconds and gains eight seconds on a trend that develops over minutes. A 1 °C deadband sends about a third of the 0.1 Hz samples and sees the rise 20 s later — a good trade for a maintenance trend. And a deadband smaller than the noise sends noise: the 0.5 °C case sends more than half of the periodic samples and sees nothing earlier. Against the noise-free trend every policy's held value is off by 1.7 to 2.3 °C at worst, because the samples themselves are noisy; filtering on the truck would help but adds delay [R].

The heartbeat matters as much as the deadband. Without it, a tyre temperature that stays inside its deadband sends nothing, and the site cannot tell a stable tyre from a dead sensor. With a 60 s heartbeat, any longer silence is a fault or a gap.

4.3 The telemetry budget

The budget of one truck is the sum of its signals and its events, plus framing:

rtruck=(∑sfsbsks+∑ebeHe+nevbev3600)(1+o)

where fs is the periodic rate, bs the bytes per sample and ks the share kept (1 for periodic, the deadband share otherwise); event-triggered signals count one heartbeat per He; nev is event records per hour of bev bytes; and o is the framing overhead (0.2 [A]). For the periodic policy: the five periodic signals give 16+3.2+2.4+16+24=61.6 B/s, the fire-system heartbeat 0.27 B/s, the events 600×160/3600=26.67 B/s, together 88.53 B/s, and with 20 % overhead 106.24 B/s per truck, 0.85 kbit/s.

Policy Per truck Fleet of 40 Per truck-day Per fleet-day
Periodic 106.24 B/s (0.85 kbit/s) 34.0 kbit/s 9.18 MB 0.367 GB
Deadband 71.01 B/s (0.57 kbit/s) 22.7 kbit/s 6.14 MB 0.245 GB

The budget is linear in the number of trucks, and deadbands save a third. ch-11-02 designed the radio network with an allowance of 2000 B/s of telemetry per truck [A]; the monitoring telemetry here uses 5.3 % of it (3.6 % with deadbands). The allowance also covers diagnostics uploads and event-recorder extracts not modelled here, plus headroom. In the class order of ch-11-03, AMS control, digital access permissions and manned-vehicle position reports go ahead of telemetry, and video behind it. Telemetry is the class that waits when a link degrades — so the buffer of section 4.5 can start to fill before the link is lost. Storage (0.367 GB per fleet-day) is not the cost; the share of marginal links on R05 and R24 is. Retention is the subject of ch-22-04.

4.4 Four times and the event schema

Every record in the handbook's event schema (ch-22-01) carries an event ID, its source and unit, its kind, a payload, a schema version, a per-stream sequence number and four times [H]:

Field Set by Clock Meaning
Event time (device time) the producing unit the unit's own clock when the event happened, as that unit saw it
Send time the producing unit the unit's own clock, or the site clock in the model when the record left the unit
Receive time site ingest site clock when the site received it
True event time — — known only in a synthetic log; used by the lab to check results

The design rule is: stamp at the source, and never overwrite the stamp [H]. Event time says when something happened; send time explains a delay; receive time is what the site knows for certain, and its fallback when a unit's clock is not trusted. If ingest replaced event time by receive time, a back-filled stop would appear to have happened at 12:45.

Sequence numbers complete the picture without any clock: a gap means a lost record (section 4.7), a repeated number with the same event ID a duplicate, a lower number than one already seen an older record arriving late.

The stamping clock must be synchronised to the site reference, the active AMS instance (ch-02-07), with a status flag (REQ-02-02-006). Which time-synchronisation protocol a site uses and what accuracy it achieves is outside this chapter; published protocols for it include the Precision Time Protocol (IEEE 1588-2019, SRC-STD-IEEE-1588-2019, catalogue level) and the Network Time Protocol (RFC 5905, SRC-STD-IETF-RFC5905) [F]; which of them mine sites commonly use is [TBV] (SRC-STD-TIME-SYNC-PROTOCOLS), and no accuracy figure is assumed. What matters is that every record states, directly or through its unit's status, whether its clock was synchronised.

4.5 Store-and-forward and prioritisation

When the link to the site is lost, the onboard telemetry agent keeps writing records into a buffer instead of sending them. When the link returns, it sends the backlog at the uplink share the network gives it, while new records keep arriving. With a per-truck rate r, an outage of T seconds and a margin m, the buffer needed and the drain time at an uplink share u (bytes per second) are:

B=rTm,tdrain=rTu−r

The drain time grows without bound as u approaches r: if the uplink share is barely more than the live rate, the backlog never empties. LAB-22-02-01 gives, for the periodic budget and a margin of 1.5 [A]:

Outage Backlog Buffer with margin Drain at 16 kbit/s Drain at 64 kbit/s Drain at 256 kbit/s
300 s 31.9 kB 47.8 kB 16.8 s 4.0 s 1.0 s
900 s (SCN-COMMS-R05) 95.6 kB 143.4 kB 50.5 s 12.1 s 3.0 s
1800 s 191.2 kB 286.8 kB 101.0 s 24.2 s 6.0 s
3600 s 382.5 kB 573.7 kB 202.0 s 48.5 s 12.0 s

These buffers are small; the model's default 4 MB [A] holds 10.5 hours of periodic monitoring telemetry. The sizing rule still matters: diagnostics uploads added to the same buffer multiply the rate, buffers are sized once at design, and the design outage must be chosen — ch-11-07 sweeps outages from 5 to 60 minutes, and an hour needs four times the buffer of the registered 900 s.

The drain also loads the site. When CT3 returns, every truck that was inside its lost area drains through CT3 at once. At 64 kbit/s each [A], the registry's 8 trucks take 512 kbit/s, 5.1 % of a 10 Mbit/s site uplink; the 18 trucks of the model in section 4.6 take 1152 kbit/s, 11.5 % of 10 Mbit/s and 23.0 % of the 5 Mbit/s of a degraded link (ch-11-03 [A]). With QoS configured as ch-11-03 requires, the drain cannot delay control messages; but the trucks are restarting at the same time, and a drain-rate limit on each truck is a cheap safeguard [R].

Prioritisation. The model sends high-priority kinds first — alarms, stops, resumes, derates, communication-lost and exclusion-zone records — then the rest oldest first [A]. The handbook's list adds fire-system status, the records of remote-stop acknowledgements and fault records [H]: after an outage the first question is what happened to each truck while it was out of sight. (The remote stop itself travels on the independent stop path of ch-04-04, in no telemetry class.) Bulk samples can wait seconds more without losing meaning.

4.6 SCN-COMMS-R05 in the telemetry model

LAB-22-02-02 generates a seeded synthetic DAY shift with the CT3 outage triggered at 12:30:00 site time (23,400 s after 06:00 [A]), so that T+0 s = 12:30:00. The registry's key events are used exactly: CT3 down at T+0 s, trucks detecting the loss and stopping within their granted paths at T+3 s, the AMS marking them communication-lost and freezing surrounding reservations at T+10 s, and CT3 restored with reconciliation at T+900 s.

The approximation. The registry's initial state has eight trucks inside the affected area [A], and ch-11-07 follows those eight, with reconciliation complete at T+932 s and a staged restart from T+962 s. The telemetry model works at segment level: it treats every segment of the CT3 lost area — R05, R09, R10, R11, R19 and R20, as network.affected_segments gives them — as lost in full, and stops every truck on them at T+3 s. In the seed-0 shift that is 18 trucks inside and 19 held short (DLY-OD-04); the count is between 14 and 19 over seeds 0 to 7. All of them resume together at T+960 s, 60 s after restoration [A]. The approximation changes fleet totals (bytes, records, uplink load), not the per-truck values (buffer depth, delay, drain time) that the design rules use.

The results:

  • Each truck inside the area buffered about 96 kB (DT21: 96.0 kB at T+900 s); the 18 together 1.73 MB. No record was dropped with the 4 MB buffer.
  • 107 onboard records had their event time inside the outage and were back-filled: 18 stops, 18 cycle-state changes, 17 segment traversals and 54 summary samples (three per truck, at T+0, T+300 and T+600 s). The longest delay between event time and receive time was 900.2 s.
  • The first back-filled records arrived at T+900.22 s, and they were the 18 stop records stamped T+3 s, because stops are high priority. The cycle-state changes of the same instant followed a few hundredths of a second later.
  • In the first second after restoration site ingest received 109 onboard records; every backlog was empty at T+912 s. A second burst of 77 records in the ten seconds from T+960 s is the model's resume of all 37 stopped or held trucks.
  • Ordered by receive time, the shift's log has 21 adjacent pairs in reversed true order; ordered by event time, none. Store-and-forward reorders arrival, not the record — as long as consumers order by event time.

Records written on the site are unaffected: the AMS's communication-lost records at T+10 s and the controller's delay codes arrive within a fraction of a second. During the outage the site knows a great deal about the trucks while knowing nothing new from them.

4.7 When the buffer overflows

A buffer that is too small for the outage must drop something. The telemetry model offers two policies [A]: drop oldest (when full, discard the oldest bulk data, then the oldest records; with prioritisation, high-priority records last) and drop newest (when full, refuse what arrives). LAB-22-02-02 re-runs the same outage with smaller buffers. Bulk signal telemetry is modelled as bytes, 106.24 B/s per truck, beside the records of the log.

Buffer per truck Policy Records lost (18 trucks) Stop records lost Bulk lost Summary samples kept
4 MB or 143.4 kB any 0 0 0 T+0, 300, 600 s
96 kB drop oldest 19 0 1.7 kB T+300, 600 s
96 kB drop newest 0 0 3.6 kB T+0, 300, 600 s
50 kB drop oldest, prioritised 71 0 826.1 kB T+600 s
50 kB drop oldest, not prioritised 89 18 822.3 kB T+600 s
50 kB drop newest 17 0 829.8 kB T+0, 300, 600 s
20 kB drop oldest, prioritised 89 0 1365.4 kB none
20 kB drop oldest, not prioritised 107 18 1361.6 kB none
20 kB drop newest 20 0 1369.1 kB T+0, 300 s

The 96 kB buffer, just below the peak, loses data in the very outage it was sized for: margin matters. With 50 kB, drop oldest without prioritisation loses all 18 stop records — the one thing the controller and the investigator needed — because they were the oldest in the buffer; prioritisation keeps them. Drop newest keeps the stops but loses the end of the outage, so the site never learns what the signals did just before the link returned.

The handbook's rule combines the two [H]: protect high-priority records, drop bulk signal data first and oldest first, and record the loss in a gap record stating the stream, the time span and the records and bytes lost. Without it the site sees a quiet period; sequence numbers show lost records, but not lost bulk bytes.

4.8 Back-fill at the site: live or history

The site has to decide, for each arriving record, whether it is news. Three mechanisms do this.

Age. A record whose receive time is far later than its event time is history. LAB-22-02-02 uses a threshold of 10 s [A]: in the COMMS shift, 125 onboard records are older than that on arrival — the 107 back-filled records, and also the 18 summary samples stamped T+900 s that queued behind the backlog and arrived 12.2 s late. An age rule alone therefore misclassifies records written just after restoration, and its threshold has to exceed the drain time.

A buffered flag. Better, the truck says so: a record sent from the buffer carries a flag [H], and the site shows it as history whatever its age. The age rule remains a check for units that do not set the flag.

Identity. Delivery over radio is at least once: a truck that missed an acknowledgement resends. The lab sends second copies of 20 % [A] of the back-filled records; 13 copies arrive, and a consumer keyed on the event ID rejects all 13. One that raises an HMI entry per arriving stop record raises 19 entries for 18 stops. ch-22-03 develops idempotent consumers; the HMI and the alarm list are consumers too.

Sorting on event time restores the order — because the trucks' clocks were synchronised. When a clock is not, sorting on event time is what goes wrong.

4.9 Clock offset: detection and correction in the analytics path

In SCN-CLOCK-DT17, DT17's onboard computer loses its time source and its clock free-runs ahead. ch-02-07 follows this on the control path with a drift of 1 ms/s [A] and an AMS tolerance of 1.8 s: the AMS marks DT17's clock untrusted at T+1800 s, issues DT17's reservations with AMS-side expiry only from T+1801 s, and the controller asks the Field Technician to inspect the time source at T+1830 s. This chapter does not change that. The question here is what the drift does to the record, and how the analytics path repairs it.

LAB-22-02-02 applies the same drift to DT17's onboard records in a seed-0 baseline shift from 06:30 (T+0 s; trigger 1800 s after 06:00 [A]). DT17 has 805 records in the shift, 630 stamped by its own clock; the AMS's and FMS's records about DT17 are stamped on the site.

What goes wrong. By 07:45 the offset is 4.5 s. A VCS sample taken at 07:45:00 carries the stamp 07:45:04.5 and sorts after an FMS delay-code record written at 07:45:02.6. Over the shift, ordering by event time reverses eight adjacent pairs of DT17's records by more than 0.5 s. At DT17's last onboard record, 95.5 s before 18:00, the offset is 41.3 s; a record in the last 41 s of the shift would have been counted in the NIGHT shift (none was, in this run).

Detection. The analytics path cannot see the true time, but it can compare each onboard record's device time with its receive time on the site clock. For a live record the difference is the offset plus a few tenths of a second of latency, and the median over a window ignores the few back-filled records. analytics.detect_clock_offset estimates the offset per unit and window and flags a unit above the tolerance [A]. With 300 s windows and 1.8 s, DT17's estimate first exceeds the tolerance in the window ending at T+2100 s, at 2.03 s; DT17 is the only unit flagged. The AMS flagged it 300 s earlier, checking every message as it arrives. The analytics check repairs the record after the fact; it does not replace the AMS's monitor, and it takes the AMS's clock-untrusted record as an input [H] rather than re-deriving that decision differently.

Correction. analytics.correct_clock subtracts the estimated offset, interpolated between windows, from the flagged unit's device times. Afterwards the eight ordering errors are gone and the largest remaining error is 0.22 s, about the latency the estimate cannot separate from the offset. Corrected times are stored beside the original stamp, never over it, and every derived product says it used them [H].

The correction needs prompt receive times. For a truck both out of coverage and drifting, back-filled records carry neither a good device time nor a meaningful receive time; the honest ordering is by sequence number within the stream, marked as uncertain. Ordering by what caused what rather than by clock readings goes back to Lamport (1978) [F] (SRC-PUB-LAMPORT-1978), introduced in ch-02-07.

4.10 Trade-offs and limits of the model

  • Rate against latency. Slower sending saves bandwidth and delays what the site sees. For anything the control room acts on in seconds, telemetry is the wrong channel; the AMS and the onboard systems already hold that information.
  • Buffer against freshness. A large buffer loses nothing but delivers history late; priority and a drain-rate limit decouple the two.
  • Model limits. Coverage at segment level, not the 200 m resolution of ch-11-07; 18 trucks instead of 8; one uplink share per truck, no radio contention; a 300 s summary sample in the log, the 1 Hz bulk stream as bytes; constant drift; synthetic tyre data. None of the numbers is a design value for a real site.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation: telemetry on an ordinary shift

On an ordinary shift each truck sends about 106 B/s of monitoring telemetry and its event records; all 40 together about 34 kbit/s, a few per cent of the radio-design allowance. Records arrive a few tenths of a second after their event time. The HMI shows each truck's live state from the AMS, and the telemetry tiles show their values with an age of a second or two. The Maintenance Planner's trends are complete within minutes. Clocks are synchronised; ordering by event time and by receive time gives the same result except for records that crossed the network at the same instant. Nobody thinks about telemetry, which is the point.

6.2 Abnormal operation: DT17's clock drifts (SCN-CLOCK-DT17)

At 06:30 (T+0 s) DT17 loses its onboard time source and its clock free-runs ahead at 1 ms/s. Nothing visible happens at first; DT17 keeps working. At T+1800 s the AMS's offset monitor marks DT17's clock untrusted (ch-02-07); from T+1801 s the AMS issues DT17's reservations with AMS-side expiry only. At T+1830 s the AHS Controller acknowledges the alert and asks the Field Technician to inspect the time source. In the analytics path, the offset check flags DT17 in the window ending at T+2100 s; the check also receives the AMS's untrusted record and marks DT17's stamps from T+0 s as suspect. The daily processing corrects DT17's onboard times, and the shift report notes that DT17's records were corrected. Without the correction, eight pairs of records in DT17's history would be reversed.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

  • Signal by signal. One fleet-wide rate is too fast for tyre temperature and too slow for localisation integrity.
  • Deadband with heartbeat for slow signals, events for states.
  • Stamp at the source, mark the clock, never overwrite. This is what makes section 4.9 possible.
  • One priority list for the buffer and the drain. A list applied only to the drain still loses the stops on overflow (section 4.7).
  • Buffer from the design outage with margin, a gap record on loss, a buffered flag, a drain-rate limit.
  • Analytics repairs copies, never the control path [H]. The AMS's records, decisions and flags are inputs, never outputs, of the analytics path (FIG-22-02-03).

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

On the shared timeline, three clocks and three paths meet.

  • T+3 s. The stop is a truck decision. Its record is written at T+3 s on the truck's clock and waits in the buffer. The site's knowledge of the stop at this moment comes only through the AMS (T+10 s, communication-lost), and the AMS does not know that the truck stopped — it knows that the truck is silent and must have stopped within its granted path.
  • T+10 s. The AMS's records are stamped and received on the site within milliseconds. These are the authoritative records of the control path during the outage.
  • T+900 to T+912 s. The drain delivers history. If the HMI raises entries by receive time without the buffered flag, it shows 18 "new" stops at 12:45:00. The reconciliation of ch-02-08 uses the trucks' re-registration reports, not the telemetry back-fill; the two must not be confused, because the back-fill can still be draining when reconciliation completes.
  • After the shift. Delay intervals start at the stop's event time, T+3 s, not at the time the record arrived. A KPI pipeline that used receive time would move 15 minutes of DLY-OD-05 per truck to after restoration.

If DT17's clock were also drifting during the outage, its back-filled records would carry a wrong device time and a receive time 900 s late — neither usable for ordering. Section 4.9's fallback applies: order DT17's records by sequence number, place them by the AMS's records of the same period, and mark the result.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

The AHS Controller's HMI shows each truck's state from the AMS and, beside it, the telemetry values with their age [H]. During the CT3 outage the trucks inside the lost area show communication lost (an AMS state with a procedure, ch-16-07) and their telemetry tiles show no data since 12:30:00. "No data" is not a state: it says when the site last heard from the truck. It does not imply that the truck has stopped (it may be driving with only its telemetry missing), that it is fine (the value shown is old), or that it has a fault (the link may be down). The procedure is chosen by the AMS state: "no data" with a normal AMS state is a telemetry fault (section 8.3); with communication lost, ch-16-07 applies.

After restoration, back-filled entries appear as history: greyed, with their event time and the note received 12:45:00, back-filled [H], acknowledged as a group, never triggering live-alarm actions. The Maintenance Planner sees the same signals as a trend that updates in minutes from deadband samples and summaries, complete only after back-fill. The HMI is for now, the planner's tools for the shift and the week; neither is a safety function.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — after a coverage outage, and reporting a telemetry fault on DT17

A. After restoration (SCN-COMMS-R05, from T+900 s).

  1. AHS Controller: follow the comms-loss procedure of ch-16-07 for the trucks; decide on resume from the AMS state and reconciliation, not from telemetry.
  2. AHS Controller: when back-filled entries arrive, check that they are marked history and that their event times fall inside the outage (12:30:00 to 12:45:00). Acknowledge them as one group. Do not re-issue any action for them.
  3. AHS Controller: if an entry arrives unmarked with an event time inside the outage, treat it as history anyway, note the truck and the entry, and report the missing flag to the Field Technician after the shift.
  4. AHS Controller: if a high-priority back-filled record shows something not known at the time (an alarm, a fire-system change), handle it under its own procedure now — the event is old, the condition may not be.
  5. AHS Controller: check that every truck's telemetry tiles show fresh data within a few minutes after the drain. A truck whose tiles stay "no data" while its AMS state is normal is a telemetry fault (part B).
  6. Pit Supervisor: at the end of the shift, confirm that the delay intervals of the outage start at the stop times (T+3 s) and that the gap record list is empty or explained.

B. Reporting a suspected telemetry fault on DT17.

  1. AHS Controller: identify the symptom — missing signal (one stream silent beyond its heartbeat while others are live), frozen value (the value has not changed for longer than plausible, heartbeats continue), or implausible value (for example payload 0 t on a loaded haul, or a tyre temperature below ambient).
  2. AHS Controller: collect the evidence: truck, signal, first time of the symptom (event time), last good value and its time, AMS state and location at that time, whether other streams of the truck are live, whether other trucks in the same coverage area show the same, and the clock status of the truck.
  3. AHS Controller: log the fault and pass the evidence to the Field Technician; inform the Maintenance Planner that the signal's trend is unreliable from that time.
  4. Field Technician: diagnose on the truck under permission (sensor, telemetry agent, time source); report the cause and the time span of bad data.
  5. Maintenance Planner: mark the span in the trend; do not plan from it.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

Telemetry creates no delay code; a gap in the record is a data-quality matter (ch-22-05), not Operating Delay. The CT3 outage produces DLY-OD-05 (stopped on communication loss) inside the area and DLY-OD-04 (held for access permission or work zone) for trucks held short. Telemetry decides whether these intervals are timed correctly: from event times they start at T+3 s, from receive times at restoration. Operating Delay and every KPI built on it (ch-01-05) inherit that choice, and a clock correction can move a record into another shift's KPIs.

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • AHS Controllers: AMS state against telemetry age; recognising back-fill; the fault evidence list (simulator exercise with SCN-COMMS-R05).
  • Pit Supervisors and Maintenance Planners: why reports and trends change after back-fill or correction; gap marks.
  • Field Technicians: telemetry agent, buffer, priorities and time source on the truck.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-22-02-001 [H] Every telemetry record shall carry its event ID, source, unit, kind, a per-stream sequence number and an event time stamped by the producing unit, together with the synchronisation status of that unit's clock; site ingest shall add the receive time and shall not overwrite the event time. Rationale: ordering, deduplication, gap detection and clock correction all depend on these fields (sections 4.4, 4.9). Verification: inspection of the schema; test (LAB-22-02-02 ordering by event and by receive time).

REQ-22-02-002 [H] While its link is lost, a truck shall buffer its telemetry. The buffer shall hold at least the periodic telemetry rate times the design outage duration times a margin of 1.5 or more [A]. On restoration, high-priority kinds (fire-system status, alarms, stops, resumes, remote-stop acknowledgements, fault records) shall be sent before bulk data, and on overflow bulk data shall be dropped first and high-priority records last. Rationale: without prioritisation, a 50 kB buffer lost all 18 stop records of the outage (section 4.7). Verification: test (LAB-22-02-02 overflow cases); demonstration on a truck with a simulated outage.

REQ-22-02-003 [H] A record sent from the onboard buffer shall be marked as buffered. The HMI and alarm list shall show buffered records as history with their event time and shall not raise them as new live alarms or as new requests for acknowledgement. Rationale: 18 stops stamped T+3 s arrived at T+900.22 s; an age rule alone also misclassified 18 records written at T+900 s (sections 4.6, 4.8). Verification: test (HMI with the LAB-22-02-02 log); inspection.

REQ-22-02-004 [H] Site ingest and every consumer that raises an entry for a person shall accept each event ID once. Rationale: at-least-once delivery after restoration produced 19 stop entries for 18 stops in the lab (section 4.8). Verification: test (LAB-22-02-02 duplicate case).

REQ-22-02-005 [H] The analytics path shall estimate the clock offset of every onboard source, shall take the AMS's clock-untrusted record as an input, and shall order and assign to shifts the records of a flagged unit only by corrected or receive time. A corrected time shall be stored beside the original stamp, and every product built from it shall state that it used corrected times. Rationale: DT17's 1 ms/s drift reversed eight pairs of records; correction removed them (section 4.9). Verification: test (LAB-22-02-02 clock case); inspection of derived products.

REQ-22-02-006 [H] No telemetry-derived value, analytics result, clock correction or inferred state shall be written into AMS, FMS, ADS or VCS records or used by them to grant or revoke a permission, release a reservation or restrict a truck. The analytics path shall receive copies of control-path records through a read-only interface. Rationale: separation principle of Volume 22; the AMS's own monitor, not analytics, governs DT17's reservations (section 4.9). Verification: inspection of interfaces; analysis of the data-flow diagram (FIG-22-02-03).

REQ-22-02-007 [H] The HMI shall show the age of every telemetry value and shall show a missing telemetry stream as "no data since <time>", distinct from any truck state; a telemetry gap shall never be presented as a truck state. Rationale: "no data" says nothing about whether a truck has stopped (section 8.1). Verification: inspection; test with a silent telemetry stream on a truck whose AMS state is normal.

REQ-22-02-008 [H] A truck that drops telemetry for any reason shall send a gap record stating the stream, the time span and the number of records and bytes lost. Each truck's drain rate after restoration shall be limited to a configured share of the uplink. Rationale: lost bulk data is otherwise invisible (section 4.7); 18 trucks draining together took 23.0 % of a degraded 5 Mbit/s uplink (section 4.5). Verification: test (overflow with a gap record); network test at restoration.

10. Verification and validation

  • Budget. Measured bytes per test truck over a cycle against section 4.3, periodic and deadband.
  • Buffer and drain. Simulated outages of the design durations: no high-priority loss, bulk loss only beyond the designed buffer, drain within the computed time, a gap record on loss (REQ-22-02-002, -008).
  • Back-fill at the site. Replay the LAB-22-02-02 log through the HMI: no live alarm from back-fill, duplicates rejected, event times shown (REQ-22-02-003, -004).
  • Clock. Inject drift on one truck; check detection, correction, kept stamps and marked products (REQ-22-02-005).
  • Separation. No interface out of the analytics store writes to a control system (REQ-22-02-006).
  • Validation. After a real outage, every stop the AMS inferred should have an onboard stop record with an event time inside the outage.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • One rate for everything. Fast signals starve, slow signals waste bandwidth.
  • A deadband below the noise. It sends noise and saves little (section 4.2).
  • No heartbeat. A stable signal and a dead one look the same.
  • Stamping at ingest. Back-filled events appear to happen at restoration.
  • Buffer sized without margin. The 96 kB case lost data in the outage it was sized for.
  • Priority only in the drain. Overflow then drops the most important records first.
  • Silent overflow. No gap record, so lost data looks like quiet time.
  • Analytics feeding control. A corrected time or an inferred state written back into the AMS.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Reading "no data" as "stopped" — or as "fine".
  • Acting twice. Re-issuing a stop, a call or a work order for a back-filled event already handled during the outage.
  • Resuming on telemetry. Fresh tiles after restoration are not reconciliation.
  • Coding delays by arrival. Delay intervals that start when the record arrived instead of when the truck stopped.
  • Reporting a fault without evidence. "DT17's telemetry is wrong" without signal, time, AMS state and clock status sends the Field Technician to search.
  • Planning from a trend with a gap or a correction mark without reading the mark.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — buffer and drain for the CT3 outage. Backlog 106.24×900=95.6 kB; with margin 1.5, 143.4 kB. Drain at 64 kbit/s = 8000 B/s: 95616/(8000−106.24)=12.1 s; at 16 kbit/s: 95616/1893.76=50.5 s.

Example 2 — tyre deadband over the fleet. The 1 °C deadband saves (2.41−0.74)×40=67 B/s — small on its own. Deadbands pay on many slow signals together, because a slow signal at a high rate is mostly repetition.

Example 3 — when the offset crosses the tolerance. Drift 1 ms/s, tolerance 1.8 s: 1.8/0.001=1800 s, the AMS flag. The analytics window from T+1800 to T+2100 s has a mean offset of 1.95 s; its estimate, 2.03 s, exceeds the tolerance, so the flag comes at the window's end, T+2100 s. With 1800 s windows it would come at T+3600 s.

Example 4 — the offset at the end of the shift. 0.001×(43200−1800)=41.4 s by 18:00; the lab's last DT17 record shows 41.3 s, 95.5 s before the shift ended, so no record changed shift in this run.

15. Key takeaways

  1. Telemetry is monitoring data; it never carries a safety decision.
  2. Signals are designed by rate, deadband and heartbeat; events by priority and completeness.
  3. The model's budget is small — 106.24 B/s per truck, 34.0 kbit/s for 40 trucks — but it waits below control traffic on marginal links.
  4. Store-and-forward needs rate × design outage × margin, one priority list for buffer and drain, and a gap record.
  5. Stamp at the source, add receive time at the site, overwrite neither; order by event time unless the clock is untrusted.
  6. Back-fill is history: flagged on the truck, shown as history, each event ID accepted once.
  7. "No data" is an age, not a truck state; the AMS state chooses the procedure.
  8. Clock offsets are corrected in the analytics path beside the original stamp, never in the control path.
