1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is the next morning. On the previous day shift DT17, loaded on mission M-20481, reported a fire on the main ramp R05 (SCN-FIRE-DT17). The Emergency Response Team put the fire out, the Pit Supervisor authorised clearance of the exclusion zone, and production resumed. Now the incident review is about to start. On the table are five printouts that do not agree. The fire-system alarm says the fire was detected at 15:30:00. The AMS log says the exclusion zone was set at 15:30:10. The shift report shows DT17's breakdown code starting at 15:30:05, but the controller remembers typing it a minute later. The live dashboard that the Pit Supervisor was watching showed DT17 "travelling loaded" for five minutes after it had stopped. And nobody can find the fire-system log of DT17, because no system was ever told that it is a record the mine keeps.

The questions the review raises are data questions:

  • Which system holds the authoritative record of each step, and who owns it?
  • Why do the times differ, and which time is the one that happened?
  • Why did the dashboard lag the AHS Controller's HMI, and did that matter?
  • Which records had to be secured before the truck was reset, and who should have asked for them?
  • Could anything on the analytics side have changed what the truck or the AMS did?

This chapter covers:

  • the data products of every architecture element in a checked, machine-readable catalogue (FIG-22-01-02, LAB-22-01-01);
  • a layered architecture (edge, site, enterprise) that keeps the analytics path outside the control path (FIG-22-01-01);
  • a common event schema that joins one SCN-FIRE-DT17 event across FMS, AMS, VCS and control-room records (FIG-22-01-03, LAB-22-01-02), and the plant–FMS–AMS join that explains a C01 loss;
  • the flows that must survive a site-to-enterprise link outage;
  • authoritative records, the dashboard against the HMI, tracing a delay to its sources, and an incident's records.

All sizes, rates, volumes and durations in this chapter are illustrative assumptions [A]; no data platform, database, streaming or cloud product is described.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Map every data-producing element of the reference architecture (FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS, independent safety functions, onboard safety-related systems, manned-vehicle devices, people) to the data products it emits, with owner, rate and consumer for each.
  2. Design a layered data architecture (edge, site, enterprise) for the reference mine that keeps the analytics path read-only with respect to the AMS control path, and justify each boundary.
  3. Specify a common event schema (event ID, source, event time, receive time, asset ID, mission ID, map version) that lets a single SCN-FIRE-DT17 event be joined across FMS, AMS and VCS records.
  4. Analyse which data flows must survive a site-to-enterprise link outage and which may be deferred.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Identify, for a given operational question (e.g. "why did EX02 starve on night shift?"), which system holds the authoritative record and which role can request it.
  2. Explain to a Pit Supervisor why the dashboard view of a truck can lag the AHS Controller's HMI view, and which one governs a live decision.
  3. Trace a delay shown on a shift report back to the source events and systems that produced it, naming the role responsible at each step.
  4. List the records an incident review of SCN-FIRE-DT17 needs from FMS, AMS, VCS, radio logs and the control-room log, and state who in the control room must request each before it is lost.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine runs 40 autonomous trucks, four shovels (EX01–EX04), crusher C01 and dump D01, with one control room, CR1, at the mine infrastructure area. Every element of the AHS writes records as a side effect of doing its job: the VCS samples speed and payload, the ADS reports its cycle state and each segment it traverses, the AMS logs reservations, permissions and exclusion zones, the FMS records assignments and delay codes, the crusher's control system reports ready and not ready, and the AHS Controller keeps a control log and talks on the radio. In the synthetic baseline shift of this chapter's lab, that adds up to 28,641 records in 12 hours, without the high-rate signal telemetry that is not carried in the log.

Three problems recur at the reference mine, and none of them is solved by buying storage.

Nobody knows what exists. "Why did EX02 starve on night shift?" is answered from FMS assignments, ADS cycle states, AMS reservations and perhaps a plant record. Each system has an owner, but no list says which products exist, who uses them and who must keep them. The fire review failed because the fire-system log was on no list.

Times do not line up. Each producer stamps records with its own clock. A truck's onboard clock can drift (SCN-CLOCK-DT17); a record sent from R05, where coverage is marginal, can arrive minutes late; a delay code typed by a controller is entered after the event it describes. A review that sorts records by "the time" without saying which time will build the wrong story.

The analytics side is tempted to act. Once the data is collected, someone will propose that a dashboard alert restrict a truck's speed, or that an inferred delay cause recode the FMS delay automatically. Each moves a decision from the control path, which is argued in a safety case, into a path that is not. The handbook's position is a principle [H]: the analytics path observes the safety and control path and never sits inside it.

This chapter answers all three with a catalogue of data products, a common event schema with two times, and a layered design in which data leaves the control path through one-way, read-only copies only.

3. Terminology and foundations

Data product [H]. A defined set of records with one producer (an element of the reference architecture or a role), one owning role, a schema, a rate class and named consumers. "AMS permissions and zones" is a data product; "the AMS database" is not. The unit of the catalogue is the product, because ownership, retention and access are decided per product.

Data catalogue [H]. The machine-readable list of all data products with their producer, owner, consumers, schema, rate class, safety relevance and, for derived products, their inputs. The reference mine's catalogue is DATA_CATALOGUE in ahs_sim.telemetry; LAB-22-01-01 checks it.

Source product and derived product. A source product is written by an element doing its job (the AMS writing a zone record). A derived product is computed from other products (shift KPIs from cycle states and delay codes). In the reference mine's catalogue, 16 products are produced by architecture elements or people and 11 are derived products on the analytics path.

Safety and control path [H]. The elements that decide or enforce who may move where: AMS, ADS, VCS, independent safety functions, onboard safety-related systems and manned-vehicle devices, with the messages between them. The FMS sits above it as the production path: it assigns, the AMS executes and enforces (ch-01-04).

Analytics path [H]. Everything that collects, stores, computes and displays records for people: ingest, stores, KPI pipelines, dashboards, reports, condition monitoring. It reads copies of control-path records; it never writes to the control path.

Event schema [H]. The fields every record carries, whoever produced it, so that records from different systems can be joined. It is not a message schema (ch-02-06, ch-02-07): a message schema governs what one element sends to another to make something happen; the event schema governs what is recorded about it.

Event time and receive time. Event time is the time at which the event happened, as stamped by the producing unit's clock. Receive time is the time at which the site's ingest received the record, stamped by the site clock. In a synthetic log a third time is known, the true time; in a real mine it is not, which is why the schema keeps both of the others.

Telemetry and event log. Telemetry is the automatic transmission of measured data from machines; the event log is the ordered record of discrete events. Signal telemetry is the subject of ch-22-02.

Authoritative record (source of truth). For each fact, the record that governs when copies disagree (glossary source-of-truth). For a live decision about a truck, it is the control path's state as shown on the AHS Controller's HMI; for a shift's delay record, it is the FMS delay product after the controller's coding; for the order of events in an incident, it is the reconstructed timeline built from the preserved records.

Preservation hold [H]. An instruction that exempts the records of a time window (and, if stated, of named units) from routine retention and reset until it is released. It is the data side of scene preservation (ch-16-06). Retention itself is the subject of ch-22-04.

Three observations follow. Volume and importance are unrelated: the VCS signals are by far the largest machine product, but the AMS's 5.30 MB per day of permissions and zones is the record an incident review cannot do without. The baseline synthetic shift maps all 28,641 records to eight products and none to "unmapped": 13,492 ADS traversals, 5,328 VCS summary samples, 4,415 ADS cycle states, 1,963 FMS delay records, 1,729 AMS reservations, 1,711 FMS assignment and dump records, two plant records and one control-log record (the shift handover). An unmapped record is a catalogue defect: something is being produced that nobody has declared.

4.2 The layered architecture: edge, site, enterprise

FIG-22-01-01 shows the architecture this chapter proposes for the reference mine [H]. It has three layers and two paths.

The edge layer is on the machines: the ADS, VCS and onboard safety-related systems of each truck, and the position reporting units and field terminals of manned vehicles. Its data duty is local: stamp each record with the unit's clock and a per-stream sequence number, keep it in an onboard buffer, and send it when the network allows (store-and-forward, ch-22-02). The buffer is on the analytics side of the boundary: whether it is full or empty has no effect on what the truck does.

The site layer is on the mine network: AMS, FMS, the independent safety function, the plant interface and CR1 on the control side; ingest, store and analytics on the other. The site ingest is where records first meet the common event schema and receive their receive time. The site store keeps recent records (the hot tier of ch-22-04) and applies preservation holds. The site analytics computes what the shift needs: KPIs, delay inference, the live dashboard and the shift report.

The enterprise layer is off site: warm and cold stores, weekly reports, fleet condition monitoring and the longer analyses of this volume, for the Maintenance Planner, site engineering and the mine's management.

The control path runs vertically on the left of the figure: FMS to AMS to ADS to VCS, with the independent safety function and the onboard safety-related systems acting on the VCS directly, and manned-vehicle devices reporting into the AMS. Its messages, timeouts and fail-closed rules belong to earlier volumes (ch-01-04, ch-02-07, ch-04-05) and are not changed by anything in this chapter.

The analytics path runs on the right. It receives one-way copies of control-path records: from the onboard buffer over the wireless network, and from AMS, FMS, the independent safety function, the plant and CR1 over the wired site network. Its outputs go to people only: the HMI of CR1 may show a dashboard tile, but the tile is read by a person, and the person acts through the control path's own interfaces. There is no data path back. The red arrow in FIG-22-01-01 is not a missing feature; it is a design decision [H].

Each boundary is justified by a failure that must not propagate.

  • Edge / site. The wireless network is the least reliable link at the reference mine (R05 and R24 have marginal coverage). The edge must keep its records through an outage, so the onboard buffer exists; but the safety reaction of the truck must not depend on the analytics side's state, so the buffer is not on the control path.
  • Control / analytics. The control path's behaviour is argued in the safety case (ch-04-10). A dashboard, a KPI pipeline or a model that could change that behaviour would have to be argued in the same way. Keeping the analytics path read-only keeps it out of the argument and lets it change quickly.
  • Site / enterprise. The link to the enterprise is outside the mine's control and may be lost for hours. Nothing the shift needs live, and nothing that must be preserved, may depend on it (section 4.6).

4.3 The data catalogue and its checks

A catalogue is useful only if it is checked. LAB-22-01-01 runs six checks on DATA_CATALOGUE [H]:

  1. Every input has a producer. A derived product's inputs must all be products in the catalogue (missing_producers).
  2. Every product has a consumer. A product that no one consumes and no product uses is an orphan (orphan_products): either it is not needed or its consumer is not declared.
  3. Every consumer resolves. A named consumer must be a product, an architecture element or a role.
  4. Every architecture element produces something. An element without a product is either silent, which is unlikely, or producing records that nobody has declared.
  5. Separation. No analytics-path product may be consumed by a control-path element (AMS, ADS, VCS, independent safety function, onboard safety-related systems, manned-vehicle devices), or be an input of a product that one of them produces. A derived product consumed by the FMS is flagged for review: it is allowed only through a person's decision.
  6. Preservation coverage. Every item of the preservation list of ch-16-06 (section 4.7 there) must map to at least one product in the catalogue.

The module's catalogue passes checks 1, 2, 3 and 5, and fails two. Check 4 finds that the onboard safety-related systems (OSS) produce no product: in the model, the fire alarm reaches the log as a VCS alarm record with the payload field system: OSS, but the fire system's own log of detection, actuation and faults is not declared anywhere. Check 6 finds the same gap from the other side (the preservation list asks for "the fire-system log of DT17") and one more: the radio recordings, which the list names next to the controller's incident record, have no product. These two gaps are the review's missing printout from section 1.1.

The lab closes them by adding oss.fire_system_log (producer OSS, owner Maintenance Planner, safety-relevant, consumed by the incident record and the Maintenance Planner) and cr.radio_recordings (producer people and control room, owner AHS Controller, safety-relevant) [A]. The extended catalogue has 29 products, 45 nodes and 86 edges in its flow graph, and passes all six checks. FIG-22-01-02 is drawn from it.

Check 6 also reports something that is not a gap but must be known: three products on the preservation list, the ADS cycle states, the FMS assignments and the FMS delay codes, are not marked safety-relevant in the catalogue. Routine retention applies to them, so only a preservation hold keeps an incident's records of them. That is acceptable if, and only if, the hold is placed before routine retention or an operational action can delete them (REQ-22-01-006).

The separation check is the one that guards the principle. To see it work, the lab adds a deliberately wrong product: a predictive tyre alert computed from vcs.signals on the analytics path and consumed by the AMS to restrict a truck's speed. The check reports one violation, analytics.tyre_alert_to_ams → AMS. The right design for the same need is the one of ch-08-05 and ch-22-09: the tyre monitoring system on the truck is an onboard safety-related system on the control path, and the predictive alert goes to the Maintenance Planner, who plans an inspection. A planning alert is never a safety alarm [H].

Finally, check 1 shows why the C01 join of section 4.7 needs the plant record. With plant.states removed, the catalogue reports two missing producers: analytics.delay_inference and analytics.production both lose an input.

4.4 The common event schema

The event schema is what makes records from different producers joinable. The handbook's common event schema [H] has these fields; the first nine are those of EventRecord in ahs_sim.telemetry (schema version 1.0), and the last two are resolved at the site ingest in LAB-22-01-02.

Field Meaning Set by Why it is needed
event_id Unique ID: shift, source, unit and sequence (e.g. 000-DAY-VCS-DT17-000115) Producer Deduplication, reference from an incident record
source Producing element (VCS, ADS, AMS, FMS, PLANT, CONTROL_ROOM, MAINT) Producer Ownership; which clock stamped the record
unit (asset ID) Truck, shovel, plant, road or area the record is about Producer Join by asset
kind Event type from a fixed list Producer Mapping to a data product
device_time_s (event time) When it happened, by the producer's clock Producer Order of events, durations
send_time_s When it left the unit Producer Store-and-forward delay (ch-22-02)
receive_time_s (receive time) When the site ingest received it, by the site clock Site ingest Plausibility of event time, latency, late data
seq Sequence number per stream (source + unit) Producer Gaps and duplicates (ch-22-05)
payload, schema_version Content of the event; version of the schema Producer Interpretation across versions
mission_id (+ cycle) Mission the record belongs to Producer, or resolved at ingest Join across FMS, AMS and ADS
map_version (+ software, configuration) Context in force at event time Resolved at ingest from the context history Interpretation of positions, segments and rules

Two choices in this schema matter more than the rest.

Two times, never one. Event time is what a review wants, but a clock the site does not control stamps it. Ingest time is stamped by the site clock but includes transport delay, buffering and, for a manual entry, typing time. Keeping both detects an implausible event time (a record cannot be ingested before it happened) and measures latency. With one time, a fast clock looks like a slow network.

Join keys by reference, not by time. Two records about the same event can be joined by time only if their clocks agree and their delays are known. A key that names the mission, the cycle and the asset survives clock faults and delays. Where the producer cannot carry the key (a VCS summary sample does not know the mission), the ingest resolves it from the record that does (the FMS assignment), and records how it did so.

In the reference mine's model, the FMS gives each truck one mission ID per shift with a cycle counter [A]: DT17's records carry M-20481 and cycle 20 at the time of the fire. LAB-22-01-02 resolves the mission of every record of the fire shift in one of four ways:

Resolution Records What it means
By key (unit + cycle in the payload) 20,637 ADS cycle states and traversals, AMS reservations, FMS assignments
By time (the mission running on the unit at event time) 7,283 VCS samples, FMS and control-room delay codes, alarms, zones, work orders
Not mission-scoped 3 Records about the plant (C01) and the shift handover
Unmatched 6 DT29 and DT39 (planned maintenance all shift) and DT40 (not scheduled): no mission exists

The six unmatched records are not errors: those trucks had no mission. The report is useful because it separates "no mission existed" from "the record could not be tied to its mission"; the second is a data-quality defect (ch-22-05).

The map version is resolved the same way, from a context history of map, software and configuration versions with their validity times (ch-12-09, ch-22-04). In the fire shift no version changed, so every record resolves to AHS-MAP-0001 [A]. The field is still needed: the same segment ID can describe a different road after a map release.

4.5 One event across five data products

FIG-22-01-03 places the records of SCN-FIRE-DT17 from LAB-22-01-02 on one timeline. The trigger is at 34,200 s after the 06:00 shift start (15:30) [A]; the registry's key events are at exactly T+0, 5, 10, 15, 60 and 600 s.

Each lane is one data product; each record shows its event time (filled circle) and its receive time (open triangle).

  • OSS signal, in vcs.faults_alarms. The fire alarm, event time T+0, ingested at T+0.43 s (wireless latency [A]).
  • AMS exclusion zone, in ams.permissions_zones. Zone set at T+10 s, ingested 0.02 s later over the wired network; cleared at T+1800 s, the model's assumed zone duration [A].
  • FMS re-plan, in fms.assignments. The re-plan of the EX02 trucks at T+60 s. The model records the re-plan but keeps the fleet's allocation [A].
  • Delay code, in fms.delay_codes. DT17's DLY-DN-01 has an event time of T+5 s, the start of the interval it describes, but an receive time of T+65 s, because the AHS Controller entered it 60 s later [A]. The first DLY-OD-14 code of a held truck (DT02) has event time T+10 s and receive time T+70 s. Over the zone's lifetime 36 trucks receive DLY-OD-14: 12 at T+10 s, and the last at T+681.4 s as it reaches R05.
  • Incident record, in cr.control_log. The controller's acknowledgement at T+15 s and the ERT's arrival at the zone boundary at T+600 s.

Joined by mission and asset, all of DT17's records in the window, from the alarm to the zone clearance, resolve to M-20481, cycle 20 and AHS-MAP-0001. The figure makes the review's first confusion of section 1.1 disappear: the breakdown code "starting at T+5 s" and the controller "typing it a minute later" are both true; they are two different times of the same record.

4.6 Which flows must survive a site-to-enterprise link outage

The enterprise link can be lost for hours. LAB-22-01-01 assigns each product to a layer [A]: onboard producers to the edge, the other source products and the shift's derived products to the site, and five derived products (segment statistics, process analysis, context, condition monitoring and the weekly report) to the enterprise. It then asks three questions of every flow.

  1. Does any enterprise product feed a site product? If it did, the site would lose an input in an outage. In the reference design no flow runs from the enterprise to the site.
  2. Does any enterprise product have a live consumer? A product used during the shift for a live decision must not be computed off site. None does: the weekly report is read by the Pit Supervisor, but not for a live decision.
  3. What must the site buffer? Nine site products feed enterprise products: the ADS cycle states, traversals and stops, the AMS reservations, the FMS assignments, the work orders, the map versions, the VCS signals and the shift KPIs. Their volume is 143.2 MB per 24 h of outage [A], almost all of it VCS signals.

The flows divide into two classes [H]:

  • Must survive (stay at the site). Sixteen products in the extended catalogue are safety-relevant or have a live consumer, and all are produced and stored at the site: among them the AMS permissions and zones, the ADS stops, the VCS faults and alarms, the remote-stop log, the PRU positions, the control log, the radio recordings, the fire-system log, the incident record, the plant states, the FMS delay codes, the live dashboard and the shift report.
  • May be deferred. Everything that crosses to the enterprise, buffered at the site and sent when the link returns.

The rule (REQ-22-01-007): the enterprise link may delay a report; it may not stop the shift's view of itself or lose an incident's records.

4.7 The C01 join: plant, FMS and AMS records together

SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN gives the second case. C01 reports not ready at T+0 (blocked chute), the AMS withholds the tipping-bay reservation at T+5 s and holds trucks at C01-Q, and the FMS proposes a reassignment at T+300 s, which the controller approves. In the lab's shift the trigger is at 16,200 s (10:30) and the outage lasts 2700 s [A].

The production loss is visible in the FMS alone: 19 trucks have DLY-OD-10 intervals totalling 10.65 truck-hours, and the shift delivers 178,420 t against 184,140 t for the same seed without the outage, 5,720 t less. But the FMS record alone does not explain the loss; it only names a code. LAB-22-01-02 joins three products:

  • plant.states gives the not-ready interval, T+0 to T+2700 s;
  • ams.reservations shows the withheld reservation at T+5 s, the control-path reaction;
  • fms.delay_codes gives the 19 DLY-OD-10 intervals.

All 19 intervals overlap the plant's not-ready interval, so all 19 are explained by a plant record. Remove the plant product from the join and none is: the FMS would hold 10.65 truck-hours of "destination down" with no record of the destination being down. The plant record belongs to the plant, not to the AHS (ch-02-01 classifies the interface as external and production-critical), so the catalogue must name it and someone must own the agreement that it is kept. The model records the approved reassignment but does not apply it, so the 5,720 t is the loss of holding the fleet [A].

4.8 Trade-offs and limits of the model

  • Central or federated stores. One site store makes joins easy but is one more system to run and secure; federated stores keep ownership clear and make every join a project. The figure shows one store, the simpler teaching case.
  • The model. The synthetic log comes from one seeded fleet run, with registry key events placed exactly and every rate, size, latency and duration assumed [A]. The model does not include message loss on the wired site network, does not emit an OSS log or radio recordings (the catalogue declares them; the log does not contain them), and gives one mission ID per truck and shift. Completeness of a dashboard can be measured here only because the full log is known.

Nothing on the right-hand side of the table changes anything on the left: the analytics path only copies. At the end of the shift the site analytics computes the KPIs from these records, and the incident record is assembled from the preserved products (section 8.3).

6.2 Abnormal: DT17's clock is wrong (SCN-CLOCK-DT17)

In this case DT17's onboard time source failed at the start of the shift and its clock has drifted ever since (SCN-CLOCK-DT17, drift 0.002 s per second [A]). The control path has already dealt with it: per the registry, the AMS marked DT17's clock untrusted 1800 s after the onset and issues DT17's reservations with AMS-side expiry only. The fire happens later in the same shift.

The control path's behaviour is unchanged; the data is not. DT17's device clock is 64.8 s ahead at the fire, so its alarm carries event time T+64.8 s and arrives at ingest at T+0.35 s. Joined by event time, the alarm now follows the AMS zone (T+10 s) by 54.8 s, and a pairing rule that looks for the zone within 30 s after the alarm [A] fails. Sorted by event time, the AMS created a zone 55 s before any fire was reported.

The schema of section 4.4 catches it. A record ingested more than 2 s before its event time [A] is impossible, and the check flags the alarm. The offset estimate of ahs_sim.analytics.detect_clock_offset (the median of device time minus receive time per 1800 s window) flags DT17 with a maximum of 80.6 s over the shift; after correction the alarm's event time is T+0.85 s and it pairs with the zone 9.15 s later. The residual 0.85 s is the estimate's error and is kept with the corrected record.

The operational point: the AMS's own record that DT17's clock is untrusted is itself a control-path record the analytics path should read. A review must use corrected times for DT17, say so, and keep the raw times (REQ-22-01-004).

6.3 Degraded: the site-to-enterprise link is lost

In this case the link to the enterprise fails at T+120 s and returns at T+3720 s [A], during the fire. On the control path nothing changes: the AMS, the ADS and the independent safety function do not use the enterprise. On the site part of the analytics path nothing changes either: the site ingest and site store receive every record, the live dashboard and the shift report are computed at the site, and the AHS Controller and Pit Supervisor see what they saw before.

What changes is off site. The weekly report and condition monitoring do not receive the hour's records, and the mine's management sees no update. The site buffers the deferred products, about 6.0 MB for the hour at the assumed rate of 143.2 MB per day [A], and sends them when the link returns. The incident's records never needed the link: they are preserved at the site, where the hold is placed.

Had the live dashboard been computed in the enterprise, the Pit Supervisor would have lost it at T+120 s, during an incident.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

One-way copies, not shared state. The analytics path reads records copied from the control path; it does not read the control path's databases or call its interfaces. A copy can be late, but it cannot lock a table the AMS needs or load a process the ADS depends on. The cost is duplicated storage and a freshness gap, which the dashboard must show.

The catalogue as the contract. Each product's entry is the agreement between its producer and its consumers. A change to a schema, a rate or an owner is a change to the catalogue, checked by the same six checks as the original. The data lineage of ch-22-05 is computed from it.

Keys resolved at the ingest, with the method recorded. Carrying a mission ID in every VCS sample would cost bandwidth on R05 and R24; resolving it at the ingest by time is cheap but depends on clocks. Recording the method lets a review weigh each join.

Safety relevance as a catalogue attribute. The flag drives retention (safety-relevant products are kept for the whole horizon in the model of ch-22-04) and the incident checklist. It does not make a product part of the control path.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

The timeline of section 6.1 contains four kinds of latency, and a design must keep them apart.

  • Transport latency of onboard records: 0.1 to 0.5 s [A], up to the outage plus drain time during a CT3 outage (ch-22-02).
  • Site latency of wired records: 0.02 s [A].
  • Human entry latency of manual records: 60 s [A]. It is part of the process, which is why a delay interval's event time is its start, not its entry.
  • Sampling latency: a 300 s VCS summary [A] describes the truck up to 300 s ago.

The FMS–AMS boundary is the handbook's version of the interface that ISO 23725:2024 defines between fleet management systems and autonomous haulage systems, including messages and telemetry [F] (SRC-STD-ISO23725, title and scope only). This chapter does not assume anything about the standard's clauses; the event schema here is the handbook's convention [H].

Interfaces to the plant (ch-15-06) and to the manned-vehicle devices carry their own times. The site ingest stamps every record on arrival, so a common receive time exists even when producers' clocks do not agree; the site's time source and its synchronisation belong to the site network (Volume 11) and to ch-22-02.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

The AHS Controller's HMI shows the control path's state: DT17 stopped, the zone, the held trucks, the alarm and its acknowledgement. The Pit Supervisor may also have a dashboard on a second screen, computed on the analytics path. In the lab's fire shift, a dashboard tile built on the VCS summary samples shows DT17 "travelling loaded" until T+300.3 s, because the summary sample taken at T+0 s, as the alarm was raised, still shows the truck moving and the next one is taken at T+300 s [A]. A tile built on the ADS cycle states shows DT17 DOWN from T+5.4 s.

Both tiles are correct for what they read; neither is the control path. The rule for the control room is [H]: a live decision about a truck, a permission, a zone or a restart is taken on the HMI. A dashboard tile shows the age of its data, and a tile older than its refresh interval is shown as stale (ch-22-08). A dashboard that looks wrong is reported to the owner of the product it reads.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — securing the data of an incident (SCN-FIRE-DT17)

The procedure complements the preservation step of ch-16-06; it names the data products and who requests each [H]. Legal duties to preserve an incident site and its records, and legal retention periods for safety records, are [TBV] (SRC-REG-AU-INCIDENT-SITE-PRESERVATION, SRC-REG-AU-RECORD-RETENTION) and are not stated here.

  1. AHS Controller, at the acknowledgement (T+15 s): note the incident's start time and the units involved (DT17, the trucks held at R05) in the control log.
  2. AHS Controller, once the immediate response allows (in the model, after T+60 s): place a preservation hold on the window from 30 min [A] before the trigger, open-ended, for the units involved and for the AMS, FMS, control-log and radio products.
  3. AHS Controller: request the AMS incident-window export (ams.reservations, ams.permissions_zones) and the remote-stop log (isf.remote_stop_log), and confirm that the radio recordings of the window are kept (cr.radio_recordings).
  4. Maintenance Planner, with the Field Technician: before any reset, recharge or power cycle of DT17, download the vehicle logs (ads.stops, ads.cycle_states, vcs.faults_alarms) and the fire-system log (oss.fire_system_log); record who downloaded them and when.
  5. Pit Supervisor: confirm that the FMS assignment and delay records of the window are under the hold, because they are not safety-relevant in the catalogue and routine retention would otherwise apply.
  6. Pit Supervisor: confirm that the field-terminal and PRU records of the ERT's vehicles are kept (mvd.pru_positions, ams.permissions_zones).
  7. AHS Controller: check the catalogue's preservation list against what has been secured, and record any gap.
  8. Pit Supervisor: release the hold only on the investigator's instruction.

Tracing a delay on a shift report back to its sources (Operations objective 3) follows the lineage: the shift report tile "Operating Delay" comes from analytics.kpi_shift, which comes from analytics.time_usage, which comes from the FMS delay codes and the ADS cycle states; each DLY-OD-14 interval has a start (the AMS zone time), an entry (the controller's coding) and an end (the clearance). At each step one role owns the record: the Pit Supervisor the report and the FMS codes, the AHS Controller the AMS zone, the coding and the ADS records.

For "why did EX02 starve on night shift?" (Operations objective 1): the starvation is in the ADS cycle states of EX02's trucks and the FMS assignments (owner Pit Supervisor); the cause may be in the AMS reservations or zones (owner AHS Controller), in plant.states if C01 was down (owner Pit Supervisor, with the plant), or in the delay codes. The Pit Supervisor can request all of them; the AMS export goes through the AHS Controller.

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • AHS Controllers: the HMI governs live decisions; reading a dashboard's age; placing a preservation hold; which products to request at an incident; entering delay codes promptly and correctly.
  • Pit Supervisors: the difference between event time and receive time; reading a shift report back to its sources; owning the incident record and the hold.
  • Maintenance Planners and Field Technicians: downloading onboard logs before any reset; the fire-system log as a record of the mine.
  • Site engineering: maintaining and checking the catalogue; rejecting designs that cross the boundary.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-22-01-001 [H] Every data product used at the mine shall be recorded in the data catalogue with its producer, owning role, consumers, schema, rate class, safety relevance and, for a derived product, its inputs; a product shall be used only after it is recorded, and the catalogue checks (inputs have producers, products have consumers, consumers resolve, every architecture element produces a product) shall pass after every change. Rationale: undeclared products are lost at the first reset; the fire-system log was missing from the catalogue (sections 4.3, 1.1). Verification: test (LAB-22-01-01 catalogue checks); inspection of the catalogue's change record.

REQ-22-01-002 [H] No product of the analytics path shall be consumed by the AMS, the ADS, the VCS, an independent safety function, an onboard safety-related system or a manned-vehicle device, or be an input of a product they produce; an analytics product shall reach the FMS only through a person's recorded decision. Rationale: the control path's behaviour is argued in the safety case; the analytics path is not (sections 4.2, 4.3). Verification: test (LAB-22-01-01 separation check, including the wrong design); inspection of the architecture.

REQ-22-01-003 [H] Every record shall carry an event ID, its source, the asset ID, the event time by the producer's clock, the receive time by the site clock, a per-stream sequence number and the schema version; the mission ID and the map version shall be carried or resolvable from records in force at event time, and the method of resolution shall be recorded. Rationale: joins across FMS, AMS and VCS need common keys and two times (sections 4.4, 4.5). Verification: test (LAB-22-01-02 join report and incident rows); inspection of the schema.

REQ-22-01-004 [H] A record whose receive time precedes its event time by more than the plausibility tolerance shall be flagged, and the event times of the unit shall not be used to order or pair events in an incident record until they are corrected; both raw and corrected times shall be kept with the method of correction. Rationale: DT17's drifted clock places the fire alarm 54.8 s after the zone (section 6.2). Verification: test (LAB-22-01-02 pairing with SCN-CLOCK-DT17).

REQ-22-01-005 [H] Every dashboard tile shall show the age of the data it reads, and a tile older than its refresh interval shall be shown as stale; control-room procedures shall state that live decisions about trucks, permissions, zones and restarts are taken on the AHS Controller's HMI. Rationale: a tile on VCS summary samples showed DT17 travelling until T+300.3 s (section 8.1). Verification: test (LAB-22-01-02 dashboard lag); inspection of procedures; demonstration in the control room.

REQ-22-01-006 [H] At an incident, the AHS Controller shall place a preservation hold covering the products of the preservation list for the incident window before any routine retention, reset, recharge or power cycle can delete them; every item of the preservation list shall map to at least one catalogue product with an owner. Rationale: three products on the list are not safety-relevant and are kept only by a hold; two items had no product (sections 4.3, 8.3). Verification: test (LAB-22-01-01 preservation coverage); inspection of the incident procedure; drill.

REQ-22-01-007 [H] Every product that is safety-relevant or has a live consumer shall be produced and stored at the site or edge; no site product shall take an enterprise product as an input; the site shall buffer the deferred flows for a stated outage duration. Rationale: the enterprise link can be lost for hours; the shift and the incident records must not depend on it (sections 4.6, 6.3). Verification: test (LAB-22-01-01 link-outage analysis); demonstration with the link disconnected.

REQ-22-01-008 [H] An explanation of a production loss shall join the delay intervals with the records of their causes (for a destination stoppage: plant, FMS and AMS records); a delay interval that cannot be joined to a cause record shall be reported as unexplained and shall not be recoded automatically. Rationale: without the plant record, 19 DLY-OD-10 intervals and 10.65 truck-hours have no recorded cause (section 4.7). Verification: test (LAB-22-01-02 C01 join with and without the plant record).

10. Verification and validation

The catalogue is verified by its checks. LAB-22-01-01 runs the six checks on the module's catalogue, on the extended catalogue and on a deliberately wrong design; the tests pin the two gaps found, the closed extended catalogue and the single separation violation of the wrong design.

The join is verified against the registry. The tests check that the synthetic fire log carries the registry's key events at exactly T+0, 5, 10, 15, 60 and 600 s, that all of DT17's incident records resolve to M-20481 and cycle 20, and that every unmatched record belongs to a truck without a mission.

The abnormal case is verified both ways. With SCN-CLOCK-DT17, the pairing fails and the plausibility check fires; after correction, the pairing holds.

Validation at a site would compare a site's catalogue with the records actually found in its systems (unmapped records are the measure), and run the preservation procedure in a drill. The reference mine is fictional; nothing here is validated against a real operation.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Cataloguing systems instead of products. "The AMS database" has no single owner, rate or consumer.
  • One time per record. A fast clock and a slow network look the same.
  • Joining by time alone. It fails as soon as a clock drifts.
  • Letting an analytics output act on a truck. A predictive alert that restricts speed is a control function without a safety argument.
  • Putting a live dashboard in the enterprise. It disappears with the link, during an incident.
  • Leaving onboard safety-related systems out of the catalogue. Their logs are lost at the first recharge.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Acting on a dashboard tile. The HMI governs.
  • Reading a delay's entry time as its start. DT17's DLY-DN-01 starts at T+5 s, not T+65 s.
  • Resetting a truck before its logs are downloaded.
  • Forgetting that FMS records need a hold. They are not safety-relevant in the catalogue.
  • Explaining a production loss from the FMS codes alone. The code names a category; the cause is in another system.

15. Key takeaways

  • Catalogue data products, not systems: each product has one producer, one owning role, a schema, a rate and named consumers.
  • Check the catalogue. In the reference mine it found two missing products, the fire-system log and the radio recordings, that an incident review needs.
  • The analytics path observes the control path and never sits inside it: one-way copies in, people out, no path back.
  • Every record keeps two times. Event time says when; receive time says when it was known and catches a wrong clock.
  • Join by keys (mission, cycle, asset), not by time alone; record how each key was resolved.
  • Live decisions are taken on the HMI. A dashboard shows the age of its data.
  • Produce and store everything live or safety-relevant at the site. The enterprise link may delay a report; it may not stop the shift's view or lose an incident's records.
  • A production loss is explained by joining the delay codes with their causes: for C01, plant, FMS and AMS records.
