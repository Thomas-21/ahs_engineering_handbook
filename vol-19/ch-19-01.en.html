1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Volume 19 tells the deployment project behind today's reference mine: the conversion of this same mine from manned to autonomous haulage [A]. Before the project the mine ran as stage S0 of the transition what-if in equipment.yaml (all trucks driven); the project took it through S1 (waste circuit autonomous) to S2, which is today's baseline of 40 autonomous trucks (ch-17-05). The truck IDs did not change. Whether the trucks were converted by retrofit or replaced by autonomous-ready trucks is a project option [A], not a fact about the mine.

The scene is set years before today [A]. Site management has asked the project manager [A] for a feasibility study: "Could we run our haulage autonomously, and what would it give us?" On the whiteboard in the Pit Supervisor's office someone has written a productivity uplift taken from a sales brochure. Next to it the Pit Supervisor has pinned the delay report of the previous Tuesday's DAY shift. At 10:00 crusher C01 tripped on a blocked chute and stayed down for 45 minutes; fifteen loaded ore trucks queued at C01-Q, the drivers sat in their cabs, and the shift lost 6,600 t of ore. The Pit Supervisor taps the report: "Would your robots have hauled one more tonne on Tuesday morning?"

That question, and the brochure figure next to it, frame this chapter:

  • Which of the mine's losses would autonomy reduce, which would it leave unchanged, and which would it add?
  • How do you estimate the productivity of an operation that does not exist yet, from data you have today, without borrowing a number you cannot verify?
  • How many trucks would the mine need, and how sure can anyone be of that number?
  • What evidence does site leadership need at the feasibility gate to decide go or no-go, and who must agree?

This chapter covers:

  • the project lifecycle and its stage-gates, and the evidence each gate requires (FIG-19-01-01);
  • a feasibility screen that turns measured time usage into tonnes through a value tree (FIG-19-01-02), checks it against the handbook's fleet model, and estimates the truck requirement for a production target (FIG-19-01-03);
  • what autonomy does not address — crusher downtime, shovel practice, road condition — and why it must be reported separately;
  • the site constraints a feasibility study must carry forward to site readiness (ch-19-02), and the technical evidence package at the feasibility gate.

Full fleet sizing, costs and returns belong to Volume 21 (ch-21-01, ch-21-04, ch-21-05); this chapter stays at screening level.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Calculate baseline manned and candidate autonomous productivity for the ore circuit (EX02 → C01, mission M-20481) and the waste circuit (EX03 → D01) from cycle time, payload and time-usage inputs, tagging every input [A] or measured.
  2. Map each site constraint found in feasibility — marginal coverage on R05, R09–R11 and the west ramp R24, high GNSS multipath risk on R05, mixed-fleet density at EX02, the plant interface at C01 — to the architecture element it stresses and the volume that treats it.
  3. Specify the technical evidence package required at the feasibility gate: draft ConOps, fleet compatibility review, infrastructure gap list and interface inventory including the C01 plant interface.
  4. Estimate the screening-level truck requirement for a production target with and without autonomy, and its sensitivity to the time-usage assumptions.
  5. Explain how the choice and staging of autonomous areas (AA-North, AA-Central, AA-South) determines which interfaces must be ready first.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Identify which operational losses autonomy may reduce (shift change at 06:00 and 18:00, crib breaks, operator absence, inconsistent driving) and which it does not address (crusher C01 downtime, shovel availability, road condition).
  2. Assess readiness indicators in the current manned operation — delay-code discipline, road-maintenance standard, access-control culture — that predict a difficult ramp-up.
  3. Define the stage-gates of the deployment project and the evidence each gate requires.
  4. Prepare a go/no-go brief for site leadership that separates measured baseline data, [A] assumptions and open risks.
  5. Identify the stakeholders whose agreement is needed at the feasibility gate and the question each must answer.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

A feasibility study for autonomous haulage is usually started by a number. Someone has heard that autonomous fleets run more hours, or haul more per truck, and the number travels from a conference slide to a board paper. The handbook does not state any published industry uplift as fact: no such figure is registered and verified in source-register.yml, and a figure measured at another mine describes that mine's roads, shifts and plant, not this one [TBV]. The study therefore has to start where the mine is.

At the reference mine "where the mine is" has a precise meaning. One pit, North Pit, with four loading units: EX01 and EX02 load ore for crusher C01, EX03 and EX04 load waste for dump D01. Two ramps leave the pit: the main ramp R05, which is the shortest route from every shovel, and the west ramp R24–R25 that carries pit traffic when R05 is closed. Two 12-hour shifts, DAY from 06:00 and NIGHT from 18:00. Thirty-seven trucks produce on a typical DAY shift; two are in planned maintenance and one is not scheduled (equipment.yaml baseline_allocation). C01 has one tipping position and is the ore bottleneck (ch-01-05). Coverage on R05, R09–R11 and R24 is marginal, the main ramp has a high GNSS multipath risk, and the RL 330 bench lies below the crest wall.

Three things make the study harder than a spreadsheet:

  1. The baseline is the manned operation, and it is only as good as its delay codes. If shift changes are booked as "operating", if a crusher stoppage is not coded, if the queue at C01-Q is mixed into travel, the study compares an autonomous model with a manned operation that does not exist. The quality of the coding is itself a finding.
  2. The mine cannot stop to find out. Every measurement — cycle times, coverage, road geometry — is taken while manned production continues. The study must say which numbers are measured, over which period, and which are assumptions.
  3. The value is not where the brochure puts it. A driverless truck does not take crib breaks. Whether that turns into tonnes depends on the shovels, which stay manned, on the crusher, which does not care who drives, and on the new stops that autonomy brings (ch-01-02). The study has to follow time through these links, not multiply a fleet by a percentage.

The outcome of the study is not "autonomy is good" or "autonomy is bad". It is a gate decision — go to the definition phase, or no-go — supported by evidence that separates what is known from what is assumed, and by a list of site constraints that becomes the work of the next chapters.

3. Terminology and foundations

  • Opportunity assessment [H]: the first, short look at whether autonomous haulage could address a defined problem at the site; it ends with a decision to fund a feasibility study (G0).
  • Feasibility study: the study that establishes the measured baseline, estimates what autonomy would change, identifies the site constraints and prepares the evidence for the feasibility gate (G1).
  • Stage-gate [H]: a decision point between project phases at which a named decision-maker decides go, go with conditions, or no-go against criteria and evidence agreed in advance. This chapter defines the six gates G0 to G5 used throughout Volume 19, as a handbook convention [H]; G4 is the operational-readiness gate of ch-18-05 (the days G−n of ch-18-05 count towards that gate and are not gate numbers).
  • Business case: the case for the investment — benefits, costs, risks and options — prepared in the definition phase and decided at G2. Its commercial and economic content belongs to Volumes 20 and 21.
  • Go/no-go decision: the gate decision; at G4 it has three outcomes, go, conditional go and no-go (ch-18-05).
  • Baseline: the measured performance of the current operation over a stated period, with its time usage coded to the GMG categories (SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020 [F], as used in ch-01-05).
  • In-cycle time [H] (ch-01-02): productive time plus travel empty — the time a truck spends in its haul cycle. Loads are in-cycle time divided by cycle time.
  • Hot-seat change (ch-01-02): handing a machine to the next operator without stopping it. A relief operator [H] is the extra operator that hot-seat changes and covered breaks require.
  • Feasibility screen [H]: a deterministic estimate of tonnes and truck requirement from cycle times, per-truck time usage and bottleneck windows. It is checked against, but does not replace, a fleet model.
  • Truck requirement [H]: the number of producing trucks a production target needs; the fleet adds an allowance for trucks in maintenance or not scheduled.
  • Constraint map [H]: the list of recorded site constraints, each mapped to the architecture element it stresses (FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS, network) and the volume that treats it.
  • Interface inventory [H]: the list of every interface the autonomous system must have at the site — to the plant, the shovels, manned vehicles, the network, the control room — with its owner and its readiness.
  • Draft ConOps: the first version of the concept of operations (ch-03-01) — how the mine would operate with autonomy — written early enough to expose disagreements.

Three foundations from earlier volumes carry the chapter. The GMG time categories and KPIs (ch-01-05) say where every second of a truck's shift goes. The haul-cycle model (ch-01-01, ahs_sim.haul_cycle) turns roads and speeds into cycle times. The loop and bottleneck view (ch-01-05, ch-10-02) says that a circuit moves no more than its slowest server allows — and at this mine the slowest server on the ore side is C01.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The project lifecycle and its gates

FIG-19-01-01 shows the lifecycle the handbook uses for the conversion [H]. Each phase ends at a gate; each gate has a decision-maker, a question and evidence.

Gate Question Decision-maker [A] Evidence required [H]
G0 Is there a problem autonomy might address, worth a study? site management Problem statement; baseline data request; study team and sponsor
G1 Feasibility Proceed to definition? site management, on the project director's recommendation Measured baseline; feasibility screen with every input labelled; constraint map; draft ConOps; fleet compatibility review; infrastructure gap list; interface inventory; stakeholder positions
G2 Investment Fund the project? owner's investment process Business case (Volumes 20, 21); site-readiness plan (ch-19-02); engagement plan (ch-19-03); contract strategy (ch-19-04); workforce plan (ch-19-05)
G3 Ready to commission Start commissioning and area activation? site management, on the project director's recommendation First area's blocking readiness items signed off (ch-19-02); contracts and support in place (ch-19-04); engagement status (ch-19-03); training pipeline started (ch-19-05)
G4 Operational readiness Start autonomous production? site management (ch-18-05) Readiness review: go, conditional go, no-go
G5 Handover to steady state Hand over from project to operations? site management Performance test against the agreed commitment (ch-19-07)

The gates do two jobs. They stop the project spending money before the evidence for the next step exists, and they force the project to write down, at each step, what it has assumed. A screen that went into G1 with a 10 % uplift [A] and a list of conditions must come back at G2 with those conditions answered or still open — not silently replaced by a better number.

Between G2 and G5 the mine passes through the transition stages of ch-17-05; the first autonomous load lies between the go at G4 and G5. The handbook's assumption for the reference mine is that the waste circuit is converted first [A] (S1: EX03 and EX04 autonomous to D01), because the waste circuit avoids the crusher interface at the start and its trucks meet the crusher road (R02) only at Intersection X. The first autonomous load (ch-19-06) is therefore a waste load from EX03 to D01.

4.2 The baseline: what to measure in the manned operation

The baseline is the denominator of every claim the study makes, so it is measured, not assumed. For the reference mine the study team asks for [H]:

  • Cycle times per circuit from the FMS or from truck data: loaded travel, empty travel, spot, load, dump and the queues, over at least several weeks of both shifts [A]. The handbook's model gives the free cycle of each circuit (LAB-19-01-01 part A): EX01 → C01 1572.4 s, EX02 → C01 1564.1 s (mission M-20481, 4.50 km loaded), EX03 → D01 1914.7 s (6.55 km), EX04 → D01 1883.4 s. Measured cycles longer than these are where the study looks for queues and driver variation.
  • Time usage per truck coded to the GMG categories: Operating Standby (shift change DLY-SO-01, breaks DLY-SO-02), Operating Delay (fuelling DLY-OD-09, waiting for a shovel DLY-OD-11, crusher down DLY-OD-10), Non-Productive Time (queues DLY-NP-01 / -02, travel empty), Downtime.
  • Bottleneck behaviour: C01's ready / not-ready log, its tipping rate and its stoppages; the shovels' loading logs and their breaks.
  • Payload per load from the truck payload system, to check that the 220 t [A] used in the model is realistic.

The baseline also measures the quality of its own data. If 10 % [A] of scheduled truck time carries no code, the study cannot say where it went, and the autonomous operation — whose losses are almost all coded operating delay (ch-01-02) — will look worse than the manned one simply because its losses are visible. Uncoded time is reported as a finding and treated as a readiness indicator (section 4.11).

4.3 From time usage to tonnes: the value tree

The productivity of a truck follows from three quantities: how many hours it spends in its cycle, how long the cycle takes, and how much it carries. The handbook's per-truck relation (ch-01-02) is

Thc=Tsched−Tdown−TSB−TOD−TQ

with in-cycle time Thc, scheduled time Tsched, downtime Tdown, Operating Standby TSB, Operating Delay TOD and queues TQ. FIG-19-01-02 draws the relation as a value tree from the tonnes of a shift down to the time items, and marks each item by what the conversion does to it: removed or reduced, added, or unchanged.

LAB-19-01-01 takes the event types and durations of ch-01-02's LAB-01-02-01 [A] — a manned driver changeover of 2 × 15 min, two 30 min crib breaks, 15 min of fuelling and a 20 min queue allowance in every case, a 10 min shovel operator changeover, and 30 min of autonomy-specific stops (nuisance stops, holds for access permissions, a stop on link loss, a stop on localisation degradation) for autonomous trucks. It evaluates four operating cases, because the honest comparison is not "manned against autonomous" but four ways of running the same mine:

Case Description [A]
M0 Manned, as today: drivers stop for changeover and crib breaks; the shovels' breaks fall inside the trucks'
M1 Manned, trucks and shovels hot-seated: relief operators keep every machine running through changeovers and breaks
A0 Autonomous, shovel break practice unchanged: trucks wait at the shovel through its changeover and breaks
A1 Autonomous, shovels hot-seated: trucks wait only for the shovel operator changeover

One truck on M-20481 over a DAY shift (LAB-19-01-01 part B; M0, A0 and A1 reproduce LAB-01-02-01):

Per truck, EX02 → C01 M0 M1 A0 A1
Operating Standby (min) 90.0 0.0 0.0 0.0
Operating Delay (min) 15.0 25.0 115.0 55.0
Queue allowance (min) 20.0 20.0 20.0 20.0
In-cycle time (h) 9.917 11.25 9.75 10.75
Cycles of M-20481 22.83 25.89 22.44 24.74

Three readings matter for feasibility. First, the conversion removes 90 minutes of Operating Standby per truck but adds 30 minutes of autonomy-specific Operating Delay; the net gain of A1 over M0 is 0.83 h of in-cycle time per truck. Second, A0 gains nothing: if the shovel operators keep their breaks, the trucks wait at the shovel instead of in the crib room, and the autonomy-specific stops make A0 slightly worse than M0. The shovel practice is therefore part of the autonomy decision, not a detail for later. Third, M1 — a manned operation that hot-seats everything — has more in-cycle time than A1, because it has no autonomy-specific stops. The feasibility comparison that leaves M1 out compares autonomy with the worst manned option instead of the best.

4.4 From one truck to the fleet: the bottleneck screen

A fleet is not 37 copies of one truck. The trucks of a circuit share a shovel, and the ore circuits share C01. The screen therefore caps each loading unit's loads at what its shovel can serve in its window, and the two ore units together at what C01 can accept in its window:

Li=min(niThctc,i, Wts),Lore=min(LEX01+LEX02, WC01tC01)

with ni trucks on loading unit i, its free cycle tc,i, shovel service ts = 167 s (35 s spot, 132 s load), C01 service 90 s, and windows W that depend on the case: in M0 the circuit stops together for 90 minutes, so shovels and C01 serve trucks for 10.50 h; in M1 and A1 they lose only the 10 min changeover (11.83 h); in A0 they lose 70 minutes (10.83 h). When the in-cycle time equals the window this is the closed form of the deterministic loop (ahs_sim.queueing.deterministic_loop; the lab tests the equality).

With the baseline allocation of 37 producing trucks (EX01 10, EX02 9, EX03 9, EX04 9) the screen gives (LAB-19-01-01 part C):

Case Tonnes Ore Waste Against M0 Ore limited by Relief operator-hours per shift
M0 166,848.2 92,400.0 74,448.2 — C01 window (420 loads) 0.0
M1 188,591.4 104,133.3 84,458.0 +13.03 % C01 window 59.5
A0 166,739.8 93,542.8 73,197.0 −0.06 % trucks 0.0
A1 183,841.3 103,137.0 80,704.4 +10.18 % trucks 4.0

The table holds the chapter's main feasibility result. With these [A] inputs, converting the trucks and hot-seating the four shovels raises a DAY shift by 16,993 t, about 10 %. Converting the trucks without changing shovel practice raises nothing. A manned operation that bought the same hours with relief operators would move more — at the cost of 59.5 relief operator-hours per shift against 4.0 for the shovels alone. The value of the conversion in this screen is therefore not "more tonnes than any manned option"; it is roughly the tonnes of the best manned option without its labour, plus whatever consistency, safety and data benefits the business case can evidence. Those are not tonnes and are not in the screen.

The ore column shows the bottleneck at work. In M0 the trucks could bring more ore than C01 accepts in 10.50 h, so the crusher's window, not the truck count, sets ore tonnes. In A1 the window grows to 11.83 h and the ore trucks become the limit: the gain on the ore side is crusher hours made usable, not faster trucks.

4.5 Checking the screen against the fleet model

A screen is useful only if it agrees with a better model where both can run. The handbook's fleet model (ahs_sim.fms.simulate_fleet behind planning_ops.transition_stage, ch-17-05) simulates one DAY shift with dispatch, queues and start-up. Its manned stage S0 uses a different set of [A] inputs from ch-01-02: 30 minutes of shift-change parking and two 20-minute breaks taken by the whole circuit, and no fuelling, queue allowance or autonomy-specific delay. Run with exactly those inputs (LAB-19-01-01 part D), the screen gives:

Like-for-like Screen Fleet model Difference
S0 (manned) 176,663.3 t 173,140.0 t +2.03 %
S2 (autonomous) 195,688.6 t 191,180.0 t +2.36 %
S2 against S0 +10.77 % +10.42 % —

The screen is about two per cent optimistic in absolute tonnes — it has no start-up at the beginning of the shift, no partial loads at the end and no queueing beyond its allowance — but it reproduces the relative gain within half a percentage point. That is the property a feasibility screen needs: the ratio is what the decision rests on, and the screen's bias is known and stated.

4.6 The truck requirement for a production target

The second question of the brief is how many trucks the mine would need. The screen answers it by searching, per material, for the fewest producing trucks that meet a target, with the most even split between the two loading units on ties. The target is a planning assumption [A]: 180,000 t per DAY shift, split ore 97,146.1 t and waste 82,853.9 t by the baseline share of the fleet model.

Case Ore trucks (EX01 / EX02) Waste trucks (EX03 / EX04) Producing Fleet (× 40 / 37) [A]
M0 unreachable: ore ceiling 92,400.0 t 10 / 11 — —
M1 9 / 9 9 / 9 36 39
A0 unreachable: ore ceiling 95,333.3 t 10 / 11 — —
A1 9 / 9 9 / 10 37 40

FIG-19-01-03 shows the same search over targets from 120,000 to 200,000 t. Two results deserve attention. M0 and A0 cannot reach the target at any truck count: their C01 windows (10.50 h and 10.83 h) cap ore below 97,146.1 t, and more trucks only lengthen the queue at C01-Q. A screen that reported "M0 needs 45 trucks" would be wrong in a way that matters: the answer is "no number of trucks", and the lever is the crusher's hours. And where the target is reachable, A1 needs one producing truck more than M1, because its autonomy-specific stops cost in-cycle time that relief operators do not.

The fleet column multiplies by 40 / 37 [A], the ratio of the baseline fleet to its producing trucks. It is a placeholder for the availability and maintenance analysis of Volume 18 and the fleet sizing of ch-21-01; whether autonomous trucks would be more or less available than manned ones is not credited in either direction.

Two inputs dominate the gain over today's operation: how long the manned breaks really are, and how many autonomy-specific stops the converted mine will have. The break-even of the second is 89.6 minutes per truck per shift: above that, A1 hauls less than M0. Against M1 the break-even is zero: with any autonomy-specific delay at all, the hot-seated manned operation hauls more, unless manned operator absence or slower manned driving — the two items the screen deliberately does not credit in its base case — are real and measured. Those two are exactly the claims a brochure makes. The study's job is to measure them in the baseline, not to assume them.

4.8 What autonomy does not address

The Pit Supervisor's question in section 1.1 has a clear answer. A 45-minute C01 stoppage (SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN, 2,700 s as computed in ch-01-07) removes the same crusher hours in every case. With the screen's capped windows (LAB-19-01-01 part G):

Case Ore without stoppage Ore with stoppage Ore lost
M0 92,400.0 t 85,800.0 t 6,600.0 t
M1 104,133.3 t 97,533.3 t 6,600.0 t
A0 93,542.8 t 88,733.3 t 4,809.5 t
A1 103,137.0 t 97,533.3 t 5,603.7 t

Where C01 is the binding constraint (M0, M1) the stoppage costs its full 30 tipping slots, 6,600 t. Where the trucks are the limit (A0, A1) it costs less, because some of the lost crusher time was spare. In no case does autonomy remove the loss; it only changes how close the circuit runs to its bottleneck. The study reports these losses in a separate line [H], so that the business case does not net plant problems against haulage gains:

Shift change at 06:00 and 18:00 Removed for trucks No driver; the control-room handover does not stop trucks
Crib breaks Removed for trucks; kept for shovels unless hot-seated Shovels stay manned
Operator absence Removed for trucks Not credited in the base case; measure it
Inconsistent driving May be reduced Not credited; the ADS drives to its own profile

4.9 Site constraints and the architecture

The screen treats roads as times. The study must also treat them as constraints on the system that will drive them. LAB-19-01-01 part H reads the recorded attributes of mine.yaml and maps each to the architecture element it stresses and the volume that treats it [H]:

Constraint Where Architecture element Treated in
Marginal coverage R04, R05, R09, R10, R11, R19, R20, R24 Network, AMS (authority, reservations) Volume 11
Poor coverage R22 (dump tip area link) Network, AMS Volume 11
High GNSS multipath risk R05 ADS localisation Volume 05
Medium multipath risk R07, R08, R17, R18, R24 ADS localisation Volume 05
Grade 8.99 % above the 8 % wet ODD [A] R05 ADS / VCS (ODD, braking) Volumes 04, 07, 12

Manned shovel, dozer and water-truck work at the face EX02 (and every loading area) AMS (access permissions) Volume 14

Two observations follow. The main ramp carries three of the constraint kinds at once — coverage, multipath and grade — and it is the shortest route for every circuit; it will dominate site readiness (ch-19-02). And every constraint in the table is recorded with a class (good / marginal / poor; low / medium / high), not measured: at the feasibility gate the study can only say which roads need measurement, not whether they pass. One of those measurements, at the feasibility stage of this project, found that lower R05 and the RL 330 bench had no usable coverage from the crest tower at all; section 6.3 follows it.

4.10 The staging of areas decides which interfaces come first

Every production circuit of the reference mine starts in AA-North (the pit) and passes AA-Central (Intersection X); the waste circuits also end in AA-South. Autonomous production on a circuit is possible only when all areas on it are activated (ch-18-04). The handbook's activation order is AA-South, AA-Central, AA-North [A], and its first circuit is waste [A]. That choice decides which interfaces the study must show to be feasible first:

  • Access points. AP-03 (Y, dump access) is needed with AA-South; AP-01 (MIA gate) with AA-Central; AP-02 (P1, ramp top) with AA-North; AP-04 (P3, west ramp crest) as soon as the R05-closure route is required.
  • The C01 plant interface is needed only when the ore circuit converts. Starting with waste gives the project time to specify the ready / not-ready signal and the hold logic of SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN (ch-15-06) — but the interface inventory must contain it from G1, because its owner is the plant, not the haulage project.
  • Mixed-fleet zones. In S1 manned ore trucks and autonomous waste trucks share R01, R05, R06, X and Z (ch-17-05). The feasibility study must flag that this needs a mixed-traffic rule that the permission constructs of Volume 14 do not provide.

4.11 Readiness indicators in the manned operation

Some signs in the manned operation predict a hard ramp-up. They cost little to observe and belong in the gate brief [H]:

  • Delay-code discipline. Share of scheduled truck time with a code; whether crusher stoppages are coded DLY-OD-10 or hidden in queue time; whether shift changes are booked as standby. A site that cannot code its losses today will not be able to attribute them in a performance commitment (ch-19-07).
  • Road-maintenance standard. Frequency of grading and watering, windrow condition on both ramps, spillage on R05. An autonomous truck stops for what a driver would steer around; dust after a watering gap produces nuisance stops (SCN-WATER-WT02).
  • Access-control culture. Whether light vehicles call before entering the pit today, whether the radio protocol is kept, whether people walk in loading areas. Positive communication with digital access permissions (ch-14-01) formalises a discipline that must already exist in spirit.

Each indicator is measured, not judged: a sample of shift logs, a road inspection record, a week of radio-call observations [A].

4.12 The technical evidence package at the feasibility gate

The screen is one of several technical items the gate requires ([H], REQ-19-01-007):

  1. Draft ConOps (ch-03-01): areas, circuits, roles in CR1 and the field, how manned and autonomous traffic are separated in each stage, what happens when R05 closes.
  2. Fleet compatibility review: whether the existing trucks can be retrofitted (drive-by-wire interfaces, braking and steering actuation, onboard safety systems) or should be replaced by autonomous-ready trucks — a project option [A] whose technical criteria are in ch-08-07 (retrofit-readiness-assessment). The screen assumes the same truck class in both cases.
  3. Infrastructure gap list: roads, coverage, GNSS corrections, survey and map, access points, park-up, fuel bay — the seed of ch-19-02's readiness register.
  4. Interface inventory: plant (C01), shovels (truck–shovel interface, ch-13-03), manned vehicles (PRU, radio, field terminal), network, control room, maintenance systems; each with an owner and a readiness status.

4.13 Trade-offs and model limits

  • Screen against fleet model. The screen runs in a second and can be explained on a whiteboard; the fleet model includes queues and dispatch. The study uses the screen for many cases and the fleet model to check one.
  • Per-truck inputs from a teaching log. The inputs are those of LAB-01-02-01 [A]. A real study replaces them with the site's coded logs before G1.
  • What is not credited. Availability, maintenance, labour cost, safety exposure and data benefits are outside the screen. They belong to the business case, each with its own evidence.
  • One shift, one target. NIGHT shift, seasonal weather and mine-plan changes are not modelled.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — a baseline week as the study sees it

In normal operation the study observes the manned mine without disturbing it. Over a baseline period [A] the FMS logs every cycle, the Pit Supervisor's crews code every interruption, and the study team samples the radio channel, the road inspections and the access calls at AP-02. Nothing in this week tests autonomy; everything in it tests the baseline. A normal week ends with time usage per truck coded to the GMG categories, cycle times per circuit, the C01 ready / not-ready log, and a list of uncoded intervals to be explained.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

The study walks through one abnormal event twice: as it happened in the manned baseline (S0), and as it would happen after the conversion (S2). The registry gives T+0, T+5 and T+300 s; the restart at T+2700 s is the stoppage length of ch-01-07 [A]. T+0 s is 10:00:00 on a DAY shift [A].

Time Manned baseline (S0) After the conversion (S2) What the study records
T+0 s C01 PLC reports not ready (blocked chute); plant control calls the Pit Supervisor by radio [A] C01 not-ready signal reaches FMS and AMS Time stamp source of the not-ready signal; who is told
T+5 s Pit Supervisor radios ore drivers to hold at C01-Q [A] AMS withholds the tipping-bay reservation; trucks held at C01-Q Whether a hold is coded DLY-OD-10 from the first held truck
T+300 s FMS proposes reassignment; Pit Supervisor approves [A] FMS proposes reassignment; AHS Controller approves Decision time and who made it
T+2700 s [A] C01 ready; drivers released by radio C01 ready; tipping bay granted Stoppage length; queue drain

The study learns two things from this walkthrough. The loss is the same in both columns: 6,600 t of ore where C01 is the bottleneck (section 4.8). And the autonomous column has machine time stamps at every step, which the manned column lacks. Whether the baseline log shows T+5 s, or only "crusher down 10:00–10:45", decides whether the study can measure the crusher's real effect on haulage.

6.3 Degraded operation — SCN-COMMS-R05 as a feasibility finding

A degraded case in a feasibility study is a constraint discovered before anything is built. During the study the network team drove the haul roads with a light vehicle and a receiver at truck-antenna height [A] (the method of ch-11-04) from the only pit comms site then planned, the crest tower CT2. On lower R05 and on the RL 330 bench (R09, R10, R11 and the EX03 and EX04 loading links) the crest wall shadowed the signal; the readiness register of ch-19-02 reproduces the gap as 1.98 km of road with no serving site.

SCN-COMMS-R05 shows what that gap would mean in operation. Its timeline is that of a pit-floor site losing power:

Time Event (registry) Meaning for the feasibility study
T+0 s Comms site serving lower R05 and the bench down Without any pit-floor site, this is the permanent state of those roads
T+3 s Trucks detect link loss and stop within their granted path No autonomous traffic could pass lower R05 or reach EX03 and EX04
T+10 s AMS marks the trucks communication-lost; reservations frozen Both pit exits pass through the gap: no exit keeps AMS authority
T+900 s Site restored; reconciliation Every outage costs reconciliation time and coded delay (DLY-OD-05)

The finding became a cost and schedule item: a mobile comms trailer on the pit floor, CT3, with a radio backhaul to CT2 and its own generator. That is how the reference mine got the CT3 it has today [A]; ch-19-02 follows it through site readiness. The feasibility lesson is general: a constraint that is recorded only as "marginal" may hide a road that cannot be served at all, and the study must find it before the business case is written.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

  • Credit only what changes measurably. The screen credits the conversion with the standby it removes and charges it with the delay it adds. Operator absence and driving consistency are left out of the base case because the baseline must measure them first [R].
  • Compare with the best manned alternative. M1 is in the screen because a hot-seated manned operation is the alternative a sceptical board will ask about. Leaving it out would overstate the tonnes benefit by the difference between 13.03 % and 10.18 %.
  • Shovel practice is a design input. A0 shows that the trucks' gain is lost if the shovels keep their breaks. The ConOps must state the shovel practice before the screen is believed.
  • Bottleneck first. The ore side of the mine is decided by C01's window, not by truck count. The screen reports the binding constraint per circuit and refuses to convert an unreachable target into a truck number (REQ-19-01-003).
  • Retrofit or new [A]: the screen uses the same truck class for both; the choice affects cost, schedule and availability, not cycle time, at screening level.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN is a timing problem for the study as much as a production problem. At T+0 s the not-ready signal comes from the plant; in the manned baseline it reaches haulage by radio, in the converted mine by the plant interface to FMS and AMS. The study needs the plant's own time stamps for T+0 s and the haulage system's for T+5 s and T+300 s, on one clock, to separate the crusher's downtime from the haulage response. If the plant log and the FMS log disagree by minutes, the interface inventory gets a time-synchronisation item. The same applies to every interface in the inventory: who sends what, how fast, with which time stamp, and what the receiver does when it does not arrive. For the converted mine those answers become interface requirements (ch-03-02, ch-15-03); at feasibility they are questions with owners.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

In the manned baseline the "control room" is the dispatch screen of the FMS and the radio. During SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN the Pit Supervisor sees the ore trucks' states change to "waiting at crusher" if the drivers press the right button, and a crusher status only if the plant interface already exists [A]. After the conversion the AHS Controller in CR1 sees a C01 not-ready alarm at T+0 s, the held trucks at C01-Q from T+5 s and the FMS reassignment proposal at T+300 s (ch-01-07, ch-15-06). For the study the difference is evidence: the feasibility team asks the Pit Supervisor to show them, on Tuesday's log, exactly what the manned system recorded at each row of the timeline in section 6.2.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — preparing the go/no-go brief for G1

  1. Project manager [A]: fix the baseline period (both shifts, several weeks [A]) and list the data sources.
  2. Pit Supervisor: export the coded shift logs; report the coded share of scheduled truck time and the uncoded intervals; mark known coding gaps (e.g. crusher stoppages not coded DLY-OD-10).
  3. Project manager [A]: run the screen with the measured inputs; label every input measured (source, period) or [A]; run the like-for-like check against the fleet model; record the difference.
  4. Project manager [A]: run the sensitivity; list the inputs whose plausible range changes the decision.
  5. Survey with the network team: drive-test the roads flagged in the constraint map, both ramps first; record unserved spans.
  6. Maintenance Planner: complete the fleet compatibility review (retrofit or autonomous-ready [A]).
  7. Project manager [A]: write the brief in three parts — measured, assumed, open risks — with the stakeholder positions of section 5.
  8. Project director [A]: recommend go or no-go; site management [A]: decide and record the conditions.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

The study reads, and later the project changes, these codes and KPIs:

  • DLY-SO-01 and DLY-SO-02 (Operating Standby): the losses the conversion removes from trucks. Their measured size is the largest single input of the screen.
  • DLY-OD-11 (loading unit not loading): where the shovels' breaks reappear for autonomous trucks unless shovels are hot-seated.
  • DLY-OD-03, -04, -05, -06: the autonomy-specific delays added; they do not exist in the manned baseline and must be estimated [A].
  • DLY-OD-10 (destination down): C01 stoppages, unchanged by autonomy; reported separately.
  • Operator absence (manned trucks): a scheduled manned truck parked because no operator is available gets no delay code — manned trucks are outside the autonomous-truck time classification; the baseline counts the truck-shifts lost instead (handbook coding rule, coding_rules in delay-codes.yml). The screen's absence sensitivity uses those lost truck-shifts as fewer crewed trucks.
  • KPIs: Use of Availability rises (no standby), Operating Efficiency falls (losses become Operating Delay); Effective Utilization and in-cycle time tell the story (ch-01-02). A study that reports only Use of Availability overstates the gain.

8.5 Training and competency implications

The feasibility team needs people who can read a GMG time-usage report, run and explain a screen, and keep measured and assumed numbers apart in writing. The Pit Supervisor and crew leaders need a short brief on delay-code discipline before the baseline period, because the baseline is their data. Site leadership needs to be able to read the brief's three parts and ask which assumption decides. No one needs AHS operating competencies at this stage; the training pipeline for CR1 starts after G2 (ch-19-05).

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-19-01-001 [H] A feasibility productivity estimate shall label every input as measured, with its source and period, or as an assumption [A] with a stated range, and shall not use an industry productivity figure unless it is registered with status verified in the source register and used within its verification scope. Rationale: an estimate is only as credible as its least visible input (sections 2, 4.2). Verification: inspection of the screen inputs and the source register.

REQ-19-01-002 [H] The feasibility comparison shall include at least the current manned practice and the best manned alternative considered feasible by site leadership (for example hot-seat changes with relief operators), and shall report the autonomous case against both. Rationale: comparison with the worst manned case overstates the benefit (sections 4.3, 4.4). Verification: inspection; analysis (LAB-19-01-01 part C).

REQ-19-01-003 [H] The screen shall check every bottleneck on each circuit (loading units, crusher C01, dump D01), report the binding constraint per circuit, and report a target above a bottleneck ceiling as unreachable, not as a truck number. Rationale: more trucks behind a capped crusher only lengthen the queue (sections 4.4, 4.6). Verification: test (LAB-19-01-01 parts C and E).

REQ-19-01-004 [H] The screen shall be run on at least one like-for-like case against a fleet model, and the difference in tonnes and in relative gain shall be stated in the gate brief; a difference above 5 % [A] in the relative gain shall be explained before G1. Rationale: a screen's bias must be known before its ratio is trusted (section 4.5). Verification: analysis (LAB-19-01-01 part D).

REQ-19-01-005 [H] The gate brief shall contain a one-at-a-time sensitivity on every time-usage input and the break-even of autonomy-specific delay against each manned case, and shall name the inputs whose plausible range changes the decision. Rationale: the decision rests on assumptions; the brief must say which (section 4.7). Verification: inspection; analysis (LAB-19-01-01 part F).

REQ-19-01-006 [H] Every site constraint recorded for the haul roads, the comms sites, the plant interface and the mixed-fleet zones shall be mapped to the architecture element it stresses and carried as an item into the site-readiness gap register. Rationale: constraints found at feasibility are the readiness work of the project (sections 4.9, 6.3; ch-19-02). Verification: inspection of the constraint map against the readiness register.

REQ-19-01-007 [H] The feasibility gate G1 shall not be decided without a draft ConOps, a fleet compatibility review, an infrastructure gap list and an interface inventory that includes the C01 plant interface, each with an owner. Rationale: the screen alone does not show that the system can be built and run at the site (section 4.12). Verification: inspection of the gate record.

REQ-19-01-008 [H] Losses that the conversion does not address — crusher downtime, shovel availability, road condition and weather — shall be reported in a separate line of the brief and shall not be netted against the haulage gain. Rationale: plant problems are neither solved nor caused by autonomy (section 4.8). Verification: inspection; analysis (LAB-19-01-01 part G).

REQ-19-01-009 [H] The baseline shall report the share of scheduled truck time that carries a delay code, by shift and by circuit, and a coded share below 95 % [A] shall be recorded as a readiness risk with an action before G1. Rationale: uncoded time makes the baseline and any later performance commitment unverifiable (sections 4.2, 4.11). Verification: analysis of the baseline logs; inspection.

10. Verification and validation

  • Verification of the screen: LAB-19-01-01 reproduces the per-truck results of LAB-01-02-01 for M0, A0 and A1, equals queueing.deterministic_loop where both apply, and is checked like-for-like against planning_ops.transition_stage (12 automated tests).
  • Validation of the screen requires site data: the measured baseline replaces the [A] inputs, and after conversion the ramp-up data of ch-19-07 show whether the screen's relative gain was right. The project keeps the screen and its inputs under configuration control from G1 so that the comparison can be made.
  • Verification of the evidence package: the gate record is inspected for each item of REQ-19-01-007 and for the labels of REQ-19-01-001.
  • Validation of the readiness indicators: whether sites with poor delay-code discipline in fact had harder ramp-ups is a question for lessons learned (ch-19-08); the chapter asserts no such relation, the indicators are handbook conventions [H].

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Multiplying the fleet by a published uplift instead of following time through the value tree.
  • Leaving the shovels out: assuming the trucks' removed standby becomes in-cycle time while the shovels keep their breaks (case A0).
  • Ignoring C01: computing a truck requirement for an ore target that the crusher's window cannot accept.
  • Comparing only with today's manned practice and not with the hot-seated alternative.
  • Treating a recorded "marginal" coverage class as a measurement.
  • Choosing retrofit or new trucks in the screen; at screening level the choice changes cost and schedule, not cycle times.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Starting the baseline without checking how crews code delays; finding out at G1 that crusher stoppages were never coded.
  • Letting the brochure number reach the board paper before the brief does.
  • Inviting workforce representatives after the decision rather than before it.
  • Presenting the brief as one number instead of measured, assumed and open.
  • Forgetting that the plant owns C01: an interface the haulage project needs from someone else is a risk until that owner has agreed.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — In-cycle time of one truck, M0 and A1. Scheduled 12 h. M0: Operating Standby 1.5 h (changeover 2 × 15 min, crib breaks 2 × 30 min), Operating Delay 0.25 h (fuelling), queue allowance 0.333 h. Thc = 12 − 1.5 − 0.25 − 0.333 = 9.917 h; with the 1564.1 s cycle of M-20481 that is 22.83 cycles. A1: Operating Standby 0, Operating Delay 0.917 h (fuelling 0.25 h, shovel changeover 0.167 h, autonomy-specific 0.5 h), queues 0.333 h. Thc = 12 − 0.917 − 0.333 = 10.75 h, 24.74 cycles. The difference, 0.833 h, is 1.91 cycles per truck per shift [A].

Example 2 — Why M0 cannot reach 180,000 t. In M0 the circuit stops together for 90 min, so C01 accepts trucks for 12 − 1.5 = 10.50 h = 37,800 s. At 90 s per truck that is 420 loads, 420 × 220 = 92,400 t of ore. The target's ore share is 97,146.1 t. No number of ore trucks closes the 4,746.1 t gap; only more C01 hours (hot-seat changes, staggered breaks) or a second tipping position could.

Example 3 — Waste trucks for A1. The A1 truck has 10.75 h = 38,700 s in its cycle. On EX03 (1914.7 s) that is 20.21 loads, on EX04 (1883.4 s) 20.55. The waste target, 82,853.9 t, is 376.6 loads. Nine trucks on each unit give 9 × 20.21 + 9 × 20.55 = 366.8 loads — short. Nine on EX03 and ten on EX04 give 387.4 loads, enough; the shovel windows (11.83 h / 167 s = 255.1 loads each) do not bind. Waste needs 19 trucks, ore 18, total 37 producing — the lab's result.

Example 4 — The C01 stoppage in A1. Without the stoppage A1 brings 468.8 ore loads against a C01 cap of 473.3. A 2,700 s stoppage lowers the cap by 30 loads to 443.3; ore falls by 25.5 loads, 5,603.7 t. In M0, where the cap already binds, the full 30 loads (6,600 t) are lost.

15. Key takeaways

  • The deployment project of Volume 19 is the conversion of the reference mine itself [A]; the feasibility study looks at stage S0 and estimates S2.
  • No published industry uplift is used; the study follows the mine's own time usage through a value tree to tonnes.
  • With the handbook's [A] inputs the conversion with hot-seated shovels gains 10.18 % over today's manned practice with the same 37 trucks; without changing shovel practice it gains nothing; a hot-seated manned operation would gain 13.03 % at the cost of 59.5 relief operator-hours per shift.
  • C01 sets the ore ceiling. A target above a bottleneck ceiling is unreachable, not "more trucks".
  • The decision rests on two inputs — the real size of the manned breaks and the number of autonomy-specific stops — and the brief must say so.
  • Plant losses (a 45-minute C01 stoppage costs 5,603.7 t to 6,600 t) are unchanged by autonomy and reported separately.
  • Feasibility finds constraints: the coverage gap on lower R05 and the RL 330 bench became the pit-floor trailer CT3.
  • The feasibility gate needs a draft ConOps, a fleet compatibility review, an infrastructure gap list and an interface inventory — and the agreement of the stakeholders who own the answers.
