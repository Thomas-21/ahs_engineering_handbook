1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is 14:10 on a hot, dry DAY shift. Water truck WT02 has just watered R03. An empty autonomous truck comes through the residual spray and stops for a detected obstacle (SCN-WATER-WT02). At T+30 s the AHS Controller has classified the stop as a nuisance from the camera snapshot; at T+45 s the truck moves on. It is the third such stop on R03 since lunch. The stop is coded DLY-OD-03, and so are the two trucks held behind it.

That afternoon a product manager from the AHS supplier visits. In the morning site management asked for "a lower cost per tonne and more predictable shifts". In the control room the controller says: "Fewer stops for nothing. And when I hand over at 18:00, I want the open items to come from the system, not from my head." In the workshop the Maintenance Planner says that the truck which stopped on R03 last week waited longer for a technician than for its repair. Each is a real need, in its owner's words, at its owner's level. None is a requirement yet.

The questions this chapter addresses are:

  • Which needs does the reference mine actually have, at the executive, site and shift levels, and how are they found?
  • How is a need written so that it can be traced to requirements, tested, and read back to the person who stated it?
  • How are needs connected to the numbers the business uses — tonnes per shift and cost per tonne?
  • How does a qualitative need such as "fewer nuisance stops" become an acceptance criterion with a metric, a measurement window and a data source?
  • How do delay records separate a need caused by system behaviour from one caused by site practice?
  • Which needs must never be traded against value?

This chapter covers:

  • the three levels of need and the discovery work that finds them (FIG-20-02-02);
  • the need statement and a chapter-local need register traced to the handbook's requirements;
  • a value driver tree from truck availability, nuisance stops and queue time to tonnes and cost per tonne (FIG-20-02-01), and its sensitivities (FIG-20-02-03, LAB-20-02-01);
  • acceptance criteria for qualitative needs, and the use of delay-code evidence to prioritise them;
  • the rule that safety-related needs are gates, not values.

The customer throughout is the reference mine [A]. Supplier-side roles such as the product manager are functions [A]. All money is in placeholder currency units "cu" [A]. No market size, price, vendor or vendor capability is stated.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Translate customer needs at the reference mine into measurable stakeholder needs and system requirements with traceability to requirement IDs.
  2. Build a value driver tree that links technical parameters (truck availability, nuisance-stop rate, queue time, waits at Intersection X) to tonnes moved and cost per tonne.
  3. Specify acceptance criteria for a qualitative need such as "fewer nuisance stops" as a requirement with metric, measurement window and data source.
  4. Analyse logged delay and event data to separate needs caused by system behaviour from needs caused by site practice.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Conduct structured discovery interviews with the reference mine's roles (AHS Controller, Pit Supervisor, Shovel Operator, Maintenance Planner, Field Technician) and synthesise their pain points.
  2. Distinguish executive needs (cost per tonne, safety performance, predictability) from shift-level needs (fewer nuisance stops, clean handovers, fast recovery of stopped trucks).
  3. Prioritise needs for the reference mine using delay-code evidence rather than anecdote.
  4. Write need statements in the language of each role and validate them back with those roles before they enter the backlog.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Customer needs arrive at different levels, in different languages and with different amounts of evidence behind them. Site management speaks in outcomes: cost per tonne, tonnes against plan, safety performance. These say nothing about what to change. The shift speaks in events: a stop on R03 after watering, a handover that missed a permission, a truck that waited fifteen minutes for a technician. Each is small, and a supplier who hears them as anecdotes may never add them up. The Pit Supervisor and the Maintenance Planner speak in between, about patterns over days.

Three things go wrong when these levels are not connected.

The executive need is accepted without a mechanism. "Lower cost per tonne" becomes a slide in a proposal. Nobody can say which technical parameter would move it, so nobody can test whether a product change helped.

The loudest shift need wins. A controller who lost an hour to an AMS failover argues strongly for failover improvements. The delay records may show that failovers cost a few truck-hours a week and nuisance stops a hundred.

The need is met in the wrong way. One way to get "fewer nuisance stops" is to make perception less sensitive. That also raises missed detections (ch-06-07). The need must be met without touching the safety function.

The reference mine has the data to avoid all three. Its delay codes follow the GMG time categories (delay-codes.yml, SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020 [F]). Nuisance stops are recorded with location, classification and minutes since watering (REQ-14-05-007), attended stops by phase (REQ-16-05-009), and the handover list is generated from system state (REQ-16-02-001). This chapter shows how a supplier and the site use these records to find, write, value and prioritise needs.

3. Terminology and foundations

Customer need [H]. A need of the organisation that buys and runs the AHS, stated by one of its people in a stated situation. At the reference mine it belongs to one of the seven mine.yaml roles, to site management, or to another stakeholder on the map of ch-03-01. It is an operational need in the sense of ch-03-01: what someone must be able to do, or be certain of, in their words and independent of design.

Need statement [H]. The structured record of one need. It extends the format of ch-03-01 with three fields: the level, the delay-code evidence, and the validation record (read-back). This chapter's needs carry chapter-local IDs NEED-20-02-nn; they are illustrative and not registered handbook IDs.

Levels of need [H]. Executive needs concern the outcomes on which the mine is judged (cost per tonne, safety performance, predictability of tonnes). Site needs concern patterns over days and the decisions of the Pit Supervisor and Maintenance Planner. Shift needs concern events inside a shift and the work of the AHS Controller, Shovel Operator and Field Technician.

Discovery interview [H]. A structured conversation with one role about one recent situation, aimed at what the person did, saw and needed, not at what product they want.

Pain point [H]. A recurring difficulty reported in an interview. "R03 is a nightmare after lunch" is a pain point; "when stops follow watering, I see them per road and per time since watering" is a need.

Value driver tree [H]. A tree that decomposes an output metric (here cost per tonne) into the quantities that produce it, down to drivers that a technical or operational change can move. Each edge is a formula.

Measurement window [H]. The period, population and data source over which an acceptance criterion is measured, for example 10 DAY and NIGHT shifts, all autonomous trucks, AMS stop records.

Physical availability, use of availability and the other KPIs are those of ch-01-05 (GMG [F]). Validation of a need — reading it back to the role that gave it — happens before any requirement is written; validation of the system (ch-03-01) happens after.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 Needs at three levels

FIG-20-02-02 shows the twelve needs of this chapter's example by level. Shift needs roll up into site patterns, and site patterns into executive outcomes — if, and only if, a model connects them.

The figure makes three points. Most executive needs have a mechanism below them: cost per tonne (NEED-20-02-01) and tonnes within the plan band (NEED-20-02-03) are moved by availability, nuisance stops and queue time, through the value tree of section 4.4. One executive need has no place in the tree: safety performance (NEED-20-02-02, red dashed border) is a gate (section 4.9). Some shift needs have no tonnes value: the handover (NEED-20-02-08) and a second controller in a busy hour (NEED-20-02-11) are about workload and risk. They stay in the register with their own measures; they are not dropped because they cannot be priced.

4.2 Discovery at the reference mine

Discovery uses the five sources of ch-03-01: observation, interviews and workshops, scenario walkthroughs, records, and constraints from other chapters. Supplier interviews have a known bias — the interviewee tells the supplier what they think the supplier can sell — so this chapter adds a discipline for the interview itself.

Plan by role and shift [R]. At least the AHS Controller, Pit Supervisor, Shovel Operator, Maintenance Planner and Field Technician, on DAY and NIGHT shifts. A NIGHT controller works with fewer people around; their pain points differ. Site management and the plant (C01) are interviewed separately.

Ask about one recent situation [H]. "Tell me about the last time a truck stopped on R03" produces facts. "What would you like the system to do?" produces wishes and design solutions. The interview guide has six prompts:

  1. Walk me through the last time this happened. What time was it, and where?
  2. What did you see on the screen, or hear on the radio, at that moment?
  3. What did you decide, and what did you need to know to decide it?
  4. Who else was involved? Who did you wait for?
  5. How was it recorded — which delay code, which log?
  6. If this had gone well, what would have been different, and how would you know?

The sixth prompt produces the measure.

Synthesise, then read back [H]. Pain points are grouped by situation (a registered scenario where one exists), then by role, and each group becomes draft need statements. A pain point that the records contradict is kept and marked. The draft is then read back to the role in their language — "so what you need is: when a stop follows watering, you see it per road and per time since watering — is that right?" — and the reply goes into the validation record. Only a validated need enters the product backlog (REQ-20-02-007). Read-back catches a need that the supplier wrote in its own words and then built.

A supplier must not interview only the people who signed the purchase, and a product description is a source of design solutions, not of needs (ch-03-01).

4.4 The value driver tree

FIG-20-02-01 shows the value driver tree of LAB-20-02-01 for one 12 h shift of the reference mine. Cost per tonne is the root. Shift cost and tonnes per shift are its two branches. Each node shows its value for the illustrative operating case described below.

The tree is built by ahs_sim.economics.value_tree [H]. Its time branch follows the GMG categories of ch-01-05. With N trucks, shift length T, physical availability PA and use of availability UoA:

Hcal=NT,Havail=PAHcal,Hop=UoAHavail=PA·UoA·NT

Nuisance stops (DLY-OD-03, Operating Delay) remove s stops per truck and shift of m minutes each from operating time:

Heff=Hop−Nsm60

The effective truck-hours are then expressed as an equivalent number of trucks working the whole shift, Neq=Heff/T. Tonnes come from the handbook's fleet model (fms.simulate_fleet, deterministic, baseline allocation scaled to the truck count), interpolated linearly between the two whole fleets around Neq. Queueing at the loading units and destinations therefore comes from the fleet model: fewer equivalent trucks queue less. If a value of queue time per cycle at the loading units is given, it replaces the model's value and every cycle lengthens or shortens by the difference. Tonnes never exceed the loading units' capacity.

The cost branch has three terms [A]: a fixed site cost per shift, a cost per operating truck-hour, and a cost per nuisance stop handled (the controller's intervention and the record):

C=Cfix+chHop+csNs,cost per tonne=Ctonnes

Two cases [A]. The default drivers of ValueDrivers are an idealised shift: 37 producing trucks (the equipment.yaml baseline allocation), 12 h, no Downtime, no Standby and no nuisance stops. With them the tree reproduces the baseline DAY shift of the fleet model exactly: 191,180 t, 869 loads, 25.8 s of queue per cycle at the loading units, and a cost per tonne of 1.191 cu/t. Nobody operates that shift; it is a calibration point. TYPICAL_DRIVERS is the illustrative operating case used everywhere else in this chapter: physical availability 0.90, use of availability 0.90, three nuisance stops per truck and shift of 5 minutes each, with the costs of 50,000 cu per shift, 400 cu per operating truck-hour and 30 cu per stop. It is an assumption for teaching, not a measurement of the reference mine or of any real mine.

For the operating case the tree gives:

The operating case moves 32,521 t less than the idealised shift. The queue per cycle at the loading units falls from 25.8 s to 11.4 s: with fewer effective trucks the shovels wait more and the trucks wait less.

The tree treats lost truck-time as fewer trucks for the whole shift. A stop on R03 can hold the whole waste circuit; a stop on a crusher-limited ore circuit may cost almost nothing. The tree gives a fleet-average, first-order value (worked example 5).

4.5 Two availabilities that multiply

In the tree, physical availability and use of availability have exactly the same effect on tonnes and on cost per tonne; their bars in FIG-20-02-03 are identical. The reason is the formula of section 4.4: operating time is PA·UoA·NT, and the tree uses only the product. Raising physical availability from 0.90 to 0.95 at use of availability 0.90 gives the same operating time as the reverse: 379.62 truck-h in both cases, 167,423 t and 1.226 cu/t (LAB-20-02-01). The cost per operating truck-hour also applies to the product, so the cost branch cannot tell them apart either.

The two cases are not the same operation. With physical availability raised, the shift has 22.2 truck-h of Downtime and 42.18 truck-h of Standby; with use of availability raised, 44.4 and 19.98. What the tree does not see:

  • Owners. Downtime is maintenance's number; Standby comes from operating decisions and external causes (ch-01-05).
  • Levers. Physical availability is moved by reliability, diagnosis and recovery time, partly the supplier's product. Use of availability is moved by shift changes, weather and site-wide outages, mostly the site's practice.
  • Costs. A truck in Downtime costs repair labour and parts; a truck in Standby may cost little. The tree's single cost per operating hour ignores this [A].
  • Time structure. Downtime comes in long blocks for single trucks; Standby often affects many trucks at once.

When a customer says "we need higher availability", the first question is which one. The tree says both are worth the same per point; the delay records say which one is lost, and who can recover it (REQ-20-02-008).

The two panels rank the drivers differently, and the difference is the lesson.

For tonnes, the truck count and the two availabilities dominate, then nuisance stops, queue time and minutes per stop.

For cost per tonne, the cost inputs dominate, and among the operating drivers nuisance stops come first, ahead of the availabilities. The reason is in the cost formula. More operating hours add tonnes and cost together, so higher availability lowers cost per tonne only through the fixed cost, which is spread over more tonnes. A nuisance stop adds cost (the handling charge) and removes tonnes without removing operating hours: it worsens both terms of the ratio.

A customer who says "cost per tonne" and one who says "tonnes" ask for different priorities with the same model. Show both panels and ask which outcome the mine is judged on. The ranges are illustrative; before a ranking is used, they are replaced by what the site's records show (section 4.8).

4.7 From a qualitative need to an acceptance criterion

"Fewer nuisance stops" cannot be tested as written. As an acceptance criterion it needs eight elements [H]:

  1. Metric — what is counted, and per what. Nuisance stops per 100 km driven by autonomous trucks (the nuisance-stop rate of ch-06-07), and separately the operating time lost per stop.
  2. Definition — what counts as a nuisance stop: a perception stop classified as a nuisance by the AHS Controller on recorded evidence (REQ-16-07-007), coded DLY-OD-03. Unresolved stops are counted separately; they are not nuisances.
  3. Population — all autonomous trucks in AA-Central and AA-North, all segments.
  4. Measurement window — for example 10 consecutive shifts, DAY and NIGHT, before and after the change [A].
  5. Data source — AMS stop records joined with the WT02 watering log (REQ-14-05-001) and the delay records.
  6. Baseline and target — the value in the window before the change, and the target change, stated per condition (clear, dust, residual spray, rain, night); a single fleet number mixes road and perception problems (ch-06-07).
  7. Exclusions — shifts with a site-wide weather outage (DLY-SE-01) or a perception release in the window, unless the release is what is being tested.
  8. Safety condition — the person-class detections, near misses with perception involved and suspected missed detections in the same window, reported separately (REQ-06-07-007). A fall in nuisance stops accompanied by a fall in correct detections fails the criterion.

Element 8 puts REQ-06-07-002 into the customer's own acceptance terms: a reduction in nuisance stops is never evidence that a change to detection is acceptable.

The second half of the need — "I can tell quickly that it is nothing" — is measured as the time from stop to classification and from classification to resume in the AMS event log, with the same window. The registry times of SCN-WATER-WT02 (T+30 s, T+45 s) are a reference scenario, not a target; a faster classification that skips the evidence step of REQ-16-07-007 does not meet the need. REQ-20-02-002 states the general rule.

4.8 Delay evidence: system behaviour or site practice?

A delay code gives the time category, not the cause and not the party who can act (ch-19-07). To prioritise needs, the delay records have to answer a further question: who can remove this lost time? This chapter uses three owner classes [H]:

  • System behaviour — time the AHS product's behaviour causes and the supplier can act on: nuisance stops in conditions not linked to watering, diagnosis and repair of a vehicle fault, an AMS failover.
  • Site practice — time the site's way of working causes and the site can act on: stops in residual spray shortly after watering, the wait for a technician and a permission, trucks held by work areas and access permissions.
  • Shared — time that needs both parties, or whose cause the record does not settle: trucks held behind a nuisance stop, stops on communication loss in a network the site owns but the product reacts to.

The split needs a cause field beyond the code (REQ-19-07-002 requires one for performance commitments).

Nuisance stops. Each stop is recorded with the minutes since the last watering at its location (REQ-14-05-007). A stop within 20 minutes [A] after watering on the same segment, with spray or dust on the snapshot, is a site-practice candidate: the Pit Supervisor's watering pattern is the lever (REQ-14-05-008). Other stops are system-behaviour candidates for perception engineering. The rule produces candidates, not verdicts.

Attended stops. In SCN-RECOVER-DT17 the technician arrives with permission and isolation confirmed at T+900 s and the fault is cleared or the truck switched to manual at T+2,400 s. The first 900 s are the site's dispatch, travel and permission practice; the remaining 1,500 s are diagnosis and repair. One DLY-DN-01 interval holds two owners.

LAB-20-02-01 applies this to an illustrative week of delay records [A] (labs/data/delay_week.csv), and values each row at the fleet-average marginal rate of the operating case, 476.7 t per truck-hour:

  • The AMS failover that a controller remembers vividly is the second-smallest row. The anecdote and the evidence rank it differently.
  • Nuisance stops are the largest item, and they divide almost evenly between the supplier's and the site's levers. A need statement that says only "fewer nuisance stops" hides that division; NEED-20-02-04 (the Pit Supervisor's view) and NEED-20-02-07 (the controller's) are two different needs.
  • The attended stops are small in fleet-average terms. Their real cost depends on where they happen (worked example 5).

4.9 Needs that are gates, and needs without a price

Safety needs are gates [H]. NEED-20-02-02 — safety performance no worse, and shown — is not weighted and not traded against tonnes. Three rules follow:

  1. A need is never met by relaxing a safety function, a detection threshold, a permission rule or a gate. If the only solution in view touches such an item, the need goes with the proposal to the safety change process (ch-03-08, REQ-06-07-002) and is assessed there.
  2. A value estimate never appears in a safety decision. "Halving nuisance stops is worth 2,205 t per shift" is a fact about the model, not a reason to accept a change to perception.
  3. Prioritisation (ch-20-05) treats safety-relevant items as gates that pass or fail, not as scores.

A mine under cost pressure may ask for "fewer stops at any cost". The supplier's answer is the acceptance criterion of section 4.7, with its safety condition.

Some needs have no tonnes value [H]. The handover need (NEED-20-02-08) and the workload need (NEED-20-02-11) are about risk and human performance. The tree can price the consequence of a failure — a permission on R03 that stays open 15 minutes [A] too long holds 7,333.3 t of waste per hour (ch-16-02), so 1,833.3 t — but not the absence of failure. These needs are measured by their own indicators: open items missed at handover, read-backs corrected, controller occupancy per hour (REQ-16-10-007). They stay in the register, visible to site management. They are the needs most often missing from a purchase specification, because nobody at the purchase table works the NIGHT shift.

4.10 Trade-offs and limits

  • Fleet-average versus location. The tree makes needs comparable but can be wrong by a factor of ten for one road (worked example 5). Use a location-aware model (ch-06-07, ch-16-05) before committing to a number (REQ-20-02-004).
  • Ranges set the ranking. Measured ranges replace the illustrative ones before a ranking is shown as the customer's.
  • Interactions. One-at-a-time bars ignore interactions; the lab can evaluate any combination directly.
  • Costs are placeholders [A]. Lifecycle cost, fleet sizing, TCO and ROI are developed in Volume 21 (ch-21-01 onwards).
  • Discovery depends on access to the NIGHT shift and the delay records, a contract matter (ch-19-04).

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

The walkthrough uses SCN-WATER-WT02 on a hot, dry DAY shift, at 14:10, on R03 near Intersection Y. Registry key events: T+0 s truck stops for a detected obstacle; T+30 s the AHS Controller reviews the camera and perception snapshot and classifies the stop as a nuisance; T+45 s authorised resume per procedure, delay coded. Times not in the registry are marked [A]. The section follows what each step produces for the customer-needs work.

6.1 Normal: a nuisance stop classified and resumed

Time System People Record for needs work

T+2 s [A] AMS alarm and snapshot on the CR1 screen AHS Controller sees the stop Two trucks behind on R03 are held: DLY-OD-03 by the coding rule

In the normal case nothing about needs happens on the shift. The work is in the record: every field that section 4.7 and section 4.8 rely on is created here. If the minutes since watering are not recorded at T+0 s, NEED-20-02-04 cannot be measured later.

6.2 Abnormal: a stop that cannot be classified quickly

At T+30 s the snapshot is inconclusive: dust hides part of the lane. The controller does not classify the stop as a nuisance, because the evidence does not support it (REQ-16-07-007). The stop stays a stop.

T+30 s Snapshot inconclusive; stop remains unresolved AHS Controller
T+35 s [A] Controller informs the Pit Supervisor; follows the unresolved-stop steps of ch-16-07 AHS Controller

The loss for this stop is more than twice that of 6.1. The controller's need "tell quickly that it is nothing" (NEED-20-02-07) shows up as a long classification time, not as an extra stop; the tree's driver for it is minutes per stop (section 4.6). This case is also where the safety condition lives: a change that let the controller "classify faster" by accepting less evidence would shorten it and fail element 8 of section 4.7.

6.3 Degraded: stops cluster after watering

On the same afternoon WT02 waters R03 heavily after refilling at WF1. Over the next hour R03 sees six nuisance stops [A], each with trucks held behind. The controller's occupancy rises (NEED-20-02-11), and the shift's tonnes fall behind the plan band (NEED-20-02-03).

  • Acceptable on the shift: the Pit Supervisor reduces the watering rate on R03 for the next passes, within the site's dust requirements (REQ-14-05-008), and the clustered stops are recorded with their minutes since watering as evidence for the weekly analysis (REQ-06-07-005).
  • Not acceptable: changing the classification rule or a detection setting to reduce stops. The controller may not change either (REQ-14-05-008), and a change to detection is a change to the safety argument (REQ-06-07-002).

The next morning site management asks the supplier to "tune the trucks so they stop less". The product manager answers with this chapter's structure: two needs, not one (NEED-20-02-04 and NEED-20-02-07); an acceptance criterion with a safety condition; and the evidence split into site practice and system behaviour.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Model choice for valuing a need. The value tree is used for discovery because it is fast, explainable to a customer and uses the same GMG categories as the site's KPIs. Its fleet-average simplification is accepted for ranking and rejected for commitments: for a need tied to a road the team uses the location-aware models of ch-06-07 and ch-16-05 and states which model produced the number (REQ-20-02-004).

Separating count and duration. The tree has two drivers for nuisance stops: the count per truck and shift, and the minutes lost per stop. Requirements keep them separate too. A perception change affects the count; snapshot quality, the HMI and the classification workflow affect the duration.

Queue time and Intersection X. The lab's queue driver is the mean queue per cycle at the loading units. The fleet model has no explicit intersection waits, so a wait at Intersection X is represented as extra seconds per cycle; every load passes X twice (ch-16-05). The queue at C01-Q (DLY-NP-02) comes from the fleet model and is not a separate input: at the baseline the model gives 16.55 truck-hours per shift of queue at the destinations and 6.22 at the loading units. An extra second per cycle costs 90.7 t per shift in the operating case, an upper bound for the ore circuits when C01 limits them.

One set of records. Needs are measured from the records that already serve operations (REQ-01-05-001, REQ-06-07-004, REQ-14-05-007, REQ-16-05-009), not from a separate "customer metrics" log that could disagree with them.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

For SCN-WATER-WT02 the data flow behind the needs is:

  • T+0 s. ADS → AMS: perception stop with truck, segment, position, speed, time and condition (REQ-06-07-004). The AMS joins the minutes since watering from the WT02 watering log (REQ-14-05-001). A 20-minute window [A] is meaningless if the clocks of the AMS and the water truck's position reporting unit differ by minutes; the AMS time is the reference [R].
  • T+2 s [A]. AMS → HMI: stop alarm and snapshot. The classification time is measured from T+0 s, not from the display.
  • T+30 s. HMI → AMS: classification with evidence reference — the end of the "tell it is nothing" interval of NEED-20-02-07.
  • T+45 s. AMS grants the path; the FMS closes the stopped truck's DLY-OD-03 interval. Held trucks' intervals close when each may move again.
  • End of shift, weekly, per release. Delay intervals with cause and owner class go to reporting; the weekly analysis of REQ-06-07-005 and the before-and-after comparison of REQ-06-07-006 provide the windows of section 4.7.

A driver value that is not measured over a stated window stays [A] in the model and is labelled so in anything shown to the customer (REQ-20-02-004).

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

During SCN-WATER-WT02 the controller sees the stop alarm, the snapshot, the stop's location on the map and the trucks held behind it. After classification the stop record shows its code, the evidence and the minutes since watering. The Pit Supervisor sees the shift's DLY-OD-03 total by road and by minutes since watering (NEED-20-02-04) and, beside each open item, what it holds and what it costs per hour (NEED-20-02-06). At handover the incoming controller sees the list of open items generated from system state and signs for each (NEED-20-02-08).

None of these views is new; earlier volumes require them. What is new is that each is also the measuring instrument of a customer need, and the controller's coding is the raw material of the supplier's priorities.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — discovery interview and read-back

Procedure [H], for one role on one shift:

  1. Product manager: agree the interview with the Pit Supervisor, at a time that does not take the role away from a first response (for the AHS Controller, never during an active event).
  2. Product manager: choose one recent situation from the records (for example the last nuisance stop on R03) and bring its record.
  3. Product manager and role: walk through the six prompts of section 4.2; write down the role's words.
  4. Product manager: draft the need statement (section 4.3) with a measure and the delay-code evidence.
  5. Role: on a later shift, hear the statement read back; confirm, correct or reject it.
  6. Product manager: record role, shift, date, reply and correction. A rejected need is kept with its rejection.
  7. Product manager: enter only validated needs into the product backlog (ch-20-05). Mark any need whose solution could touch a safety function and route it to the safety change process.
  8. Site engineering: review the register monthly with site management, showing executive, site and shift needs together, and the owner split of the delay evidence.

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • Product managers and sales engineers [A]: GMG time categories and the site's delay codes; the value tree and its limits; the interview guide and read-back; the safety-gate rule.
  • AHS Controllers: why the cause field and the minutes since watering are recorded; their coding is the evidence for product priorities.
  • Pit Supervisors and Maintenance Planners: reading the owner split of their delay records; stating needs as measures; validating need statements.
  • Site management: the difference between the tonnes and cost-per-tonne rankings (section 4.6), and that safety needs are gates.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-20-02-001 [H] Every customer need that enters the product backlog shall be recorded as a need statement with ID, level, stakeholder role, situation (a registered scenario where one exists), the need in the stakeholder's words, a measure, its source, its delay-code or event evidence, the value-tree node or the statement that it is a gate or has no tonnes value, and the trace to requirement IDs once they exist. Rationale: a need without a measure or a trace cannot be shown to be met (sections 4.3, 4.4). Verification: inspection of the register; analysis by script that every traced ID exists (LAB-20-02-01 check_traces).

REQ-20-02-002 [H] A qualitative need shall be accepted as a requirement only after it has been stated as an acceptance criterion with metric, definition, population, measurement window, data source, baseline and target per condition, exclusions and, for a need that touches a safety-related function, a safety condition measured in the same window. Rationale: "fewer nuisance stops" cannot be tested, and can be met in a way that raises missed detections (section 4.7). Verification: inspection of the acceptance criteria.

REQ-20-02-003 [H] Prioritisation of needs shall use delay and event records over a stated window, with each interval's code, recorded cause and owner class (system behaviour, site practice, shared), and shall show lost truck-hours and their valuation per owner class; an anecdotal priority shall be shown beside the record-based one, not instead of it. Rationale: the anecdote and the evidence can rank the same item differently, and nuisance stops divide between supplier and site levers (section 4.8). Verification: inspection of the prioritisation record; test (LAB-20-02-01 delay week).

REQ-20-02-004 [H] Every value estimate of a need shall state the model and its version, whether the value is fleet-average or location-specific, the inputs with their evidence labels and the measurement window of each measured input, and shall show tonnes per shift and cost per tonne with a one-at-a-time sensitivity over stated ranges; a value committed to a customer for a need tied to a location shall come from a location-aware model. Rationale: the fleet-average tree can differ from a location model by a factor of ten for one road (sections 4.4, 4.10; worked example 5). Verification: inspection; test (LAB-20-02-01 tornado and need cases).

REQ-20-02-005 [H] No customer need, value estimate or priority shall be met or justified by relaxing a safety function, a detection threshold, a permission rule or a gate; a need whose proposed solution touches a safety-related function shall be routed with the proposal to the safety change process and assessed there as a pass or fail gate, not as a weighted value. Rationale: safety stays out of reach of commercial pressure; a reduction in nuisance stops is not evidence that a detection change is acceptable (sections 4.9, 6.3; REQ-06-07-002). Verification: inspection of the backlog and the change records; review of a sample of needs that touch perception.

REQ-20-02-006 [H] Discovery shall include interviews with the AHS Controller, Pit Supervisor, Shovel Operator, Maintenance Planner and Field Technician on both DAY and NIGHT shifts, and shift-level needs without a tonnes value shall be kept in the register with their own measures. Rationale: handover and workload needs are felt on the shift, mostly at night, and rarely appear in a purchase specification (sections 4.2, 4.9). Verification: inspection of the discovery plan and interview records.

REQ-20-02-007 [H] A need statement shall enter the product backlog only after it has been read back to the role that stated it and the reply, any correction and the date have been recorded. Rationale: read-back catches a need written in the supplier's words before it is built (sections 4.2, 8.3). Verification: inspection of validation records against backlog entries.

REQ-20-02-008 [H] A need about availability shall state whether it concerns physical availability or use of availability, with the Downtime and Standby hours behind it and the role that owns each. Rationale: the value tree gives both the same value per point, but they have different owners, levers and costs (section 4.5). Verification: inspection of the register; test (LAB-20-02-01 availability equivalence).

10. Verification and validation

The register is verified by inspection and by script. LAB-20-02-01 checks that every traced requirement ID exists in the handbook, that every level has needs, that the five operational roles of REQ-20-02-006 appear and that need IDs are unique. A site register would add a check that every need has a validation record (REQ-20-02-007).

The model is verified against a known case: with the default drivers the tree reproduces the fleet model's baseline DAY shift exactly (191,180 t). Its monotonic behaviour is tested, and the lab's tests pin the operating-case values so that a later change to ahs_sim that moves them is noticed.

The needs are validated by read-back (section 8.3), and later in operation: after a change aimed at a need, the acceptance criterion of section 4.7 is measured in its window. If the measure moved and the role still reports the pain point, the need was misunderstood and goes back to discovery.

The tree is not validated against the reference mine, which is fictional. At a real site it would be validated by comparing predicted and recorded tonnes for measured drivers over a window, and stating the error.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Building to the executive slogan: "lower cost per tonne" accepted without the drivers below it.
  • One number for availability: physical availability and use of availability merged; the need goes to the wrong owner.
  • Pricing everything at the fleet average: a recovery on R03 valued as an average truck-hour (worked example 5).
  • A target without a condition: one fleet nuisance-stop rate that mixes watering effects with perception behaviour.
  • Meeting "fewer stops" by detection settings: a safety change justified by productivity.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Interviewing only the DAY shift and the purchasers: handover and workload needs never appear.
  • Asking what the person wants: the answer is a design solution; the need behind it is lost.
  • Skipping the read-back: the need enters the backlog in the supplier's words.
  • Coding without cause: DLY-OD-03 without minutes since watering cannot be split between site and supplier levers.
  • Letting the memorable event set the priority: an AMS failover outranks a hundred hours of nuisance stops.

12. Worked examples

All values from LAB-20-02-01 (ahs_sim 0.22.0) unless stated. Drivers and costs [A].

Example 1 — The two cases. Idealised shift: cost 50,000 + 400 × 444 = 227,600 cu over 191,180 t = 1.191 cu/t. Operating case: 444 × 0.90 × 0.90 = 359.64 operating truck-h; 37 × 3 × 5/60 = 9.25 truck-h of nuisance stops; cost 50,000 + 400 × 359.64 + 30 × 111 = 197,186 cu over 158,659 t = 1.243 cu/t. It is the base of every other example.

Example 2 — "Fewer nuisance stops", two ways. From the operating case:

Change [A] Tonnes per shift Change (t) Cost per tonne (cu/t) Change (cu/t)
Halve the count: 3 → 1.5 stops per truck-shift 160,864 +2,205 1.2154 −0.0274
Shorten the loss: 5 → 2 minutes per stop 161,305 +2,646 1.2224 −0.0204
Loss per stop equal to the registry's 45 s (0.75 min) 162,407 +3,748 1.2141 −0.0287
No nuisance stops at all 163,068 +4,409 1.189 −0.054

Shortening the loss per stop is worth more in tonnes than halving the count, but halving the count saves more cost per tonne, because each stop handled costs 30 cu [A]. The two halves of NEED-20-02-07 are both worth pursuing, by different means. The 45 s row shows how much rides on the assumed 5 minutes: it is the first value to measure.

Example 3 — Which availability? Physical availability 0.95 with use of availability 0.90, against 0.90 with 0.95: both 379.62 operating truck-hours, 167,423 t (+8,763 t) and 1.2255 cu/t. Downtime 22.2 against 44.4 truck-h; Standby 42.18 against 19.98 truck-h. Same value; different owners.

Example 4 — A wait at Intersection X. A wait of 10 s [A] on each of the two passes of every cycle adds 20 s per cycle: 156,866 t (−1,794 t) and 1.2570 cu/t (+0.0142). One second per cycle is worth 90.7 t per shift in the operating case. Over 14 shifts a week, 10 s per pass at X is 25,112 t [A] — a need of the Pit Supervisor that nobody would state as "throughput at X" but everyone would feel.

Example 5 — Fleet-average against location: a recovery on R03. In the tree, DT17's 2,400 s from stop to clearance is 0.667 truck-h, worth 0.667 × 476.7 = 318 t. LAB-16-05-01 (ch-16-05) prices the same recovery on R03 at 4,055.8 t in steady state (3,483.3 t of it the closure of R03 that holds EX03 and EX04) and at 2,420 t in its dynamic run. The tree understates it by a factor of 8 to 13. The reverse also happens: ch-06-07 values 3.45 truck-hours of DLY-OD-03 at 880 t because the ore circuits are crusher-limited; the tree would give 3.45 × 476.7 = 1,645 t. Use the tree to rank needs and to explain them; quote a location-aware value for a need tied to a road.

Example 6 — One week of delay evidence. The illustrative week of section 4.8: 185.89 truck-h in nine rows, 88,608 t at the fleet-average rate. Ranked by truck-hours, the two nuisance-stop rows lead (77.83 and 51.67 truck-h) and the AMS failover is eighth (1.75 truck-h). By owner: system behaviour 82.08 truck-h, site practice 83.81, shared 20.00. The largest single site-practice lever is the watering pattern on R03 and R05 (W01); the largest system-behaviour lever is nuisance stops in other conditions (W02).

Example 7 — The cost of an open item at handover. A permission on R03 left active 15 minutes [A] longer than needed because the handover missed it holds every waste circuit: 7,333.3 t per hour (ch-16-02), so 1,833.3 t. In fleet terms that is about 3.8 truck-hours (1,833.3 / 476.7). NEED-20-02-08 has no tonnes value of its own; this is the price of its failure, not its value.

15. Key takeaways

  • Needs come at three levels. Connect them with a model, or the executive need stays a slogan and the shift need an anecdote.
  • Write each need in the role's words, with a measure, evidence and a trace. Read it back before it enters the backlog.
  • The value tree turns availability, nuisance stops and queue time into tonnes and cost per tonne. Its default drivers are an idealised shift (191,180 t); the illustrative operating case gives 158,659 t and 1.243 cu/t.
  • Physical availability and use of availability multiply, so the tree values them equally; their owners differ.
  • Tonnes and cost per tonne rank the drivers differently. Ask which one the mine is judged on.
  • A qualitative need becomes testable with metric, definition, population, window, data source, baseline and target, exclusions and a safety condition.
  • Delay records with cause and owner class separate system behaviour from site practice.
  • Safety needs are gates. No need is met by relaxing a safety function.
