1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is 10:00 on a DAY shift at the reference mine. Crusher C01 trips on a blocked chute (SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN). At T+0 s the plant sends not ready. At T+5 s the AMS withholds the tipping bay and holds the ore trucks at C01-Q. At T+300 s the FMS puts a reassignment proposal on the AHS Controller's screen, and the controller approves it. Every step works, and every step crosses an organisational boundary. The crusher belongs to the plant. The AMS that held the trucks comes from one supplier. The FMS that proposed the reassignment comes from another, bought years before anyone spoke of autonomy. The controller who approved it is a site employee.

An hour later, at EX02, the Shovel Operator calls dozer DZ03 to clean up the loading floor (SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02). The dozer is manned and carries a position reporting unit, a radio and a field terminal. The AMS reserves a work zone and holds the trucks at EX02-Q until DZ03 has left. Again the job works only because devices on a manned machine, the AMS and the people on the radio agree on what each message means.

Both events are routine, and both show that an autonomous haulage system is never bought as one thing. It is a stack of layers joined by interfaces. Each layer can come from a different party, and each interface is a place where parties compete, cooperate or blame each other. That is what this chapter calls market structure.

The questions this chapter addresses are:

  • Into which layers does an AHS offering decompose, and how do the layers map onto the handbook's reference architecture (FMS–AMS–ADS–VCS)?
  • Where can suppliers compete for one layer, and where must they partner? What role do interface openness and the safety argument play?
  • For a brownfield mine with an FMS already in service, how does an offering's FMS–AMS boundary change the integration work and what the site must replace?
  • What ties a mine to its supplier after deployment, and how does that shape the market?
  • Which mines find autonomy attractive, which people in a mining company shape the purchase, and where can reliable market information be found?

This chapter covers:

  • the value chain of an AHS offering in eight layers and ten interfaces, mapped onto the architecture (FIG-20-01-01);
  • a qualitative model of how interface openness, the safety argument and switching costs decide where suppliers compete or partner;
  • four illustrative offering archetypes evaluated against the reference mine's existing FMS, trucks and plant;
  • technical switching costs, counted for the reference mine from the shared data;
  • a qualitative customer-segment map (FIG-20-01-02), the buying organisation and the rules for recording market information with evidence labels.

No market size, growth rate, market share, price, supplier name or supplier capability is stated in this chapter. Where a market figure would be needed, the chapter says so and marks it [TBV]. Commercial models belong to ch-20-06, and fleet sizing, total cost of ownership and return on investment to Volume 21.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Decompose an AHS offering into value-chain layers (vehicle platform, retrofit kit, ADS, AMS, FMS, mine network, survey and mapping, services) and explain how architecture boundaries determine where suppliers compete or partner.
  2. Explain, as a qualitative model, how interoperability and interface openness change market structure, without asserting any supplier's position or capability.
  3. Analyse how the FMS–AMS boundary of an offering determines integration effort with a brownfield mine's existing FMS, using the reference mine as the example.
  4. Explain the technical switching costs a mine faces after deployment (AHS map data, configuration, interfaces, safety-case evidence, trained staff) and how they shape competition.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Classify mine operators by the drivers that make autonomy attractive (commodity, mine life, brownfield or greenfield, labour availability, remoteness, mixed-fleet composition) and relate each driver to an operational problem at a mine like the reference mine.
  2. Identify the stakeholders inside a mining company who shape an AHS purchase (site operations, mine planning, maintenance, safety, IT/OT, procurement, executive) and what evidence each needs.
  3. Locate public sources of market information and record each figure with an evidence label, leaving unverified figures [TBV].
  4. Describe how the site's existing manned and ancillary fleet and its plant (the C01 control system) constrain the realistic choice among supplier offerings.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine is a brownfield site (mine.yaml). Before autonomy it already had an FMS, a fleet of manned trucks, a crusher with its own control system, a wireless network and a survey team. In this volume it is the customer [A]. It is deciding, or has decided, how to buy autonomy, and every option it can choose is shaped by what it already has.

Three kinds of operational problem make market structure a site topic rather than a head-office topic.

  • The production chain does not stop at the haul road. Haulage feeds C01, and C01 can stop the haulage. The plant–fleet interface (ICD-C01-STATUS, ch-02-06) is needed whichever party supplies the AMS and the FMS: an absent plant state is not ready (REQ-02-06-004), so without it no truck tips at C01.
  • The fleet is mixed. Four dozers, three water trucks, twelve light vehicles and a grader enter the autonomous areas with position reporting units, radios and field terminals (equipment.yaml). Four manned shovels load the trucks. Whichever autonomy offering the mine buys must work with these machines and the people in them every shift.
  • What the mine already owns has value and inertia. The FMS holds years of dispatch rules, delay coding and reporting, and the trucks are paid for. An offering that replaces them has fewer interfaces and more to change.

The people who live with these problems — the AHS Controller, the Pit Supervisor, the Maintenance Planner — are often not asked when offerings are compared. This chapter gives them a way to read an offering as layers and interfaces.

3. Terminology and foundations

Value chain [H]. The sequence of layers that together deliver autonomous haulage at a mine, from the vehicle platform to services. Each layer can be supplied by a different party. The handbook uses eight layers (section 4.1).

Offering and offering archetype [H]. An offering is the set of layers one supplier proposes to supply. An offering archetype is an illustrative pattern of offerings [A] used in this chapter to reason about market structure. It describes no real product.

Interoperability keeps its meaning from ch-02-05: the ability of systems from different suppliers to work together through agreed interfaces. Conformance is a product's meeting the specification it claims to implement. Conformance is necessary, not sufficient, for interoperability (ch-02-05).

Interface openness [H]. How far an interface is documented and available to parties other than its supplier. The handbook distinguishes four levels (section 4.3).

Switching cost [H]. The cost and effort of replacing a supplier's layer with another's: re-creating data, re-integrating interfaces, rebuilding safety-case evidence, retraining people. Section 4.5 treats the technical part; no money value is estimated.

Customer segment [H]. A group of mines that share the characteristics that make autonomy attractive or difficult, defined by operational drivers (section 4.6).

Brownfield site keeps its glossary meaning: an operating site into which a new system is introduced. A greenfield site is one built from the start with the new system in mind.

Party. In this chapter the parties are the site, a primary AHS supplier P, a second technology supplier K, a base-truck manufacturer M and the incumbent FMS supplier F. All are functions [A]. None stands for a real company.

Supply scope and interface owner keep their meanings from ch-19-04 and ch-02-06. Every element has one supplying party, and every interface has one accountable party.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The value chain of an AHS offering

FIG-20-01-01 stacks the eight layers of the handbook's value chain and maps each one onto the reference architecture of ch-01-04.

L1 Vehicle platform Base truck: chassis, drivetrain, VCS, onboard safety-related systems (fire detection and suppression, payload, tyre monitoring) VCS and onboard systems
L2 Retrofit / autonomy kit Perception sensors, onboard computer, radios, positioning; added actuation where needed (ch-08-07) Hardware on which the ADS runs
L3 ADS software Perception, localisation, planning, motion control ADS
L4 AMS Missions, traffic coordination, permissions, exceptions; the manned-vehicle devices and the remote stop are supplied with it in this model AMS; manned-vehicle devices; independent safety function
L5 FMS Production planning, dispatch, assignments, reporting FMS

L7 Survey and mapping Surveys, road design, the AHS map data AHS map
L8 Services Deployment, support, training, safety-case support, data services Across all layers

Three features of the mapping matter for market structure.

The layers are not the architecture. The architecture says what each element decides; the value chain says what each party sells. They coincide for the FMS and the AMS, not below them: the ADS runs on the kit's hardware, and the kit is fixed to someone else's truck. The remote stop is drawn in L4 because it is supplied with the AMS in this model. Architecturally it is independent of the AMS and the ADS (master-instructions §3), and I-10 runs directly to the truck.

Ten interfaces join the layers. I-01 joins FMS and AMS; I-02 to I-04 run down the stack to the truck; I-05 joins the AMS to the devices on manned vehicles; I-06 and I-07 bring the C01 status to the AMS and the FMS; I-08 and I-09 join the network and the map data to the AMS; I-10 joins the remote stop to the truck. They are interfaces of the market model [H], coarser than the ICDs of ch-02-06.

Five interfaces lie inside the safety argument. I-02, I-03, I-04, I-05 and I-10 carry path grants, motion commands, access permissions and stop commands. Hazard analysis and verification must cover both sides of each together (Volume 04). The other five carry production, carriage or data, and the receiving element treats their failure fail-closed (for example REQ-02-06-004 at the plant interface).

The plant is drawn outside the value chain. C01's control system belongs to the processing plant and its control room, not to any AHS layer. Its interfaces I-06 and I-07 are needed in every offering.

4.2 Where suppliers compete and where they partner

A supplier can compete for one layer only if a customer can buy that layer alone and join it to the layers it has from others. Whether that is possible depends less on the supplier's intentions than on the interfaces at the layer's boundary. The handbook's qualitative model [H] names four factors.

  1. Interface openness. A layer can be bought separately only at a boundary whose interface is documented and available to the other parties (section 4.3). At a closed boundary, the layers on both sides are sold together or not at all.
  2. Safety coupling. Where the safety argument of the autonomous truck crosses a boundary, the parties on both sides must share hazard analyses, design information and verification evidence. A purchase across such a boundary is therefore a partnership with defined joint responsibilities, not a purchase against a specification alone. ch-08-07 shows the same effect at I-04: the integration pattern decides who carries which evidence.
  3. Site integration effort. Some interfaces need site-specific work whatever the product — the plant interface, the network, the survey and map data. They stay with the site or its integrator. They are not a place where suppliers of AHS products compete.
  4. Switching costs. Once data, configuration, evidence and skills have accumulated around one supplier's layer, the mine's next purchase is no longer a free choice (section 4.5).

From these factors the model draws five statements [H]. They are reasoning, not observations of any market. Published analyses of how interface standards affect competition in mining technology have not been read for this chapter [TBV] (SRC-PUB-MINING-TECH-INTEROP-MARKET).

  • M1. Separate purchase is most practical at open interfaces outside the safety argument (I-01, I-08, I-09).
  • M2. Across interfaces inside the safety argument (I-02 to I-05, I-10), layers are supplied together or by parties who agree to share evidence. A buyer who splits them must plan that agreement as part of the purchase.
  • M3. The plant, network and survey interfaces stay cross-party in every arrangement, so the site needs its own integration capability (the integration authority of ch-02-01) whatever it buys.
  • M4. Covering more layers reduces cross-party interfaces. It does not remove the interfaces. It moves them inside one supplier, where the site sees less of them.
  • M5. Switching costs collect where site-specific data and evidence live — the AMS, its map and configuration, and the safety case — more than in any hardware.

4.3 Interoperability and interface openness

Interface openness is a property of each interface, not of a supplier. The handbook uses four levels [H]:

Level Description What the site can do
O0 Closed Not documented outside the supplier Connect only the supplier's own products
O1 Documented to the customer ICD under contract to the site; not shareable Integrate its own systems; not invite a third party
O2 Documented and licensable ICD available to third parties the site names, under agreed terms Buy the other side from a different party
O3 Published standard Implements a published interface standard with a conformance statement naming parts and options Compare offerings on the same basis; still test the combination

Higher openness widens the choice at a boundary, because more parties can supply the other side. It also lowers switching cost there, because a replacement need not be integrated from nothing. Neither effect removes integration work at the site. Interoperability has technical, syntactic, semantic and operational levels (ch-02-05). A published standard can fix the lower levels, but the meaning of a field in the mine's own procedures is agreed at the mine.

For the FMS–AMS boundary there is a published standard. The handbook's source register records, at catalogue level, that ISO 23725:2024 is titled Autonomous system and fleet management system interoperability, that its published scope defines the interfaces between fleet management systems and autonomous haulage systems for dispatching haul trucks and coordinating production information in surface mining, and that it excludes authentication, authorization and cybersecurity and leaves safe machine operation to agreement between the FMS and AHS suppliers [F] (SRC-STD-ISO23725, catalogue level). Its clause content is not verified. This chapter does not say what the standard requires or whether any product conforms to it.

Three consequences follow. First, the boundary with a published standard is I-01, outside the safety argument. Second, the standard's published scope covers no other interface of the model — not I-05, I-06, I-07 or I-04. There, openness depends on what each party documents and agrees. Third, a conformance statement is evidence of conformance only. Two conformant products can differ where the standard leaves options open, and the site must test the combination (ch-02-05, REQ-20-01-005).

Inside the safety chain, openness has a limit that commercial wishes cannot move. An interface that carries motion commands, path grants or stop commands is only as trustworthy as the evidence behind both sides. Opening it to a third party moves part of the safety argument to that party. That needs the joint hazard analysis and verification of statement M2. Declaring the interface open does not provide them.

4.4 Offering archetypes and the FMS–AMS boundary at a brownfield mine

To see how offerings interact with what a brownfield mine already owns, the figure script holds four archetypes [A]. None of them describes a real product. In all four the site keeps the plant, the network and the survey function. The site also keeps its trucks (from M) and its FMS (from F) unless the archetype replaces them.

  • OA-1 — Integrated stack. P supplies new autonomy-ready trucks, the kit, the ADS, the AMS with its devices and remote stop, and its own FMS. The site's FMS and its manned trucks are replaced.
  • OA-2 — Autonomy for the existing fleet, site keeps its FMS. P supplies the kit, the ADS, the AMS, the devices and the remote stop. The existing trucks are converted, and the existing FMS stays. This is the split assumed in ch-02-05 and in option OPT-1 of ch-19-04 [A].
  • OA-3 — Autonomy for the existing fleet with the supplier's FMS. As OA-2, but P also replaces the FMS.
  • OA-4 — Layered. P supplies the AMS, the devices and the remote stop. K supplies the kit and the ADS. The existing trucks and the existing FMS stay.

For each archetype, figures/make_figures.py assigns every element a supplying party and counts the interfaces whose two ends are supplied by different parties:

OA-1 OA-2 OA-3 OA-4
Parties P, site P, F, M, site P, M, site P, K, F, M, site
Cross-party interfaces 4 7 6 8
New or changed cross-party interfaces 4 6 6 7
Cross-party interfaces inside the safety argument 0 2 (I-04, I-10) 2 (I-04, I-10) 3 (I-02, I-04, I-10)
Site-side cross-party interfaces I-06, I-07, I-08, I-09 same same same

"New or changed" excludes the plant–FMS interface I-07 where the site's existing FMS stays: that interface already works in the manned operation. Four conclusions follow, and none depends on the particular counts.

The FMS–AMS boundary is the main brownfield choice. In OA-2 and OA-4 the existing FMS must speak to a new AMS across I-01: assignments, cancellations, held trucks and their reasons, cycle events, delay codes. In OA-3 the boundary moves inside P. The price is a new FMS, with new dispatch rules, delay coding and reports for every shift. The site trades cross-party integration for internal change.

Fewest interfaces means most replacement. OA-1 has only the four site-side interfaces because it replaces both the FMS and the 40 manned trucks. At a brownfield mine that is a fleet decision, not an integration decision.

The site-side interfaces never go away. An offering that is silent about the plant, network and survey interfaces has left them to the site (statement M3).

Splitting the safety chain adds parties to the safety argument. In OA-4, I-02 crosses between P and K, so every path-grant and vehicle-state message needs their joint verification. OA-2 and OA-3 have two such interfaces: I-04, where the kit meets the base truck (ch-08-07), and I-10, where the stop receiver is fitted to it.

The table counts interfaces, not effort. A cross-party interface that conforms to a published standard and has been tested elsewhere can be cheap. One whose meaning differs between the parties can dominate a project. The counts show where to look.

4.5 Technical switching costs

After deployment a mine accumulates assets that are specific to the layers it bought. The figure script counts them for the reference mine from the shared data:

Asset Reference mine (shared data) What a change of AMS supplier would require
AHS map data 25 nodes, 25 road segments, 15.27 km of road; 3 autonomous areas; 4 access points (mine.yaml) Map converted or rebuilt, then validated against the as-built roads (ch-12-07)
Configuration Areas, permissions, speed limits, delay codes, device registry (ch-03-08 configuration register) Rebuilt in the new system; every item re-verified
Interfaces 10 interfaces of the market model, each with one or more ICDs I-01, I-02, I-05, I-06, I-08 and I-09 re-integrated; ICDs rewritten at O0 or O1
Equipment 40 trucks with kits and stop receivers; 20 manned and ancillary vehicles with PRU, radio and field terminal; 4 shovels with SOT and SPS; 3 remote-stop transmitters (equipment.yaml) Kits, receivers and devices replaced or re-integrated, truck by truck and vehicle by vehicle
Safety-case evidence Hazard analyses, verification results, site acceptance records (Volume 04, ch-18-04) Largely rebuilt: evidence is bound to the system it was produced for
Trained staff 7 roles (mine.yaml), each with procedures and HMI skills Retrained on the new HMI and any changed procedures
Operating history Event logs, delay-coded history, KPI series Kept only if exportable in a documented format (ch-19-04 data-access provision)

Two assets deserve emphasis.

Safety-case evidence does not transfer. Hazard analyses and verification results are produced for one system in one configuration at one site. A new AMS needs new evidence even when it is functionally similar. No contract removes that switching cost.

Training in handbook terms lowers the cost of change. ch-01-04 recommends neutral terms in requirements, procedures and training. A controller who has learned "permission", "work zone" and "remote stop" as functions retrains on a new screen. A controller who has learned only one product's names relearns the job.

High switching costs protect an incumbent supplier and weaken the mine's position at renewal, the effect usually called lock-in. A mine reduces it by agreeing openness at I-01, I-05, I-06 and I-09 and data access to the map, configuration and logs before it buys (REQ-20-01-006). How far switching costs shape competition in the AHS market is not verified [TBV] (SRC-PUB-MINING-TECH-INTEROP-MARKET).

4.6 Customer segments: what makes autonomy attractive

Mines differ in why autonomy would help them and in what makes it hard. FIG-20-01-02 is the handbook's qualitative map [H]. It sets six characteristics of a mine against five operational drivers. A filled circle means the described pole of a characteristic strengthens the operational case through that driver. An open circle means it weakens it, and a square means it depends on the site.

The drivers are operational problems, not slogans:

  • D1 Staffing 24/7 haul rosters. Two 12-hour shifts (mine.yaml) need an operator in every truck on every shift. Where operators are hard to recruit or retain, autonomy addresses a staffing problem.
  • D2 Exposure of people in haul areas. Autonomy removes the truck operator from the haul road, but manned ancillary work continues inside the autonomous areas. The net change depends on the mixed fleet.
  • D3 Predictable cycles and plant feed. Where a fixed plant such as C01 must be fed steadily, consistent cycles matter. Whether autonomy changes them at a given mine must be measured in the site baseline. The handbook uses no published productivity figure [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AHS-PRODUCTIVITY-UPLIFT).
  • D4 Integration and change burden. Existing systems, roads, workforce and mixed fleet must be integrated or changed.
  • D5 Time to recover the investment. Mine life, commodity cycles and the length of the conversion decide whether benefits have time to accumulate. Volume 21 quantifies it.

Combinations of characteristics define segments. Four illustrative segments [A] show how the drivers combine:

Segment [A] Characteristics Strong drivers Main burden
SEG-1 Remote, long-life, bulk brownfield Remote, scarce labour, long life, fixed plant, existing systems D1, D3, D5 D4 (FMS, trucks, plant interface)
SEG-2 Greenfield designed for autonomy Roads, network and procedures designed for autonomy from the start D1, D3 depending on location Lower D4; the whole system is new
SEG-3 Short remaining life or small fleet Few years left or few trucks Possibly D1 D5: little time to recover the investment
SEG-4 Brownfield with heavy mixed-fleet work Much manned ancillary work in haul areas, labour available D2 partly D3 and D4: work-zone holds, devices on every manned vehicle

The shared data cannot place the reference mine in a segment. mine.yaml says it is brownfield, in an Australian setting, and equipment.yaml lists 20 manned and ancillary vehicles with devices and 4 manned shovels. The commodity, mine life, labour availability and remoteness are not specified, so the mine is SEG-1 or SEG-4 depending on facts the handbook has not set. A segment assigned without those facts would be a guess presented as analysis. Published analyses of why mines adopt autonomous haulage have not been read for this chapter [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AHS-ADOPTION-DRIVERS).

Each driver points to a problem that can be checked at the site: D1 against rosters and vacancies, D2 against manned work in autonomous areas, D3 against delay-coded losses at C01, D4 against the interface inventory of ch-19-01, D5 against the mine plan. ch-20-02 turns these drivers into measurable customer needs.

Much of the evidence is site data — delay-coded history, the interface inventory, the road network. Operations evidence arrives late unless someone asks for it: a purchase driven by executive and procurement questions can be well advanced before the Pit Supervisor sees what the trucks will do when C01 stops. And safety is not a scoring function. The safety function judges whether every offering meets the site's safety requirements. It does not trade safety against price (REQ-20-01-008; ch-20-05).

4.8 Market information and evidence labels

People in every function will meet market figures: numbers of autonomous trucks in operation, growth forecasts, supplier shares, typical prices. The handbook's rule is simple. A figure without a verified source, used within the scope of that verification, does not enter a document as fact. It is [TBV].

Public sources differ in what they can support:

Source type Can support Typical limit
Mining-company reports and media releases Statements by a company about its own operations Baselines and methods rarely given; not representative of other mines
Supplier publications What a supplier says about its own product Claims, not verification; the handbook does not use them except as labelled [V] examples
Regulator publications (incident reports, safety bulletins, guidance) What the regulator published about an event or a topic About incidents and expectations, not about markets
Standards bodies' catalogues Designation, title, edition, published scope Clause content not verified at catalogue level
Industry guidelines (for example GMG) Definitions and methods Not market data
Peer-reviewed studies and government statistics Measured results with methods May be dated or about other settings
Commercial market-research reports Market estimates Methods often not stated; access often restricted

The handbook's source register already holds two entries that show how scope limits use. A large mining company's 2018 media release stated that trucks fitted with autonomous haulage technology had moved more than one billion tonnes across five of its sites since trial operations began in 2008 [F] (SRC-PUB-MINING-CO-AHS-RELEASE-2018). That is the company's statement about its own operations, verified as published. It supports no figure for the market as a whole, no share and no comparison with other mines. The same release contains estimates of operating hours and unit costs whose baseline and method it does not give. The handbook does not use them as inputs. The second entry is ISO 23725 at catalogue level (section 4.3). It supports what the standard is called and what its published scope covers, and nothing about any product.

No source for AHS market size, growth, deployment counts or supplier shares has been verified for this handbook [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AHS-MARKET-DATA). Section 12.4 works through the recording of one figure.

4.9 The site's existing fleet and plant as constraints

Four things the reference mine already has narrow its realistic choice among offerings.

Manned and ancillary vehicles. Every manned vehicle that enters an autonomous area carries a position reporting unit, a radio and a field terminal (master-instructions §4) — 20 vehicles at the reference mine. An offering must supply these devices or integrate another party's across I-05. ch-02-05 shows what happens when a device supplier changes its message version without the AMS supplier knowing. A mine with many such vehicles gains most from openness at I-05.

Manned shovels. EX01 to EX04 carry a shovel operator terminal and a shovel position source (equipment.yaml). An offering must name who supplies them.

The plant. C01's control system speaks ICD-C01-STATUS to the AMS and the FMS (ch-02-06, ch-15-03). The plant is not a market the AHS supplier competes in, but its interface is a precondition of ore haulage and needs a named owner.

The existing FMS and trucks. Keeping them (OA-2, OA-4) makes the incumbent FMS supplier a party at I-01 and the base-truck manufacturer a party at I-04 (ch-08-07). Replacing them (OA-1, OA-3) is a fleet and dispatch decision.

The real choice is therefore narrower than the offerings on paper. Whatever it buys, the mine must own or integrate the four site-side interfaces and represent 20 manned vehicles and 4 manned shovels in the AMS.

4.10 Trade-offs and limits of the model

  • More layers from one supplier or fewer. More layers reduce cross-party interfaces, raise switching costs and replace more of what the site owns. Fewer layers keep choice and existing systems, with integration the site must lead.
  • Openness or integration. An open interface widens choice. A closed one may be simpler to verify inside one supplier. The handbook prefers openness outside the safety argument and explicit joint evidence inside it [R].
  • Counts are not costs. Prices, fees and integration costs belong to ch-20-06 and Volume 21.
  • Archetypes are not products. Real offerings mix the archetypes and change between product generations. The handbook maps no product onto them.

Manned-vehicle devices L4 (supply) P in all (another party in ch-02-05) I-05 Fitting to every manned vehicle that enters an autonomous area
Mine network L6 site I-08 Coverage, backhaul, time
Survey and mapping L7 site I-09 As-built surveys; map validation
Plant (C01) outside site and plant I-06, I-07 ICD-C01-STATUS; the plant control room
People — site employees; supplier staff under contract procedures, HMI, radio Decisions on shift; safety decisions at the site

The boundary that matters most is between supply and accountability. Suppliers supply layers. The site, as integration authority (ch-02-01), is accountable for the combination, because no supplier controls the plant, the network, the survey data and the people on shift.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

The shared scenario for sections 6, 7 and 8 is SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN. Its registered key events are: T+0 s C01 not-ready signal; T+5 s tipping-bay reservation withheld and trucks held at C01-Q; T+300 s FMS reassignment proposal, which the controller approves. The times T+30 s and T+60 s are taken from ch-15-06, which uses the same scenario. T+0 s is 10:00 on a DAY shift. The installed arrangement is OA-2 [A]: the AMS comes from supplier P, the FMS from the incumbent supplier F, and the plant and the controller belong to the site. Other intermediate times are [A] and marked so.

6.1 Normal operation — every interface has an owner

Time Event Interface Parties
T+0 s C01 sends NOT_READY, reason CHUTE_BLOCKED, to the AMS and the FMS I-06, I-07 site → P; site → F
T+5 s AMS withholds the tipping-bay reservation; ore trucks held at C01-Q; AMS reports the held trucks with DLY-OD-10 to the FMS (ch-15-06) I-01 P → F
T+30 s Alarm "C01 not ready - chute blocked" on the AMS HMI; the controller acknowledges AMS HMI P's screen, site's controller
T+60 s Controller calls the plant control room; estimate recorded (ch-15-06) voice site
T+300 s FMS reassignment proposal on the FMS HMI; controller approves FMS HMI F's screen, site's controller

Three parties take part in five minutes, across three interfaces. The event works because each interface was tested with its meaning agreed: not ready means the same at both receivers (REQ-02-06-004), and a held truck reported on I-01 carries its reason and is shown by the FMS as held, not available.

6.2 Abnormal operation — a meaning that two suppliers read differently

Replay the event with one difference [A]: when the AMS was integrated with the incumbent FMS, the held state on I-01 was mapped to the FMS's own state "waiting". In this FMS, a waiting truck can be reassigned.

Time Event Interface Parties
T+0 s to T+30 s As in 6.1 I-06, I-07, I-01 site, P, F
T+300 s FMS proposal lists loaded ore trucks held at C01-Q as available for reassignment; its estimate of tonnes saved by reassigning is overstated I-01 (semantic) F's logic, P's data
T+330 s [A] Controller checks the proposal against the AMS view, as the procedure of ch-15-06 requires, and sees that the listed trucks are held and loaded AMS and FMS HMIs site
T+420 s [A] Controller rejects the reassign option and, with the Pit Supervisor, approves hold; defect raised against I-01 in the site–supplier issue log (ch-19-04) — site

The protective behaviour is unchanged: the AMS held the trucks at T+5 s whatever the FMS proposed, and it grants the C01 tipping bay only while the state is ready. What went wrong is a production decision proposed on wrong information and caught by a person. The fault sits between two suppliers, each of whom can show that its product meets its own specification — the semantic failure of ch-02-05 and the characteristic cost of a two-supplier FMS–AMS boundary. The remedy is an owner for I-01 who writes down what "held" means and tests it with this scenario (REQ-20-01-003).

6.3 Degraded operation — the plant interface without an owner

Replay the normal event with a different defect [A]: during the stoppage, the C01 status link fails. The AMS's plant gateway expects a message every 5 s and treats a state older than 15 s as lost (ch-15-03).

Time Event Interface Parties
T+0 s to T+300 s As in 6.1 — —
T+600 s [A] Last C01_STATUS message reaches the AMS; then nothing I-06 site, P
T+616 s [A] Gateway marks the status lost; HMI "C01 status lost - treated as not ready" (ch-15-03); trucks already held, so nothing changes on the road I-06 P
T+2700 s Plant is ready again (ch-15-06) — plant
T+2710 s In 6.1 the gateway would have confirmed READY here (ch-15-06); now nothing arrives I-06 —
T+3600 s [A] Link repaired; READY confirmed; staged release begins I-06 site, P

Only the plant's own messages make C01 ready; a phone call does not (ch-15-03). That is the fail-closed design working. The cost lies between T+2710 s and T+3600 s, when all 19 ore trucks are held (ch-15-06; DT01 to DT19 in baseline_allocation): 4.7 truck-hours [A] (section 12.2). In this replay the time went not on the repair but on finding whose interface it was — the plant network, the AMS supplier's gateway, or site IT between them. I-06 is cross-party in every archetype, so its owner is a question for the purchase (REQ-20-01-004).

6.4 The same pattern at EX02 — SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02

The second scenario shows the same structure at I-05. Its registered key events are: T+0 s the Shovel Operator requests DZ03; T+120 s the AMS reserves the work zone and holds the trucks; T+900 s DZ03 leaves, the work-zone reservation is released and loading resumes (ch-02-05 walks through it in detail). The path runs from the Shovel Operator (site) through DZ03's devices (P in OA-2, another party in ch-02-05's split) to the AMS (P). If the device supplier changes a message version without telling the AMS supplier, the AMS rejects DZ03's reports and the job continues under voice contact (ch-02-05, section 6.2). The trucks held from T+120 s to T+900 s are coded DLY-OD-04.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

These decisions are taken early, often before any supplier is chosen.

  • Where to draw the FMS–AMS boundary. Keeping the existing FMS (OA-2, OA-4) needs a site ICD for I-01 that fixes the meaning of every state the scenarios use — assigned, held with reason, cancelled, complete — and an integration test that runs SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN across it. Replacing the FMS (OA-1, OA-3) needs new dispatch logic and a new delay-code mapping, and breaks the KPI history unless the mapping is rebuilt.
  • Which interfaces to require open. The handbook's recommendation [R] is to require level O2 or O3 at I-01, I-05, I-06 and I-09. These are where the site has other parties (the FMS, device suppliers, the plant, Survey) and where switching costs collect. Inside the safety chain (I-02 to I-04, I-10), require instead a written allocation of evidence between the parties.
  • What data the site must be able to export. The AHS map, the configuration register, the event logs and the delay-coded history, in documented formats (ch-19-04 data-access provision). These are the assets that make the next decision a real choice.
  • What the comparison scores. Offerings may be compared on integration effort, openness, switching cost and fit with the site's fleet. They are not compared on whether they meet safety requirements. Every offering must meet them (REQ-20-01-008).

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

For SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN, the script traces the registered events through each archetype and counts the steps whose two ends belong to different parties:

Step Time OA-1 OA-2 OA-3 OA-4
C01 not ready to the AMS (I-06) T+0 s site → P site → P site → P site → P
C01 not ready to the FMS (I-07) T+0 s site → P site → F site → P site → F
Held trucks and DLY-OD-10 to the FMS (I-01) T+5 s inside P P → F inside P P → F
Reassignment proposal T+300 s inside P inside F inside P inside F
Party boundaries crossed 2 3 2 3

The plant boundary is crossed twice at T+0 s in every archetype; the FMS–AMS boundary is crossed at T+5 s only where the site keeps its FMS. The proposal at T+300 s is only as good as the held-truck information that crossed I-01 at T+5 s. When F and P are different parties, its meaning and latency belong in a site ICD, not in a design detail inside one product.

Two timing rules hold whatever the market structure. The AMS fails closed at the plant boundary: a missing, invalid or stale C01 state is not ready, and no truck is granted the tipping bay (REQ-02-06-004). The FMS proposes and the controller decides. No archetype gives the FMS authority to grant a reservation or to override a vehicle-side safety decision (ch-01-04). Commercial arrangements change who supplies each element. They do not change which element decides.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

In OA-2 the AHS Controller works with two suppliers' screens. The AMS HMI shows the alarm "C01 not ready - chute blocked" at T+30 s and the trucks held at C01-Q. The FMS HMI shows the proposal at T+300 s. In 6.2 the screens disagree: the FMS lists as reassignable the trucks the AMS shows held and loaded. In 6.3 the AMS HMI shows "C01 status lost - treated as not ready".

Three things help the controller whichever suppliers are involved:

  • One source per fact. The procedure states that the AMS view is the source of truth for where a truck is and whether it is held, and the FMS view for assignments and proposals.
  • Interface alarms that name the interface. "C01 status lost" names I-06. A controller who knows which interface failed knows whom to call.
  • A support card by interface, not by product. For I-01, I-06, I-08 and the other cross-party interfaces, the card names the accountable party and the number that answers on DAY and NIGHT shifts (ch-19-04).

8.2 Who decides what

Two decision sets matter here: the purchase, and the event on shift. The purchase is decided in the buying organisation of section 4.7.

On shift, the decisions of SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN stay where ch-15-06 puts them. The AMS holds the trucks. The FMS proposes. The AHS Controller approves or rejects, with the Pit Supervisor. Supplier staff diagnose interface faults, and the site's people decide (ch-19-04). No market arrangement moves a decision on shift to a supplier.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — operations review of an offering

The operations readiness lead (ch-19-04) runs these steps with the roles named, before an offering is chosen [H].

  1. Integration authority: draw the offering on FIG-20-01-01. Mark each layer with its supplying party and each interface with its accountable party and openness level. Any interface without an owner is a finding.
  2. AHS Controller and Pit Supervisor: walk SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN on the drawing (T+0, 5, 300 s). For each step, name the screen that shows it, the party that supplies the screen and whom to call when it fails, on DAY and on NIGHT shifts.
  3. Shovel Operator and Pit Supervisor: walk SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02 (T+0, 120, 900 s). Confirm how DZ03, the water trucks, the light vehicles and the grader are represented in the AMS and who supplies their devices.
  4. Maintenance Planner and Field Technician: list who maintains the kit, the devices and the base truck, and who decides on-site priorities (ch-19-04).
  5. Survey: confirm how the AHS map is built from site surveys and in which format the site receives it back.
  6. All: record each gap as a finding in the issue log with an owner. A gap in a safety requirement is a pass/fail finding for the offering, not a weighting (REQ-20-01-008).
  7. Operations readiness lead: report the findings to the decision of section 8.2 before the archetype is chosen.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

  • DLY-OD-10 (destination down) records the trucks held at C01-Q in SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN, including the 890 s of 6.3. The code classifies the time. Whether the time is attributed to the plant, the site or a supplier is a contract term (ch-19-07; delay-codes.yml coding_rules). In 6.3 the cause is an interface without an owner.
  • DLY-OD-04 (held for access permission or work zone) records the trucks held at EX02-Q in SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02.
  • KPI continuity. The time categories (Operating Delay, Downtime and so on) follow the GMG time usage model [F] (SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020), and the codes are handbook conventions [H]. If the FMS is replaced (OA-1, OA-3), the mapping from the new FMS's records to the site's delay codes must be rebuilt. Without it, Physical Availability, Use of Availability and the delay history break at the go-live date.

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • Operations leaders learn to read an offering as layers and interfaces and to ask, for every interface, who owns it and whom the control room calls at 03:00.
  • AHS Controllers learn which screen is the source of truth for which fact, and how interface alarms name the interface.
  • Everyone involved in the purchase learns the evidence-label rule for market figures (section 4.8).
  • Training is written in handbook terms, so that a change of supplier changes the screens, not the job.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-20-01-001 [H] Every market figure in a product, pre-sales or purchase document (market size, growth, deployment count, share, price) shall carry a source-register ID and an evidence label. A figure without a verified source used within its verification scope shall be labelled [TBV] and shall not be used as an input to a decision model. Rationale: a decision must not rest on a number nobody can check (section 4.8). Verification: inspection of the market-fact register against source-register.yml.

REQ-20-01-002 [H] An offering assessment shall map every supplied element to a value-chain layer and an architecture element, name the supplying party of each element, and list every cross-party interface with its accountable party, its ICD reference and its openness level. Rationale: interfaces between parties are where integration effort, defects and switching costs arise (sections 4.1 to 4.4). Verification: analysis by script (as figures/make_figures.py); inspection.

REQ-20-01-003 [H] For a brownfield site, an offering assessment shall state (a) whether the site's existing FMS is kept or replaced, (b) the ICD and integration test of the FMS–AMS interface with that FMS, including the meaning of held trucks and their reasons, and (c) the changes to dispatch, delay-code mapping and reporting if the FMS is replaced. Rationale: the FMS–AMS boundary is the main brownfield choice (sections 4.4, 6.2). Verification: inspection; integration test with the SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN timeline (T+0, 5, 300 s).

REQ-20-01-004 [H] An offering assessment shall name the supplying party and the accountable party of the plant status interface (ICD-C01-STATUS) at both the AMS and the FMS, and shall not assume any tipping at C01 without that interface in service. Rationale: an absent plant state is not ready (REQ-02-06-004); an interface without an owner costs production time (section 6.3). Verification: inspection of the supply-scope matrix; test with the status link interrupted.

REQ-20-01-005 [H] A statement of conformance to an interface standard shall name the parts and options implemented. It shall be accepted as evidence of conformance only. Interoperability at the site shall be verified by an integration test of the combination, and the statement shall not be used as evidence of safe machine operation. Rationale: conformance is necessary, not sufficient, for interoperability (ch-02-05); the published scope of ISO 23725:2024 leaves safe machine operation to agreement between suppliers [F] (section 4.3). Verification: inspection; integration test record.

REQ-20-01-006 [H] The site shall keep a switching register that lists its AHS map data, configuration items, ICDs, safety-case evidence, event logs and training records. For each it shall state the format, the data-access provision under which the site can obtain it, and what would have to be redone if the supplier changed. Rationale: switching costs decide whether the next purchase is a real choice (section 4.5). Verification: inspection; trial export of the AHS map and one day of event logs in the stated formats.

REQ-20-01-007 [H] An offering assessment shall list every class of manned and ancillary vehicle that enters the autonomous areas, with the devices it needs, the party that supplies them and the accountable party of the interface to the AMS. A class without a supplier for its devices shall be recorded as a gap. Rationale: every manned vehicle in an autonomous area carries a position reporting unit, a radio and a field terminal (master-instructions §4); the mixed fleet constrains the choice (section 4.9). Verification: inspection against equipment.yaml; walk-through of SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02.

REQ-20-01-008 [H] In any comparison of offerings, the site's safety requirements — including the independent remote stop, digital access permissions, fail-closed permissions and the response to fire detection and suppression — shall be pass/fail conditions that every offering must meet. They shall not be weighted criteria traded against price, productivity or any other criterion. Rationale: commercial or market choices never relax a safety function (sections 4.7, 7.1; ch-20-05). Verification: inspection of the comparison template and of each completed comparison.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Comparing offerings by feature lists. Offerings with the same features can differ in which interfaces cross a party boundary, and that decides integration effort and switching costs.
  • Taking conformance for interoperability. A conformance statement says what a product implements. Only a test of the combination at the site shows that it works with the mine's FMS, devices and plant.
  • Leaving the site-side interfaces out of scope. The plant, the network and the survey data are needed in every arrangement. An offering that is silent about them has left them to the site.
  • Opening a safety-chain interface by declaration. A published message format for I-02 or I-04 is not the joint hazard analysis and verification that opening it requires.
  • Ignoring switching costs until renewal. Data formats and access rights are cheapest to agree before the first contract.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Leaving operations out of the purchase. The questions in sections 4.7 and 8.3 are answered best by the people who will run the shift.
  • Quoting a market figure because everyone quotes it. A figure repeated often is still unverified until its source has been read within scope.
  • Assigning the mine a segment without its facts. Segments follow from drivers that can be checked at the site, not from the impression a site makes.
  • Calling the supplier for every fault. The first question is which interface failed; the support card should answer it.
  • Letting delay history break at a system change. If the FMS changes, the delay-code mapping must be rebuilt before go-live.

12. Worked examples

All numbers come from figures/make_figures.py or from the shared data. Archetypes, parties and the repair time in 12.2 are [A].

12.1 Cross-party interfaces by archetype. For the reference mine the script counts 4, 7, 6 and 8 cross-party interfaces for OA-1 to OA-4, of which 4, 6, 6 and 7 are new or changed. OA-1 is lowest because it replaces the FMS and the 40 manned trucks. OA-4 is highest because I-02 crosses between two suppliers inside the safety argument. Reading for a purchase: OA-2 and OA-4 put the integration work at I-01 and I-04, OA-1 and OA-3 put the change work in the FMS, and all four leave I-06 to I-09 to the site.

12.2 The cost of an interface without an owner. In the degraded case of 6.3, the plant is ready at T+2700 s and the gateway would have confirmed it at T+2710 s (ch-15-06). The link is repaired at T+3600 s [A]. All ore trucks are held by then: the baseline allocation assigns DT01 to DT10 to EX01 and DT11 to DT19 to EX02, 19 trucks (equipment.yaml), which matches the 19 held in ch-15-06. The extra stop is

Δ=n(trepair−tconfirm)=19×(3600−2710) s=16,910 truck-s≈4.7 truck-h

The staged release after the repair is not included. The number is small for one event. Its point is the cause: the 890 s were spent finding the owner of I-06, a question the purchase could have answered.

12.3 The switching inventory of the reference mine. From mine.yaml and equipment.yaml, the script counts 25 map nodes, 25 road segments with 15.27 km of road, 3 autonomous areas, 4 access points, 40 trucks with kits and stop receivers, 20 manned and ancillary vehicles with devices, 4 shovels with operator terminals and position sources, 3 remote-stop transmitters and 7 trained roles. A change of AMS supplier would touch all of them. The inventory does not price the change. It shows that the mine's dependence lies mostly in data, evidence and devices, not in the central software alone.

12.4 Recording a market figure. A draft business case states: "autonomous trucks have moved more than a billion tonnes". Recording it:

Field Entry
Claim Trucks fitted with autonomous haulage technology moved more than one billion tonnes across five of one company's sites since trials began in 2008
Source SRC-PUB-MINING-CO-AHS-RELEASE-2018 (a large mining company's 2018 media release)
Verification scope Full release read; company's statements as published
Label [F] for the company's statement about its own operations
Permitted use As one company's published statement, with its date
Not permitted As a market total, a share, a growth rate or a productivity input for another mine

The draft's wording "autonomous trucks have moved…" implies an industry total, and the source does not support that. The corrected sentence names the source as one company's statement. A figure for the whole market would need its own verified source. None is held, so any such figure is [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AHS-MARKET-DATA).

15. Key takeaways

  • An AHS offering is eight layers joined by ten interfaces. Market structure is decided at the interfaces.
  • Suppliers compete where interfaces are open and outside the safety argument. Inside it, split supply needs agreed joint evidence.
  • At a brownfield mine the FMS–AMS boundary is the main choice: integrate the existing FMS across a party boundary, or replace it and change how every shift dispatches and codes delays.
  • The plant, network and survey interfaces stay with the site in every arrangement. Each needs a named owner.
  • Switching costs collect in map data, configuration, safety-case evidence, devices and skills. Agree data access and openness before the first contract.
  • Segments follow from operational drivers that can be checked at the site. The shared data does not place the reference mine in one.
  • No market figure enters a document as fact without a verified source used within scope. Otherwise it is [TBV].
  • Safety requirements are pass/fail conditions for every offering, never weighted criteria.
