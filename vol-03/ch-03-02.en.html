1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Tuesday, 07:30, the weekly site review at the reference mine. Last night the crew ran a fire drill on R05 with DT17 standing in for a burning truck. Two documents lie on the table. The first is the drill report. At T+140 s, while the zone around DT17 held DT14 on the ramp, the AHS Controller tried to shrink the zone so that DT14 could pass on the far side. The HMI refused. The controller wrote: "zone could not be reduced — defect?" The second document is a page from last month's workshop with the Emergency Response Team (ERT). One line on it reads: The system shall quickly set up an appropriate exclusion zone around a burning truck and hold other trucks if possible. Engineering has been asked to "turn this into requirements" before the next AMS release.

The Pit Supervisor asks three questions. Was the refusal a fault, or the system doing what it was asked to do? Can the workshop sentence be tested, and by whom? And if engineering writes "within 10 s" and "150 m", where do those numbers come from?

The questions behind the scene:

  • What is the difference between a need written by the ERT, a requirement that engineering can verify, and a design that satisfies it?
  • Who must do what — the onboard fire system, the VCS, the AMS, the controller — and how is that written down so that a central permission never overrides a decision taken on the truck?
  • How do we know that a requirement is good enough: unambiguous, verifiable, atomic, consistent, feasible?
  • How does an operational report like last night's become either a defect against an existing requirement or a new requirement?
  • How are procedures and training kept in step when a requirement changes?

This chapter covers:

  • the handbook's requirement format and its parts (FIG-03-02-02), and the hierarchy from stakeholder need to system and element requirements (FIG-03-02-01);
  • kinds of requirement — functional, performance, interface, safety-related, operational — and why every number needs a source or an [A];
  • quality attributes, and what an automated checker can and cannot find, run on the handbook's own 1,086 requirements (LAB-03-02-01);
  • deriving and allocating requirements from a scenario timeline: SCN-FIRE-DT17 for the exclusion zone and traffic holding, SCN-POSCOM-STALE for fail-closed permissions;
  • the path from an operational report to a defect or a new requirement, acceptance criteria a controller can observe, and the link from requirements to procedures and training.

The handbook's requirements are the worked corpus. Every chapter of the handbook ends its section 9 with requirements in one format. This chapter uses those of Volumes 01, 02, 04 – 16 and 18 (the corpus volumes). LAB-03-02-01 reads all of them with ahs_sim.traceability 0.20.0 and checks them. Where the checker reports something about one of these requirements, it is a teaching observation about how real requirement sets look, not a defect report; this chapter refers to them without changing them.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Write system requirements in the handbook format (REQ-VV-CC-NNN, evidence label, rationale, verification method) for the exclusion-zone behaviour in SCN-FIRE-DT17.
  2. Derive fail-closed requirements for access permissions from SCN-POSCOM-STALE, and allocate each to the AMS, the field terminal or people.
  3. Evaluate requirement quality (unambiguous, verifiable, atomic, consistent, feasible) and rewrite defective requirements.
  4. Distinguish functional, performance, interface, safety-related and operational requirements, and explain why numeric limits in requirements must be sourced or tagged [A].

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Review a set of operational requirements (alarms, procedures, roles) and identify ones that cannot be verified or that conflict with current site practice.
  2. Explain how an operational incident report at the reference mine becomes either a defect against an existing requirement or a new requirement.
  3. Write an acceptance criterion that a controller can observe, such as what the HMI shows when LV07's grant expires.
  4. Explain why procedures and training material should be linked to requirements, so that a requirement change triggers a procedure review.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

An autonomous haulage system is specified by many hands. The vendor of the vehicle writes what the truck does; the supplier of the AMS writes what the central system does; the mine writes its site procedures; the network team writes coverage targets; and the people who work the system — the AHS Controller, the Pit Supervisor, the Field Technician, the ERT — carry needs in their heads that nobody has written at all. ch-03-01 showed how to collect those needs. This chapter is about what happens next: turning them into statements that someone can build, someone else can verify, and everyone can read in the same way.

Three things go wrong at the reference mine when this step is skipped or rushed.

Needs are written as solutions. "Hold every truck within 150 m of a truck on fire within 10 s" sounds precise. But 150 m is a design choice about one geometry on one ramp (ch-04-07 uses 150 m [A] on R05 and makes the geometry configurable), and 10 s is a key-event time of the registered scenario, not a measured capability. Written into a requirement without a source, both numbers become commitments nobody can justify.

Responsibility is left unsaid. "Trucks shall be rerouted around the zone and/or held" names no actor. Is it the AMS, the FMS, the controller, or the Pit Supervisor who decides? In SCN-FIRE-DT17 the answer matters: rerouting via R24 sends loaded trucks across Intersection V, a hazard-log item of its own (HZ-11 in ch-04-01). ch-04-07 answers it: the AMS presents the options and reroutes only on a recorded decision of the Pit Supervisor.

Failure behaviour is missing. A need says what should happen when things work. The hard requirements are about what must happen when a message is late, duplicated or lost. In SCN-POSCOM-STALE a grant reaches LV07's field terminal 30 s after it was issued. The workshop draft says "the field terminal shall reject old grants" — but how old is old, who decides, what does the driver see, and what happens to the area lock afterwards?

The scale of the problem is visible in the handbook itself. The 128 chapters of the corpus volumes define 1,086 requirements, and every one of them has an ID, an evidence label, a rationale and a verification line. That is a disciplined corpus. Run through an automated checker (section 4.5), it still shows warnings on most requirements: compound statements, passive voice without an actor, verification methods the checker does not recognise. Those warnings are not failures of the chapters; they show what a checker can find, what it cannot, and where a human reviewer must decide. A mine that starts with workshop notes instead of a reviewed corpus starts much further back.

3. Terminology and foundations

Stakeholder need. What a person or organisation needs from the system, in their words (ch-03-01). "We approach the burning truck only when nothing can move into us" (ERT). A need is the input; it is not verified directly.

Requirement (glossary requirement). A statement of a needed capability or constraint that can be verified. In the handbook it has an ID, an evidence label, a statement with shall, a rationale and a verification line [H].

Design solution. One way to meet a requirement. "A circle of 150 m around the truck's position" is a design; "a zone of configurable geometry around the truck that closes every segment it touches" is the requirement. Keeping them apart lets engineering change the design without reopening the need.

System requirement, element requirement. A system requirement applies to the AHS as a whole (Volume 01 states several, e.g. REQ-01-04-003: no central command moves a truck under a vehicle-side stop). An element requirement is allocated to one element of the architecture: FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS, independent safety functions (ISF), onboard safety-related systems (OSS), manned-vehicle devices (MVD), or people (ch-02-03).

Derived requirement [H]. A requirement that does not come straight from a need but follows from a higher requirement and a design choice. "The VCS shall stop the truck without the ADS if the ADS does not respond in time" (in REQ-04-07-002) follows from the system rule that the stop must not depend on the driving software.

Allocation (glossary function-allocation). The assignment of a requirement to the element or role that must satisfy it. An unallocated requirement has no owner and no test.

Interface requirement [H]. A requirement on what crosses a boundary between elements: message content, timing, order, versioning. REQ-02-07-002 (every message carries an idempotency key and a sequence number) is one.

Verification method (glossary verification-method). How a requirement is shown to be met. The handbook names five [H]: test (run the system under defined conditions and compare with a criterion), analysis (calculation or model), inspection (examine a document, code, configuration or record), demonstration (show the function in operation without detailed measurement, e.g. in a drill), and review (a structured reading by competent people). ch-03-07 and Volume 18 treat their selection and levels.

Acceptance criterion (glossary acceptance-criterion). The observable condition under which a requirement counts as met in a given test or check — for operations readers, best written as what someone can see.

Requirements baseline [H]. The set of requirements, each at a stated version, agreed at a point in time. Changes after that point go through change control (ch-03-08).

Evidence label. [H], [A], [F], [R] and the others of master-instructions §5. In requirements the label says where the requirement comes from. Almost all handbook requirements are [H] (handbook convention); the recommendations are [R] and use should.

Requirements-engineering standards and guidance exist that define requirement quality characteristics and specification content; the handbook does not state what any of them requires [TBV] (SRC-PUB-REQUIREMENTS-ENGINEERING). Safety-related development standards such as the IEC 61508 series (SRC-STD-IEC61508) and ISO 17757:2019 (SRC-STD-ISO17757) address safety requirements as a topic; both are registered at catalogue level only, and their clause content is [TBV]. ch-04-03 maps the standards landscape. Nothing in this chapter is derived from a standard clause; the format, rules and examples are handbook conventions [H].

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The handbook requirement format

Every requirement in the handbook is a block of four parts [H]:

REQ-VV-CC-NNN [label]
<statement with "shall", one or more lines>
Rationale: <why — the section, scenario or decision it rests on>
Verification: <method(s), with the test, lab or record that provides the evidence>

FIG-03-02-02 dissects REQ-04-07-005, the exclusion-zone requirement of ch-04-07, quoted unchanged.

Each part answers one question:

  • ID — which requirement. VV-CC is the chapter that defines it, NNN its number. IDs are stable: a withdrawn requirement is deprecated, never renumbered.
  • Evidence label — where it comes from. A number inside a requirement needs a source or [A].
  • Actor — who must do it. "The AMS" makes the allocation explicit.
  • Condition — when it applies: a truck reporting fire detection or suppression activation.
  • Response — what must happen: a zone of configurable geometry; every touched segment closed.
  • Constraint — what must never happen: no new route or granted path through the zone until it is cleared.
  • Link — what it depends on: the zone is reduced only through the clearance of REQ-04-07-009.
  • Rationale — why. A reviewer who wants to change the requirement must be able to see what it protects.
  • Verification — how we will know: the method and where the evidence comes from (LAB-04-07-01, a demonstration on the HMI).

The checker of LAB-03-02-01 finds no error in REQ-04-07-005 and two warnings: three modal verbs in one statement (compound) and a passive "shall not be reduced" without an actor. Both are deliberate in ch-04-07 — the controller's freedom to enlarge and the ban on reducing belong together — and both are exactly what a reviewer should look at.

4.2 From need to system to element requirement

FIG-03-02-01 shows the hierarchy for the fire on DT17. Needs come from people; system requirements state what the AHS as a whole must do; element requirements are allocated to one element or role. The arrows are the trace links of ch-03-03: a need is refined into requirements; a system requirement derives element requirements.

Three points stand out.

Vehicle-side decisions are allocated to the vehicle. The ERT's need — nothing moves into us — is met first by requirements on the truck: the fire system detects and actuates on board (REQ-04-07-001), the VCS stops and holds the truck (REQ-04-07-002), the ADS treats a resume as an offer it can refuse (REQ-02-03-003). The central requirements are written as constraints on the centre: REQ-04-07-004 forbids any AMS or FMS command that clears the onboard safe state. This is how a principle of the handbook's reference architecture (ch-01-04 section 4.6) — central permissions never override vehicle-side safety decisions — appears in a requirement set: as shall not on central elements and shall on the vehicle, never as a central shall that the vehicle must obey.

A level can be missing. The containment of the event — zone, hold or reroute, clearance by people — has no system-level requirement with its own ID; ch-04-07 states it directly per element. That is a legitimate choice for a chapter, and it is also the kind of gap a trace review notices (ch-03-03): the ERT's need reaches the AMS requirements through prose, not through a link.

People are an element. REQ-04-07-009 — clearance by the AHS Controller on the Pit Supervisor's authorisation — is allocated to people. It is verified by inspection of the clearance record and by demonstration in drills, not by a software test. A requirement set that only contains software requirements has silently allocated these decisions to nobody.

4.3 Kinds of requirement

The handbook does not tag requirements by kind, but reading the corpus by kind is useful, because each kind fails in its own way and is verified in its own way.

Kind What it constrains Handbook example Typical verification Typical defect
Functional What an element does REQ-04-07-005: the AMS establishes the exclusion zone Test Missing condition or missing failure case
Performance How fast, how far, how often REQ-04-07-002: the VCS stops the truck if the ADS does not respond within a configurable time [A] Test, analysis A number with no source
Interface What crosses a boundary REQ-02-07-002: idempotency key and sequence number on every message Inspection of the ICD, test Meaning left to each side
Safety-related A behaviour that controls a hazard REQ-10-06-008: no permission delays or cancels a remote stop Analysis, test Written as a central command the vehicle must obey
Operational What people and procedures do REQ-02-07-006: radio read-back and a confirmed permission on the terminal before entry Inspection, demonstration Verified only on paper

A requirement can belong to more than one kind. REQ-02-07-003 is functional (withdraw an unacknowledged grant), an interface requirement (the terminal returns to "not granted") and safety-related (it controls HZ-03).

Numbers in requirements. A number in a requirement is a commitment. In the handbook it must carry a source or [A] [H]. The preferred pattern is configurable: the requirement fixes the behaviour and names the parameter ("within a configurable time [A]"); the value is set, justified and recorded per site. ch-02-07 and ch-10-06 follow this for the acknowledgement deadline and the message age limit (30 s and 15 s in the labs, both [A]). Three requirements of the corpus contain a number with a unit and no [A] (section 4.5). They are not careless: REQ-15-03-001 and REQ-15-03-002 restate values of ICD-C01-STATUS 1.0, the interface control document fixed at version 1.0 in ch-02-06, and REQ-07-01-003 quotes the handbook's own reference cycle times — sourced inside the handbook and labelled [H]. The checker cannot know that; the reviewer can. That is why the rule is a warning, not an error.

Never invent a safety threshold. A stopping distance, a zone radius, a timeout or a speed limit that protects people is a safety parameter. If it is not derived in the handbook (as stopping distance is in ch-04-05) or in a verified source, it is [A] and illustrative, and the requirement must say that the site sets and justifies it.

4.4 Quality attributes and what a checker can find

A good requirement has a short list of properties [H]:

Attribute Meaning Automated rule in LAB-03-02-01 What only a human can check
Identified Unique, stable ID in the right chapter id-format, id-chapter Whether it duplicates another requirement in other words
Labelled Evidence label present and valid tag Whether the label is true
Unambiguous One reading for everyone vague (a word list) Words outside the list: "old", "promptly", "near"
Atomic One requirement, one shall compound Whether the parts belong together
Allocated An actor that must do it passive (no "by …"), no-shall Whether the actor is the right one
Verifiable A method that can show it is met no-verification, unknown-method Whether the method can actually decide pass or fail
Justified A reason a reviewer can weigh no-rationale Whether the reason is the real one
Sourced Numbers traceable or [A] number-untagged Whether the value is credible
Consistent No conflict with another requirement — All of it
Feasible Can be built and run at the site — All of it

The checker has eleven rules (DEFAULT_RULES), six with severity error and five warning [H]. Errors are structural: no ID, no label, no shall, no rationale, no verification. Warnings point to something a reviewer must look at. Two attributes — consistency and feasibility — have no rule at all. They need people who know the system and the site.

Every rule is switchable. LAB-03-02-01 uses this to model two stages: at a workshop, drafts have no ID yet, so the ID rules are off; at baseline, every rule is on and an ID is mandatory.

4.5 The checker on the handbook's own requirements

LAB-03-02-01 parses the 128 English chapter files of the corpus volumes (01, 02, 04 – 16 and 18). It finds 1,086 requirements, none unparsed and none duplicated; two lines that begin with a requirement ID are prose mentions, not blocks. The Japanese files give the same 1,086 IDs. 1,074 requirements are labelled [H] and 12 [R].

Verification methods. Test is named 745 times, inspection 743, demonstration 125, analysis 122 and review 45. Most requirements name two methods (664), 403 name one, 15 name three and one names four. Three name none of the five: REQ-15-07-002, REQ-15-07-008 and REQ-15-07-011, whose verification lines name a lab, a figure, observation sheets and a drill instead.

Quality. No requirement has an error. 203 of 1,086 (18.7 %) have no warning at all. The warnings:

Rule Requirements What it means here
compound 788 More than one modal verb in the statement
passive 372 "shall be recorded / reported / used …" with no "by …"
unknown-method 188 A verification clause names none of the five methods
vague 4 REQ-04-06-005 and REQ-05-02-002 "sufficient", REQ-04-08-007 "normally", REQ-13-02-006 "robust"
number-untagged 3 REQ-07-01-003 (1564.1 s, 1800.1 s), REQ-15-03-001 (5 s), REQ-15-03-002 (15 s)

Compound (788) is mostly a choice, not a defect. The chapter template's own example has two shalls: establish the zone, and do not issue routes through it. The handbook writes a behaviour and its prohibition as one requirement, because they are verified together and must not drift apart. Of the 788, 180 contain a shall not; 515 have two shalls, 209 three, 42 four and 16 five. The ones that deserve a second look are the 66 compound requirements whose shalls have different architecture elements as subjects (for example REQ-02-03-006 and REQ-02-04-001): they are allocated to two owners in one sentence, and a change by one owner reopens the other's requirement. Splitting those is a recommendation for new work [R], not a correction of the existing chapters.

Passive (372) is dominated by record-keeping: "shall be recorded" (64), "reported" (23), "used" (16). In most of these the recording element is obvious from the chapter — but not from the sentence. A requirement read alone, as it is in a test plan or a contract, must name its actor.

Unknown method (188) shows how the handbook verifies people requirements. The words found in the unrecognised clauses are "exercise" (70 requirements), "drill" (28), a bare lab ID such as "LAB-15-07-01" (22), "audit" (18), "observation" (4), "replay" (3) and 43 others ("automated comparison", "fault injection in SIL", "interview of shift staff" …). Exercises, drills and audits are real verification activities for procedures and roles; they map most closely to demonstration and inspection. A site requirement set should either map them explicitly or add them to its list of methods [R]; the handbook keeps its five methods — a convention of the handbook, not a rule for every site.

Allocation by a simple heuristic (the subject of each shall) finds the AMS in 282 requirements, the FMS in 76, people in 68, the ADS in 63, the VCS in 31, ISF in 22, MVD in 14 and OSS in 9; the AHS Controller is named in 43. 593 requirements are not allocated by the heuristic at all, because their subject is "the truck", "the HMI", "site procedures", "every record" or "the interface". That number is a limit of the heuristic, not of the handbook: the chapters allocate in their section 5 tables. It is also a reminder: a requirement whose subject is "the truck" leaves open whether the VCS, the ADS or the onboard fire system must act.

The lesson for a site is not a percentage. A carefully written corpus with zero errors still carries hundreds of warnings that need a person. A checker makes the review faster; it does not replace it.

4.6 Deriving requirements from a scenario timeline

A registered scenario gives a timeline of key events with actors. Each event is a place where something must be true, something must happen, and something must not happen. A simple method [H]:

  1. For each key event, write who acts and what they observe.
  2. Ask what must happen (response), under which condition, within which time — and whether the time is a key-event time of the scenario (illustrative) or a measured capability.
  3. Ask what must never happen (constraint). Most safety-related requirements live here.
  4. Ask what happens if the event is late, lost, duplicated or wrong (failure behaviour). Fail closed.
  5. Allocate each statement to an element or role. If two actors appear, split.
  6. Look for an existing requirement before writing a new one. In the handbook, LAB-03-02-01 lists the requirements that cite a scenario: 19 cite SCN-FIRE-DT17 and 7 cite SCN-POSCOM-STALE.

Sections 6.2 and 6.3 apply the method to both scenarios.

4.7 From an operational report to a defect or a new requirement

Operations produces reports every shift: alarms, holds, incidents, drill findings, near misses. Each one that concerns the AHS's behaviour must end in one of four places [H]:

Question, in order If yes Result
1. Did the system do something an existing requirement forbids, or fail to do something it requires? Name the requirement ID(s) Defect against those requirements
2. Did the system do what its requirements say, but the result is unacceptable for people or production? Name the requirement and the need it fails Change of an existing requirement (ch-03-08)
3. Is the behaviour not covered by any requirement? Name the scenario and the gap New requirement (and possibly a new scenario)
4. Did people do something the procedure does not say, or the procedure does not fit the system? Name the procedure step Procedure or training finding

Last night's report is answered by question 1 no and question 2 perhaps: the HMI refused to reduce the zone because REQ-04-07-005 forbids reducing it below its configured geometry without the clearance of REQ-04-07-009. The system was right. Whether the controller's need — let a held truck pass on the far side — deserves a change is a question for the next review, and it goes there with the requirement IDs attached. A report that says only "zone could not be reduced — defect?" cannot be answered without someone finding the requirement first; ch-03-03 shows how a trace graph makes that search a lookup.

4.8 Acceptance criteria a controller can observe

A requirement says what the system must do; an acceptance criterion says what someone will see when it does. For operations, the most useful criteria are written in terms of the HMI, the field terminal and the radio [H]:

  • Situation — the state before (LV07 inside the area under an active permission, PERM-LV07-0001).
  • Event — what happens (the validity window ends).
  • Observable result — what the controller and the requester see, and within which time.
  • What must not be seen — the forbidden outcome.

For LV07's expiring grant, ch-10-06 gives the behaviour (REQ-10-06-003: the permission moves to the expired-inside state, the area stays locked, new entries are refused, the permission owner is alarmed; no timer releases the permission or unlocks the area) and the HMI content (REQ-10-06-009). An acceptance criterion a controller can check reads:

With LV07 inside AA-North under PERM-LV07-0001, when its validity window ends: the HMI shows the permission as "expired — person may be inside", raises an alarm to the permission owner, still shows the area locked and the trucks held; the field terminal shows that the permission has expired. At no time does the area show as unlocked or a held truck as released until the requester has reported out and been confirmed outside.

Every phrase in it can be observed by a person at a console during a site acceptance test. ch-18-01 calls this an end-state criterion; the last sentence is an invariant.

4.9 Linking procedures and training to requirements

Many requirements are met partly by people. REQ-02-07-006 requires site procedures to demand both the radio read-back and a confirmed permission; REQ-04-07-009 requires the clearance to be done by the controller on the Pit Supervisor's authorisation. If the requirement changes and the procedure does not, the system and the people drift apart — the most dangerous kind of inconsistency, because both sides believe they are compliant.

The remedy is a link [H]: every procedure step and every training item that implements a requirement names its ID (REQ-03-02-006). A change to the requirement then lists the procedure steps and training items to review, as a change to a test case lists the requirements to re-check (ch-03-03, ch-03-08). The link also works the other way: a procedure step with no requirement behind it is either a local practice worth writing down as a requirement, or a step nobody needs.

4.10 Trade-offs and limits

  • Atomic versus readable. One shall per requirement is easier to allocate and test; a behaviour with its prohibition is easier to read and keep consistent. The handbook chooses readability within one owner, and atomicity across owners [R].
  • Precise versus premature. A number makes a requirement testable; an unjustified number makes it wrong. Configurable parameters with [A] values and a site justification are the middle way.
  • Checker versus reviewer. Word lists miss words ("old" passes in DRAFT-03-02-05) and flag legitimate ones ("sufficient evidence of integrity" in REQ-05-02-002 is precise in context). The checker shortens a review; it never approves a requirement.
  • Model limits. The parser and the allocation heuristic of ahs_sim.traceability are educational: they read the handbook's own format, not natural language in general.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — the requirement life at the reference mine

In normal work a requirement moves through five steps [H]: a need is captured with its source (ch-03-01); a draft is written in the format of section 4.1; the workshop check runs (ID rules off); a review with engineering, safety and operations resolves every warning or records why it stays; the requirement enters the baseline with an ID, and the baseline check runs with every rule on. From then on it changes only through change control (ch-03-08), and every change raises the trace links that depend on it (ch-03-03).

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-FIRE-DT17 (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

The registered key events are at T+0, 5, 10, 15, 60 and 600 s. T+7.4 s and T+120 s are the intermediate times used in ch-04-07 [A]. Read down the "Allocated to" column: the first three events belong to the truck, the middle to the AMS and the FMS, the last to people. A workshop that writes "the system shall" for all of them has hidden that structure.

DRAFT-03-02-04 is the most dangerous of the four, and the checker sees only its form. It says when the zone is cleared but not who clears it or on what evidence. A literal implementation would clear the zone when the fire system reports "fire out" — a central action releasing traffic into the former hazard area on a sensor reading. The rewrite makes it a people decision:

RW-03-02-04: The AHS Controller shall clear an exclusion zone only on the authorisation of the Pit Supervisor, recorded in the AMS with the ERT's declaration that the scene is safe.

The rewrites of the fire drafts (RW-03-02-01 to RW-03-02-04 in the lab) pass every workshop rule. Compared with the existing REQ-04-07-005, -006 and -009 they are narrower — the existing requirements also cover enlargement of the zone, reporting to the FMS and the staged release of held trucks — and that comparison is part of the exercise: a rewrite that is cleaner than the existing requirement may also say less.

6.3 Abnormal operation — SCN-POSCOM-STALE: fail-closed permission requirements

The checker on the permission drafts:

  • DRAFT-03-02-05, "The field terminal shall reject old grants", carries the malformed ID "REQ-10-6-12" — the only rule it triggers is id-format. "Old" is not in the vague-word list.   • DRAFT-03-02-06, "Grants shall be acknowledged as soon as possible by the driver", is flagged as vague but not as passive, because it has an actor ("by the driver"). It is still the wrong requirement: the safety lies not in a fast acknowledgement but in never entering on the radio alone.
  • DRAFT-03-02-07 bundles two owners' behaviours and an untagged 30 s; the rewrite splits it into the AMS's withdrawal of an unacknowledged grant (RW-03-02-07) and the AMS's lock kept until the controller confirms (RW-03-02-08).
  • DRAFT-03-02-08 uses should in an [H] requirement (an error, no-shall), "normally" (vague) and "observation in operation" as its verification (unknown method).

Allocation. The method of section 4.6 allocates each rewrite by hand; the lab compares this with the heuristic. They agree for four of the nine rewrites (RW-03-02-01, -05, -07, -09). For RW-03-02-02, -03, -04 and -08 the heuristic finds the element but misses a role named in an object position ("on a decision of the Pit Supervisor"). For RW-03-02-06, "The requester shall enter … only after both the radio read-back and a confirmed permission", it finds nothing, because "requester" is neither an element nor a mine.yaml role. The manual allocation is people. That is the point of the exercise: fail-closed permissions are a chain across three owners — the AMS, the field terminal and the person in LV07 — and each link needs its own requirement and its own verification.

6.4 Degraded operation — the same fire with the link lost

ch-04-07 section 6.3 runs SCN-FIRE-DT17 with DT17's link down when the fire starts. The truck still detects, actuates and stops at T+5 s; the AMS sees only communication lost and creates no zone until the event reaches it, at T+90 s in LAB-04-07-02 [A]. For requirements this is the test of allocation. Every requirement allocated to the truck still holds; every central requirement is late. Two requirements exist only because of this case: the vehicle-side reaction waits for no central message (REQ-01-04-006), and safety-related events raised while the link is down are stored and forwarded with their original time (REQ-02-01-006). A requirement set written only from the nominal timeline would contain neither.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Behaviour fixed, value configurable. Write the behaviour and name the parameter; keep values out of the statement unless they are sourced. RW-03-02-01 says "within the configured time [A] of receiving the fire event"; the site's parameter record gives the value and its justification. A test then checks two things: that the parameter exists and is honoured, and that the site's value is within what the analysis supports.

One owner per requirement. Split where the shalls have different subjects (DRAFT-03-02-07). Keep together where a behaviour and its prohibition belong to one owner (REQ-04-07-005). The 66 compound requirements of the corpus that span two elements are the pattern to avoid in new work.

Constraints on the centre, duties on the vehicle. Where a requirement touches a vehicle-side safety decision, write it as shall not for central elements (REQ-04-07-004) and as shall for the vehicle (REQ-04-07-002). Never as a central command the truck must follow.

Failure behaviour in the requirement, not in the design notes. For every message, timer and person in a chain, write what happens when it is late, lost, duplicated or wrong. SCN-POSCOM-STALE exists because this was once left to the implementation.

Name people as actors. "The AHS Controller shall clear …" can be inspected, trained and drilled. "The zone shall be cleared" cannot.

7.2 Failure modes and mitigations

Vague term ("quickly", "appropriate", "old") Each side implements its own reading; tests pass on both Word-list check plus review; replace with a configurable parameter or an observable criterion
No actor (passive) Nobody builds it, or two elements both do Name the actor; check allocation against the section 5 table of the chapter
Compound across owners A change by one owner silently changes another's requirement Split; one owner per requirement
Invented number A safety parameter nobody can justify [A] plus a site parameter record; source register for any claim
Central shall over a vehicle-side decision A permission or command defeats an onboard stop Review rule: no requirement allocated to the FMS or AMS conditions a vehicle-side decision (REQ-03-02-004)

Requirement in a procedure only A system change breaks it unnoticed Write it as a requirement with an ID; link the procedure to it
Contradiction between chapters or documents Two compliant behaviours that cannot both happen Review by scenario, not by document; trace review (ch-03-03)

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

The SCN-FIRE-DT17 timeline sets the AMS's containment at T+10 s. That time is a key event of a registered scenario, not a measured capability. If engineering adopts a time limit, it must decompose it across the chain: the fire system's report to the VCS and ADS, the truck's message to the AMS over the site network, and the AMS's processing, each with its own budget, each [A] until measured (section 12, example 2). The decomposition is itself a set of interface requirements, and each belongs to a different owner.

Clocks matter in requirements as much as in code. ch-02-07 requires expiry and acknowledgement deadlines to be evaluated on the issuer's clock, with the receiver's age check as additional protection (REQ-02-07-004). A requirement that says "a grant older than 15 s" without saying whose clock measures the age is ambiguous in exactly the case it was written for: a terminal whose clock has drifted.

Interface requirements carry versions. An ICD value restated in a requirement (REQ-15-03-001 restates the 5 s heartbeat of ICD-C01-STATUS 1.0) must change when the ICD's major version changes; the requirement should name the ICD version it depends on, as that one does.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

Requirements are invisible in the control room until they are written as acceptance criteria. Then they become things a controller can check:

  • Fire on DT17 (T+15 s): one view with the truck, its stop and suppression state, the zone, the closed segments and the trucks held (REQ-04-10-006); the zone cannot be reduced without clearance (REQ-04-07-005) — which is what the controller met in last night's drill.
  • Stale grant for LV07 (T+31 s): the permission shows as not acknowledged, the area as locked until checked, and an alarm asks the permission owner to resolve it (REQ-10-06-007, REQ-10-06-009). The field terminal shows not granted.
  • Logs: every refusal the HMI gives should name the rule behind it. "Zone cannot be reduced: clearance required (REQ-04-07-009)" turns a suspected defect into an explained behaviour [R].

8.3 Procedure excerpt — reporting an AHS observation so that it can be triaged

Keyed to the drill on the timeline of section 6.2 [H].

  1. AHS Controller (during the event, T+140 s [A]): when the HMI refuses an action or does something unexpected, note the time, the truck and area IDs, the action tried and the exact HMI text. Do not repeat the action to "test" it.
  2. AHS Controller (before the end of shift): write the observation with the scenario ID if one fits (here SCN-FIRE-DT17), the requirement ID if the HMI or the procedure names one, the AMS version on the HMI's about page, and the log extract (event, time, source). Hand it over at shift handover as an open item.
  3. Pit Supervisor (next working day): check the report is complete; add the production effect (trucks held, delay codes).
  4. Systems engineer (function) [A]: apply the four questions of section 4.7. Record the result — defect, change, new requirement, or procedure / training finding — with the requirement IDs.
  5. Systems engineer and Pit Supervisor: for a change or new requirement, write the operational need in the controller's words, then the draft requirement; run the workshop check.
  6. AHS Controller who reported: receive the outcome. Last night's report closes as no defect: REQ-04-07-005 and REQ-04-07-009, with the controller's need (pass a held truck on the far side of a zone) logged for the next review.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

Requirements decide how time is coded, because they decide what holds a truck. During SCN-FIRE-DT17, DT17 is DLY-DN-01 from T+0 s and trucks held by the zone are DLY-OD-14 (ch-04-07 section 8.4). In last night's drill the same holds are DLY-OD-04: by the handbook's rule, trucks held for a planned drill are DLY-OD-04, and DLY-OD-14 is only for the exclusion zone of a real incident (delay-codes.yml, coding rules). During SCN-POSCOM-STALE, trucks held because the area stays locked until checked are DLY-OD-04 (held for access permission or work zone). That time is the price of REQ-10-06-007: a fail-closed requirement costs production minutes every time it acts, and the delay record is how the site sees that cost. A proposal to "release the lock automatically after 5 minutes" is a proposal to change a safety-related requirement, and goes through change control with the delay data attached.

Requirement-set indicators worth reporting at the site review [R]: requirements without acceptance criteria an operator can observe; operational reports triaged within the agreed time; reports closed as "no defect" (a high share means the HMI does not explain its refusals).

8.5 Training and competency implications

Controllers and supervisors learn to read a requirement in the handbook format and to find it from an HMI refusal. Training items are linked to the requirements they teach (REQ-03-02-006), so that a requirement change appears in the training plan. Engineers learn to write needs back in the operators' words before writing shall.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-03-02-001 [H] Every requirement of the AHS programme shall carry a unique identifier of the form REQ-VV-CC-NNN, an evidence label, a statement with one actor, a rationale, and a verification line that names at least one of the methods test, analysis, inspection, demonstration or review together with the source of its evidence. Rationale: an unidentified, unlabelled or unverifiable requirement cannot be traced, allocated or accepted (sections 4.1, 4.4). Verification: test (LAB-03-02-01 baseline check of every rule on the requirement set); inspection of the baseline.

REQ-03-02-002 [H] A numeric value in a requirement statement shall name its source or carry the label [A] and refer to a site parameter record that states the value and its justification. Rationale: an unsourced number is an unjustified commitment; safety parameters are site matters (sections 4.3, 7.1). Verification: test (LAB-03-02-01 rule number-untagged); inspection of the parameter record.

REQ-03-02-003 [H] The systems engineer function [A] shall allocate every system requirement to at least one of the FMS, the AMS, the ADS, the VCS, the independent safety functions, the onboard safety-related systems, the manned-vehicle devices or a named role of the site. Rationale: an unallocated requirement has no owner and no test; people are an element (sections 4.2, 6.3). Verification: inspection of the allocation table against the requirement set; review with operations.

REQ-03-02-004 [H] The requirements review shall reject any requirement allocated to the FMS or the AMS that clears, delays, conditions or overrides a vehicle-side safety decision or an independent safety function. Rationale: central permissions never override vehicle-side safety decisions (ch-01-04 section 4.6, principle 1; section 4.2). Verification: review of every requirement allocated to the FMS or the AMS; inspection of the review record.

REQ-03-02-005 [H] The systems engineer function [A] shall classify every operational report about AHS behaviour as a defect against named requirement IDs, a change of a named requirement, a new requirement, or a procedure or training finding, recorded with the scenario ID and the log reference. Rationale: a report without a classification and references cannot be acted on (sections 4.7, 8.3). Verification: inspection of the report register; demonstration with the drill report of section 1.1.

REQ-03-02-006 [H] Every procedure step and every training item that implements a requirement shall name the requirement ID. Rationale: a requirement change must reach the procedures and training that carry it out (section 4.9). Verification: inspection of procedures and training material; analysis of the trace links (LAB-03-03-01).

REQ-03-02-007 [H] Every requirement allocated to people or to the HMI shall have an acceptance criterion stating what the AHS Controller or the requester observes and what they must not observe. Rationale: operations accepts what it can see; a criterion in the operators' terms is testable at site acceptance (section 4.8). Verification: inspection of the acceptance criteria; demonstration at site acceptance.

REQ-03-02-008 [H] The change control board function [A] shall admit a requirement to the requirements baseline only when the quality check reports no error-severity issue for it and every warning carries a recorded disposition. Rationale: errors are structural; warnings need a reviewer's decision, not silence (sections 4.4, 4.5). Verification: test (LAB-03-02-01 error rules on the baseline); inspection of the dispositions.

These eight requirements pass every rule of the checker (tests/test_requirement_quality_lab.py).

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • "The system shall" — an actor that allocates nothing.
  • Numbers copied from a drill or a scenario into a requirement without [A] or a source.
  • A central requirement that the vehicle must obey instead of a constraint on the centre.
  • Requirements only for the nominal timeline: nothing on late, lost or duplicated messages.
  • Splitting a behaviour from its prohibition across owners, or bundling two owners in one sentence.
  • Trusting the checker's clean result: "reject old grants" passes the word list.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Reporting "defect" without the scenario, the requirement or the log extract, so that nobody can triage it.
  • Treating a fail-closed refusal as a fault and pushing for a workaround (section 1.1).
  • Changing a procedure without asking which requirement it carries out.
  • Accepting a requirement in a review without an acceptance criterion you could check at the console.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — the corpus in proportions. 203 of 1,086 requirements have no warning: 203 / 1086 = 18.7 %. 788 are compound: 788 / 1086 = 72.6 %. Of the compound ones, 66 span two or more elements: 66 / 788 = 8.4 % — the share worth splitting in new work. 593 are unallocated by the heuristic: 593 / 1086 = 54.6 %, a measure of the heuristic, not of the handbook.

Example 2 — decomposing T+10 s. Suppose the site adopts the scenario's 10 s as a limit for the zone to be established after fire detection [A]. With a fire-system report to the VCS and ADS of 1 s [A], a truck-to-AMS message over the site network of 2 s [A] (including one retry), and AMS processing including zone geometry and segment closure of 2 s [A], the chain takes 1 + 2 + 2 = 5 s, leaving 5 s of margin. Each term is an interface requirement for a different owner, and each is [A] until measured. If the link is down (section 6.4), the chain is broken and no time limit holds: that case needs REQ-01-04-006 and REQ-02-01-006, not a faster AMS.

Example 3 — rewriting DRAFT-03-02-07. "The AMS shall treat a grant that is not acknowledged within 30 s as not granted and shall keep the area locked." Two shalls, an untagged 30 s, and an ambiguity about whose clock measures 30 s. Rewrite as two requirements: RW-03-02-07 (withdraw a grant with no accepted acknowledgement within the configured deadline [A], measured on the AMS clock) and RW-03-02-08 (keep the lock of a permission that ended without becoming active until the AHS Controller has confirmed the requester outside). Both pass the workshop check; the existing REQ-02-07-003 and REQ-10-06-007 state the same intent.

15. Key takeaways

  • A need is in people's words; a requirement has an ID, a label, one actor, a shall, a rationale and a verification method; a design is one way to meet it.
  • Allocate every requirement — to the FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS, ISF, OSS, MVD or a named role. Vehicle-side decisions are duties of the vehicle and constraints on the centre.
  • Every number needs a source or [A]; write behaviour with a configurable parameter and keep values in a site record.
  • The handbook's 1,086 requirements have no structural error, and 883 carry at least one warning; a checker speeds a review and never replaces it.
  • Derive requirements from scenario timelines, including what happens when a message is late, lost or duplicated.
  • An operational report becomes a defect, a change, a new requirement or a procedure finding — and it needs the scenario, the requirement and the log to get there.
