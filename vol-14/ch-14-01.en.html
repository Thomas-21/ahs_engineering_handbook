1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

06:45, CR1, before the DAY shift. The night-shift Pit Supervisor hands over a list that looks like any open-pit mine's: the EX02 loading floor needs a dozer, the pit-floor roads need water by nine, the windrow on R06 was reported soft and needs a look, and Survey wants the west ramp R24 for half an hour in the afternoon. In a manned mine this list would go on the whiteboard and each crew would sort itself out by radio. Here every item touches roads and loading areas on which 37 autonomous trucks are running, and none of them can be done by simply driving in.

The day-shift AHS Controller reads the list differently. The DZ03 job is a work zone at EX02 that holds the EX02 circuit while it is active. The windrow inspection is an access permission for LV07 that locks R05 and R06 and sends the whole pit over the west ramp. The R24 survey costs nothing — unless it happens while LV07's permission is active, when the two together leave AA-North without a way out. The watering is a moving task among trucks that ch-14-05 deals with. At 07:20 WT03 arrives at AP-02 and its driver reports that the field terminal will not log in.

The questions are the subject of this chapter:

  • Which AMS construct does each ancillary job need — a permission, a work zone, a closure, or none?
  • What must the AMS know about a dozer, a water truck or a light vehicle before it lets one in, and where does each item come from?
  • How much autonomous haulage does a shift of ancillary work cost, and how can the plan reduce it?
  • Who may grant what, and what happens to WT03?

This chapter covers:

  • the ancillary fleet of the reference mine and the jobs of its drivers in an autonomous mine;
  • the three AMS constructs, plus "none", and the classification of every ancillary activity (FIG-14-01-01);
  • the minimum data the AMS holds about a manned vehicle, and the device fit and checks behind it;
  • the cost of ancillary interventions per shift and the shift plan of ancillary work (LAB-14-01-01, FIG-14-01-02);
  • the walkthrough of SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02 from both sides, with SCN-POSCOM-LV07 and SCN-WATER-WT02 as the other two patterns.

The classification, procedures and requirements are handbook conventions [H]. Every time, duration, count and tonnage is an illustrative assumption [A] computed with ahs_sim and is not a recommended value. Traffic-management plans, codes of practice and legislation that apply to mixed fleets are named only as topics [TBV].

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Classify each ancillary activity at the reference mine (floor clean-up, windrow repair, watering, inspection, recovery, survey) by the AMS construct it needs: access permission, work zone, closure or none.
  2. Specify the minimum data the AMS must hold about a manned vehicle (identity, device health, position, permission, intended area) and the source of each item.
  3. Analyse how the number and duration of ancillary interventions in AA-North per shift reduce autonomous haulage time [A].
  4. Specify the device fit (PRU, radio, field terminal) and pre-entry health checks for DZ01–DZ04, WT01–WT03 and LV01–LV12 from equipment.yaml, and what the AMS records for each.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Describe the duties of the DZ03, WT02 and LV07 drivers in an autonomous mine and how each differs from the same job in a manned mine.
  2. Build a shift plan of ancillary work in AA-North with the Pit Supervisor that sequences interventions around loading and haulage.
  3. Explain which ancillary requests the AHS Controller can grant alone and which need the Pit Supervisor's agreement.
  4. Decide the response when an ancillary vehicle arrives at an access point with a missing or faulty device.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine runs 40 autonomous haul trucks, 37 of them producing on the DAY shift, beside a manned ancillary fleet of four dozers (DZ01–DZ04), three water trucks (WT01–WT03, about 100 kL each) and twelve light vehicles (LV01–LV12), all listed in equipment.yaml with a position reporting unit (PRU), a two-way radio and a field terminal (FT). The fleet also has one motor grader, GR01, added after the baseline with the same three devices (equipment.yaml fleet_additions); its performance values and all grader content in this volume are [A] (ch-14-04).

Four features of the mine make ancillary work a design problem rather than a matter of habit:

The loading areas are shared. A dozer cleaning the EX02 floor works where trucks spot and where the shovel swings. Trucks cannot be asked to "keep an eye out"; they have to be kept out, and the loading unit loses its trucks while they are (ch-04-08 section 4.7).

The roads are shared, and two of them are single points of failure. D01 is reachable only over R03 and R04, so any construct that takes either road away holds both waste circuits. The pit has two ramps, R05 and R24; taking one away reroutes the pit, taking both isolates it (ch-16-04 section 4.3).

Ancillary work is frequent and short. A floor clean-up of fifteen minutes, a patrol of half an hour, a survey of a ramp: each is small, and each is a separate request, approval, lock and release. Over a shift they add up, and they add up differently depending on when they happen.

Every manned vehicle must be visible to the AMS. A dozer that the AMS cannot see or reach cannot be protected, so a device failure at the gate is an operational decision, not a nuisance (REQ-04-08-006, REQ-16-04-001).

In a manned mine the same ancillary jobs are planned by the Pit Supervisor and coordinated by radio among drivers who can see each other. In an autonomous mine the coordination is done by the AMS, on data, and the drivers' and the controller's jobs change accordingly. The rest of the volume treats each construct in depth: work zones (ch-14-03), dozers (ch-14-04), water trucks (ch-14-05), light vehicles (ch-14-06), closures and handover (ch-14-07) and awareness failures (ch-14-08). This chapter is the map of the whole.

3. Terminology and foundations

Ancillary equipment. Support machines such as dozers, graders and water trucks (glossary ancillary-equipment). In this volume the term also covers light vehicles when they carry supervisors, technicians and Survey into autonomous areas.

AMS construct [H]. The object by which the AMS represents a manned activity in an autonomous area and keeps trucks away from it. The handbook uses three, plus "none" (FIG-14-01-01): the digital access permission for a person or vehicle that moves to a task (ch-10-06), the work zone for manned work in a defined place inside a loading area or on a lane (glossary work-zone; ch-14-03), and the road closure that removes segments from routing until they are handed back (glossary road-closure; ch-14-07). Work zones, closures and permission areas are AMS overlays, not changes to the map version (glossary map-overlay; ch-12-08).

Ancillary intervention [H]. One instance of ancillary work in an autonomous area: a machine, an activity, a construct, an area and a time window. Interventions are the unit of planning and costing in this chapter.

Shift plan of ancillary work [H]. The list of interventions agreed between the Pit Supervisor and the AHS Controller for a shift, with the construct, planned window and estimated cost of each.

Device fit [H]. The manned-vehicle devices fitted to and registered against a vehicle: PRU, radio and FT (equipment.yaml, ch-02-05).

Access check. The checks a requester makes at the access point before every request — PRU reporting with the correct identity, radio check answered, FT logged in, task stated (glossary access-check; ch-16-04).

Fail closed. A lost, stale or unacknowledged permission is treated as not granted; a stale position never means the vehicle has left; a work zone or closure is released only by handback and inspection, never by its planned end time (ch-10-06; ch-04-08).

4.2 Three drivers, three jobs

Operations objective 1 asks how the drivers' jobs change. The answer is the same in kind for all three: the work at the task changes little; the work around it changes a great deal [H].

DZ03's operator. In a manned mine the operator is called by the Shovel Operator, drives in, cleans the floor while trucks wait where they can see the dozer, and leaves. In an autonomous mine the operator runs the access checks, requests the work zone by radio and field terminal, waits until the controller confirms that the zone is reserved and the shovel made safe, enters, works only inside the zone, and reports out so that the zone can be released — and does not leave the zone towards a truck at EX02-Q, which is held, not isolated, and will move when it gets a path (ch-16-04 section 4.6). The trucks no longer adapt to the dozer; the dozer works inside a boundary the AMS enforces.

WT02's driver. In a manned mine the water truck drives with the traffic and the haul-truck drivers slow down behind it. In an autonomous mine WT02 waters under a permission; the trucks behind it cannot see the water, only the spray and dust in their perception, and some of them stop (SCN-WATER-WT02). The driver's job now includes keeping to the watering pattern agreed with the Pit Supervisor, reporting where and when the road was watered, and knowing that a nuisance stop behind WT02 is reviewed by the controller with WT02's position as evidence (ch-16-07). The design choices between a moving permission and a rolling work zone are ch-14-05's.

LV07's driver. In a manned mine a patrol vehicle drives where it needs to and talks to whoever is nearby. In an autonomous mine LV07 stops at AP-02, runs the access checks, requests a permission for a stated area and task, waits until the area is confirmed clear — minutes, not seconds, with baseline traffic (ch-16-04 section 4.4) — stays inside the permitted area, and reports out at the access point. If the PRU fails, the driver stays put and answers the radio; the release then needs the substitute release confirmation of ch-16-04. The driver is also the person who reports what the patrol found, which is often the trigger for the next intervention.

For all three, a radio that works and a PRU that reports are part of the job, not accessories. A device failure ends the entry before it begins (section 4.5).

4.3 Three constructs, "none", and the classification of ancillary activities

FIG-14-01-01 maps the activities to the constructs.

The classification follows four questions, asked in order [H]:

  1. Is the work outside every autonomous area? Then no construct is needed (travel in the MIA, work at the workshop).
  2. Does the activity change the road or need it for longer than a task visit, or must the road be validated before trucks return? Then it is a road closure: segments removed from routing, handed to manned control and handed back only after inspection and, where the geometry changed, a map update (SCN-MAP-R05; ch-12-09, ch-14-07).
  3. Does a machine work in one place that trucks would otherwise use — a loading floor, one lane of a road? Then it is a work zone: an area or lane reserved for the work, with trucks held outside it (SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02, SCN-WORKZONE-R03; ch-14-03).
  4. Otherwise — a person or vehicle that moves to a task — it is an access permission: a permitted area made of the route from the access point and the task, locked against every truck (SCN-POSCOM-LV07; ch-10-06, ch-16-04).

Applied to the reference mine:

Activity Example Construct Reasoning Cost mechanism
Loading-floor clean-up DZ03 at EX02-L work zone (area) machine works in the loading area loading unit without trucks for the zone's duration
Windrow repair on one lane DZ01 on R03 work zone (lane) machine works on one lane; the other lane may stay open under a site rule [A] single-lane capacity (ch-14-03)
Road-edge rework and survey DZ03 and Survey on R05 road closure geometry changes; map update before reopening reroute or hold until handback
Dust suppression WT02 on R03 access permission (moving) vehicle moves along the road among trucks nuisance stops (ch-14-05)
Pit patrol, inspection LV07 on R06 access permission vehicle moves to a task reroute or hold for the lock
Attending a stopped truck LV with Field Technician to DT17 on R03 access permission and isolation of DT17 at the machine vehicle moves to the truck; the truck itself must be isolated (ch-16-05) lock of R03 holds both waste circuits
Check survey of a road Survey LV on R24 access permission vehicle moves along the road none while R05 is open
Travel in the MIA LV on R16 none manned area none

Three boundary cases deserve a sentence each. Watering is a moving task, so it is classed as a permission; a rolling work zone is the alternative that ch-14-05 compares. Recovery needs two things that are easy to confuse: the permission keeps other trucks away, and the isolation of DT17 keeps DT17 from moving; the AMS can provide only the first (ch-16-04 section 4.6). A survey of a loading face is a work zone, not a permission: the surveyor stays in one place that trucks would otherwise use.

All three constructs fail closed in the same way: lost, stale or unconfirmed means not granted; a construct ends only when its holder's exit is confirmed (permission) or after handback and inspection (work zone, closure). ahs_sim models the work-zone life cycle explicitly — REQUESTED, CLEARING, ACTIVE, EXTENDED, RELEASING, RELEASED — and a planned end time that passes while the zone is still locked raises an overdue flag for the controller; it releases nothing (ahs_sim.mixed_fleet.WorkZone).

Two items come from people and are worth noticing: the task and the intended area. The AMS cannot infer them; it can only check that the position and the area agree once both exist. That is why the request is spoken and read back as well as typed (ch-16-03).

4.5 Device fit and pre-entry checks

Engineering objective 4 starts from equipment.yaml: all 19 manned vehicles that enter autonomous areas — DZ01–DZ04, WT01–WT03 and LV01–LV12 — carry the same three devices, 57 devices in all (LAB-14-01-01 part B). The handbook layers three checks on them [H]:

Check When What Source
First-entry check new, replaced or updated PRU or FT registry entry; correct identity on the HMI; position at a surveyed check point; test request ch-02-05 section 8.3, REQ-02-05-007
Shift-start check before the first entry in a shift PRU, radio and FT working and registered to the vehicle REQ-04-08-006
Access check before every request, at the access point PRU reporting with the correct identity and a fresh age on the HMI; radio check answered; FT logged in; task stated REQ-16-04-001

What the AMS records for each device [H]: the device identifier, the vehicle it is registered to, its type and interface version (REQ-02-05-001), the result and time of the last first-entry check, the result and time of each shift-start and access check with the controller's name, and every device failure with the action taken. A vehicle whose PRU has failed inside an area is tagged out of service for autonomous areas until it passes the access checks again (ch-16-04 REQ-16-04-006).

A vehicle with a failed device at the access point does not enter. That is the handbook rule and the answer for WT03 in section 1.1: the driver reports the failure, the controller records it, and WT03 withdraws (REQ-16-04-001).

The handbook does not define an escorted entry for a vehicle with a failed device. ch-16-04 defines an escort only for bringing out a requester whose PRU failed inside, under the escort's own permission. A site that wants to escort a vehicle in would have to show how the escorted vehicle stays visible and reachable to the AMS, and it would still conflict with REQ-16-04-001 as written. The handbook rule is therefore to refuse entry; an escorted entry stays a site procedure [A] outside the handbook.

4.6 How interventions cost autonomous haulage

Engineering objective 3 needs a cost model. LAB-14-01-01 uses two mechanisms, both from earlier volumes [A].

Segment constructs — a permission's area lock or a closure — remove segments from the truck network. The lab runs one deterministic ahs_sim.fms DAY shift on the restricted network and compares it with the open network's 191 180 t; circuits left without a route are held. This is the method of LAB-10-06-02:

Locked or closed segments Example Circuits rerouted / held Lost haulage per hour
R05 + R06 LV07 windrow inspection 4 / 0 311.7 t
R24 Survey of the west ramp 0 / 0 0.0 t
R05 + R06 + R24 inspection and survey at the same time 0 / 4 (37 trucks) 15 931.7 t
R03 Field Technician to DT17 on R03 0 / 2 (EX03, EX04; 18 trucks) 7333.3 t
R04 dump-road work 0 / 2 (18 trucks) 7333.3 t

The first and third rows reproduce ch-16-04; the R03 row reproduces ch-16-05. A permission on R24 costs nothing while R05 is open, because no truck uses R24 then — and costs the whole pit when R05 is also locked.

Loading-area work zones take a loading unit's trucks away for the zone's duration. The lab models the zone as an outage of the loading unit in simulate_fleet, so the queue at EXnn-Q builds and drains as it would. Trucks waiting for a loading unit held by a work zone are coded DLY-OD-04, not DLY-OD-11 (delay-codes.yml coding rules). The DZ03 zone of SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02, 780 s from T+120 s to T+900 s, costs 660 t (three loads) and 0.58 truck-hours of DLY-OD-04. The truck-hours depend on where in the trucks' cycles the 780 s fall, which sets how many trucks reach EX02-Q during the zone and how long each waits: ch-14-03 places the same zone later in the shift and gets the same 660 t with 0.44 truck-hours, and prices it at 858.0 t in steady state, where no truck is already loaded or on its way.

How the cost grows with the number and duration of clean-ups is the second half of the objective. The lab spreads n clean-ups of m minutes over the shift at each of the four loading units:

Clean-ups per loading unit per shift 10 min 15 min 20 min 30 min
1 1320.0 t 2640.0 t 4180.0 t 6600.0 t
2 3740.0 t 6160.0 t 8800.0 t 13 420.0 t
3 5060.0 t 9460.0 t 13 640.0 t 20 240.0 t
4 6600.0 t 12 980.0 t 18 260.0 t 27 280.0 t

Two readings matter. First, the cost is roughly proportional to the zone-hours, but not exactly: the same two zone-hours per shift cost 6600.0 t as one 30-minute clean-up per unit, 6160.0 t as two of 15 minutes and 5060.0 t as three of 10 minutes. Short stops are partly absorbed by the queue at EXnn-Q; long ones empty it and then starve the shovel. Second, the truck-hours of DLY-OD-04 grow faster than the tonnes: 9.08 truck-hours for one 30-minute clean-up per unit against 3.51 for three of 10 minutes. The delay code shows the trucks waiting; the lost tonnes show the shovel waiting.

Finally, 200 seeded synthetic shifts — clean-ups per loading unit drawn from a Poisson distribution with mean 1.0, each 10–20 minutes, and LV patrols of R05 and R06 with mean 1.0 per shift, each 20–40 minutes [A] — give a mean loss of 2966.9 t per shift (1.55 % of the open-network shift), with 10 % of shifts below 1100.0 t, a median of 2860.0 t and 10 % above 4960.8 t. The spread comes almost entirely from the clean-ups: one patrol costs a few hundred tonnes, one clean-up about as much again as three of them.

The plan costs 3006.3 t: 2860.0 t for the loading-area work zones and 146.3 t for LV07's permission, 1.63 % of the reference shift. The work zones take the four loading units out of production for 0.97 h in total and cause 2.41 truck-hours of DLY-OD-04 when simulated together (2.34 truck-hours when each is simulated alone). Operating Efficiency of the truck fleet falls from 0.979 to 0.9729 (ahs_sim.kpi). All of the priced cost is in AA-North.

Sequencing. In this model the production loss depends on how long each loading unit is without trucks, not on the hour of the day; the model has no haulage peaks, and its trucks keep cycling between the units and the destinations at a steady rate. The lever it does show is the shovel's own stops. I05, the EX04 clean-up, is placed inside EX04's meal break and costs nothing extra. Placed immediately after the break, so that the two stops join into one long one, it costs 1100.0 t; placed two hours later as a separate stop, 660.0 t. The general rule [R]: put loading-area work inside stops the loading unit has anyway — meal breaks, shift change, face moves — and never directly next to them. Real sites have haulage peaks (shift start, crusher campaigns); the same reasoning applies to them, with site data.

The plan check. Constructs that are harmless alone can be harmful together. LAB-14-01-01 checks every pair of segment interventions that overlap in time. In the typical plan there is none. Move I06, the R24 survey, to 07:30 and it overlaps LV07's permission on R05 and R06: together they lock R05, R06 and R24, leave no loading unit a route and cost 15 931.7 t per hour. Each request on its own would have been granted by the controller without a second thought (sections 4.6, 4.8). The check belongs at planning time and again at every request.

4.8 Who may grant what

Operations objective 3. The rule [H] follows REQ-10-06-011 and REQ-16-04-004:

  • The AHS Controller grants alone a request that is in the agreed shift plan, holds no loading circuit beyond what the plan foresaw, isolates no autonomous area or destination, and stays within its planned window. A permission that only reroutes trucks (LV07 on R05 and R06) is such a request; so is a planned loading-area work zone, whose held circuit was agreed when the plan was agreed.
  • The Pit Supervisor agrees to any request that holds a loading circuit and was not in the plan (an unplanned clean-up, a Field Technician on R03), that isolates an area or a destination (anything covering both ramps, R03 or R04), or that asks to extend a work zone or closure past its planned end.

The point of the shift plan is that the Pit Supervisor's agreement is given once, with the costs in front of everyone, rather than twenty times by telephone. LAB-14-01-01 applies the rule: LV07's patrol is the controller's; DZ03's planned zone is the controller's; the same zone unplanned, the R03 lane zone's extension and a permission on R03 are the Pit Supervisor's.

4.9 Trade-offs and model limits

  • Few constructs, applied consistently, against tailored ones. Three constructs are enough for every activity at the reference mine. A site that adds a construct for each new activity multiplies the HMI symbols and the procedures that people must know.
  • Plan-level approval against request-level approval. Approving the plan once saves the Pit Supervisor's time and puts the cost on the table, but the plan is a forecast: anything outside it goes back to the Pit Supervisor.
  • Model limits. One deterministic shift with a fixed dispatch policy; loading-area zones are whole-unit outages; segment constructs are priced at steady-state cost per hour; lane work zones, watering and nuisance stops are not priced; clearing time before a zone is active is not modelled (ch-16-04); no haulage peaks; ancillary machines never queue for each other. The numbers show structure, not values.

The boundary that matters for planning is between the plan (people) and the constructs (AMS): the AMS enforces each construct, but only the plan can see that two harmless constructs together are not harmless.

T+900 s PRU outside and read-back: zone released; trucks get paths to EX02; loading resumes RELEASED normal AHS Controller, Shovel Operator

The zone holds EX02 for 780 s, from T+120 s to T+900 s; LAB-14-01-01 prices it at 660 t and 0.58 truck-hours of DLY-OD-04, on the controller's authority because it is in the plan.

6.2 Abnormal operation — unplanned request, overrun and a failed device

Three departures from 6.1 are common [A]:

  • Unplanned request. The same request arrives at T+0 s but is not in the plan. The controller does not grant it alone: the zone holds a loading circuit, so the Pit Supervisor agrees or the job waits for the next planned window or the next shovel stop (section 4.8). The Pit Supervisor reads the cost from the HMI (about 660 t for 13 minutes at EX02 in this model) and weighs it against the floor condition the Shovel Operator describes.
  • Overrun. At T+880 s DZ03 is not finished. The planned end passes; the zone stays ACTIVE and the HMI flags it overdue. Nothing is released. The operator asks for an extension by radio, the controller passes it to the Pit Supervisor, and the new end is read back. ch-14-03 follows SCN-WORKZONE-R03 through the same pattern on a road.
  • Failed device at the access point. At T+30 s DZ03's FT does not log in. The access check fails, DZ03 does not request, the controller records the failure and informs the Maintenance Planner, and the clean-up waits for another dozer with working devices or for the repair. This is WT03's case in section 1.1.

6.3 Degraded operation — the PRU fails inside the zone

Between T+140 s and T+880 s DZ03's PRU stops reporting. The AMS marks the position stale, keeps the zone ACTIVE and the trucks held — nothing changes for production, because the trucks were held anyway — and the controller calls DZ03 by radio. The zone cannot be released on a report out alone: the release needs either the PRU back, consistent with the voice position, or the substitute release confirmation of ch-16-04 (an identified observer whose own PRU places them at the named exit, read-back, record and the Pit Supervisor's approval; REQ-16-04-006). The Shovel Operator, who can see the floor, is a natural observer only if their own position is known to the AMS; ch-14-02 treats the device side.

The other two scenarios of this chapter show the remaining patterns: in SCN-POSCOM-LV07 a vehicle moves to a task under a permission that reroutes the whole pit (146.3 t for the 1690 s lock); in SCN-WATER-WT02 a vehicle moves with the traffic, and what goes wrong is not the construct but the perception of the trucks behind it (ch-14-05, ch-16-07).

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

One construct per activity, chosen by rule. The four questions of section 4.3 give every activity one construct. A dozer on a loading floor is never "a permission with a small area", because the release conditions and the cost mechanism differ.

Constructs as overlays. Permissions, work zones and closures are AMS overlays on the released map version, with owner, reason and review condition (ch-12-08). They change what the AMS grants, not the map; a geometry change becomes a new map version only through the change process of ch-12-09.

The plan as an object. The shift plan is held by the AMS (or the FMS) as data, not as a whiteboard: each entry references its construct, so that a request can be matched to the plan, the plan can be checked for conflicts, and the cost can be reported per intervention (REQ-14-01-003).

Cost before submission. For every construct the HMI shows trucks rerouted and held, loading units without trucks and lost tonnes per hour before the controller submits (REQ-14-01-005; ch-16-04 REQ-16-04-002 for permission areas).

Planned ends that never release. The planned end of a work zone or closure drives the plan and an overdue alert; it never drives a release (REQ-14-01-007).

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

On the shared timeline of section 6.1:

  • Shift start [A]. Pit Supervisor and AHS Controller agree the plan; the AMS stores it with each intervention's construct and checks it for conflicts.
  • T+0 s. Shovel Operator → AHS Controller (radio): clean-up needed. Controller → AMS: request matched to plan entry I07.
  • T+30 s [A]. DZ03's devices → AMS: PRU fresh at the bench beside EX02 with the registered identity; FT logged in; radio check answered. FT → AMS: request with zone, task and duration.
  • T+60 s [A]. AMS → trucks: no new granted path onto R18 or into the loading area; the truck at the spot finishes. AMS → HMI: EX02 circuit held, cost of the zone, plan reference.
  • T+120 s. AMS: zone ACTIVE; AMS → FMS: EX02 without trucks, trucks held at EX02-Q coded DLY-OD-04.
  • T+130 s to T+140 s [A]. Shovel Operator → controller: shovel made safe; controller → DZ03: permission, read-back, confirmation.
  • T+140 s to T+880 s. PRU → AMS every report period: DZ03's position checked against the zone (ch-14-02).
  • T+880 s to T+900 s. Report out; PRU outside and read-back; zone released; AMS → trucks: paths to EX02; AMS → FMS: end of DLY-OD-04; AMS log: intervention closed with times and evidence.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

The ancillary layer of the HMI shows [H]: every manned vehicle with its awareness state and device status; every active construct on the map with its owner, state and planned end; the shift plan as a timeline beside the map, with planned and actual windows and an overdue mark on any construct past its planned end; the cost of every construct (trucks rerouted and held, loading units without trucks, tonnes per hour) and the running total of the shift; and a warning when a request, together with what is already active, would isolate a loading unit, a destination or an area.

8.3 Procedure excerpts

Building the shift plan of ancillary work (at shift start, [A] 06:45).

  1. Pit Supervisor: collect the requests: Shovel Operators (floor condition), patrol findings, Survey, maintenance, watering needs for the weather.
  2. AHS Controller: for each request, name the construct (section 4.3) and read its cost from the HMI or the cost table (section 4.6).
  3. Both: place loading-area work inside the loading units' own stops (breaks, shift change, face moves) where the job allows; otherwise spread it so that no loading unit has two stops close together.
  4. AHS Controller: run the plan check; resolve any pair that together isolates a loading unit, a destination or AA-North (move one, or agree the isolation explicitly).
  5. Pit Supervisor: agree the plan; it is stored with its interventions. Anything outside it comes back to the Pit Supervisor during the shift.

DZ03 floor clean-up at EX02 (keyed to section 6.1).

  1. Shovel Operator (T+0 s): request DZ03; state the floor condition.
  2. AHS Controller: match the request to the plan; if it is not in the plan, call the Pit Supervisor.
  3. DZ03's operator (T+30 s): run the access checks; request the work zone by radio and FT.
  4. AHS Controller (T+60 s to T+120 s): confirm the truck at the spot leaves and the trucks are held at EX02-Q; confirm the zone is ACTIVE.
  5. Shovel Operator (T+130 s): make the shovel safe; confirm to the controller.
  6. AHS Controller (T+140 s): give the permission; DZ03's operator reads back and enters.
  7. DZ03's operator: work only inside the zone; do not approach the trucks at EX02-Q; if the job will overrun, ask for an extension before the planned end.
  8. DZ03's operator (T+880 s): leave, stop outside the zone, report out.
  9. AHS Controller (T+900 s): release only with the PRU outside and the read-back (or the substitute confirmation); confirm with the Shovel Operator that the floor is fit for loading; record the intervention.

A vehicle at the access point with a missing or faulty device (WT03 at AP-02).

  1. Driver: stop outside the area; run the access checks; report the failed check by radio.
  2. AHS Controller: confirm on the HMI what is missing (no PRU report, wrong identity, FT not logged in); do not start a request.
  3. AHS Controller: record the failure; inform the Pit Supervisor and the Maintenance Planner.
  4. Pit Supervisor: decide how the job is covered — another vehicle with working devices, or later.
  5. Driver: withdraw. The vehicle enters no autonomous area until the device passes the access checks (after a replacement, the first-entry check of ch-02-05).

Time categories and KPI names follow the GMG Time Usage Model (SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020) [F]; codes are [H]. Two reports per shift make ancillary work visible [R]: ancillary cost per area (lost tonnes and DLY-OD-04 truck-hours by intervention and area, against the plan), and plan adherence (planned versus actual windows, extensions, unplanned requests). A reroute does not appear as a delay code at all, only as lower productivity; the intervention record is the only place where its cost is attributed.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Ancillary drivers learn that the access checks, the request, the read-back and the report out are part of their job, and that a failed device means no entry. DZ03's operators learn the work-zone boundary and that held trucks are not isolated. Water-truck drivers learn the watering record and why it is evidence. Light-vehicle drivers learn that the area is confirmed clear in minutes, not seconds. AHS Controllers practise classifying requests, reading costs and saying "that one goes to the Pit Supervisor". Pit Supervisors practise building a plan with the cost table and the plan check. Authorisation to approve isolations and extensions is a competency (ch-16-09). Training content required by codes of practice for traffic management at mines — for example the Western Australian code of practice on road and traffic management (SRC-COP-WA-RTM-2026), whose published scope includes mobile autonomous systems [F] — is not restated here [TBV].

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-14-01-001 [H] Every ancillary activity planned or requested in an autonomous area shall be assigned one AMS construct — access permission, work zone or road closure — by the classification of section 4.3 before it is requested. No manned vehicle or person shall work in an autonomous area under no construct. Rationale: the construct decides what is locked, what it costs and how it is released (section 4.3). Verification: inspection of the classification table against the site's activity list; test (LAB-14-01-01 part A); audit of intervention records.

REQ-14-01-002 [H] For every manned vehicle that requests entry to or is inside an autonomous area, the AMS shall hold the vehicle's identity from the device registry, the health of its PRU, radio and FT, its position with measurement time stamp and age, its access point, its task, intended area and duration, its driver and role, the state of its construct and its shift-plan reference. A request for which any item is missing or invalid shall not be granted. Rationale: the AMS can protect only a vehicle it can identify, locate and reach (section 4.4; REQ-02-05-003). Verification: test (request with each item missing in turn is refused); inspection of the data model.

REQ-14-01-003 [H] Ancillary work in autonomous areas shall be planned per shift in a plan agreed by the Pit Supervisor and the AHS Controller, listing for each intervention the machine, activity, construct, area, planned window and estimated cost. The AMS shall check the plan, and every request against the constructs active at that time, for combinations that leave a loading unit, a destination or an autonomous area without a route, and shall flag them before submission. Rationale: two constructs that are harmless alone can together isolate the pit (section 4.7: I01 and I06 cost 15 931.7 t per hour together). Verification: test (LAB-14-01-01 part E: the moved R24 survey is flagged); inspection of plan records.

REQ-14-01-004 [H] The AHS Controller may grant an ancillary request without the Pit Supervisor only when the request is in the agreed shift plan, holds no loading circuit beyond the plan, isolates no autonomous area or destination and stays within its planned window. Every other request shall be agreed by the Pit Supervisor before submission. Rationale: consistent with REQ-10-06-011 and REQ-16-04-004; plan-level approval puts the cost in front of the decision once (section 4.8). Verification: test (LAB-14-01-01 authority cases); inspection of grant records; exercise.

REQ-14-01-005 [H] Before a work zone or road closure is submitted, the AMS shall show the loading units left without trucks, the circuits rerouted and held, the trucks affected and an estimate of the lost haulage per hour, together with the running ancillary cost of the shift. Rationale: costs differ by orders of magnitude between constructs that look alike (section 4.6); extends REQ-16-04-002 to work zones and closures. Verification: demonstration of the HMI; test against LAB-14-01-01 part C.

REQ-14-01-006 [H] A manned vehicle whose PRU, radio or field terminal fails the access check shall not enter an autonomous area. The failure shall be recorded and the Maintenance Planner informed; the vehicle shall not request entry again until the device passes the access check, and after a device replacement the first-entry check of REQ-02-05-007. An escorted entry of a vehicle with a failed device is not a handbook procedure. Rationale: a vehicle the AMS cannot see or reach cannot be protected (section 4.5; REQ-04-08-006, REQ-16-04-001). Verification: exercise (WT03 at AP-02); inspection of access records.

REQ-14-01-007 [H] The planned end of a work zone or road closure shall be used only for planning and for alerting. When it passes while the construct is locked, the AMS shall flag the construct as overdue to the AHS Controller and shall not release it; the construct shall be released only after handback and inspection, and an extension shall be agreed by the Pit Supervisor. Rationale: fail closed; a clock does not know whether a dozer has left (section 4.3; ahs_sim.mixed_fleet.WorkZone). Verification: test (LAB-14-01-01: I09 (the R03 lane work zone, SCN-WORKZONE-R03) overdue and EXTENDED at its planned end, released only after handback and inspection); demonstration.

REQ-14-01-008 [H] Every ancillary intervention shall be recorded with its machine, activity, construct, area, planned and actual windows, extensions, approver, the trucks held and loading units without trucks (DLY-OD-04 under the coding rules of delay-codes.yml) and the estimated lost haulage, so that ancillary cost can be reported per area and per shift. Rationale: reroutes do not appear as delay codes; without per-intervention records the ancillary cost is invisible (section 8.4). Verification: inspection of records against the shared timeline; analysis of a shift report.

10. Verification and validation

Verification (make test CH=ch-14-01): the classification of the eight activities to the four constructs; the device fit of 19 vehicles, each with PRU, radio and FT; the segment costs (311.7, 0.0, 15 931.7 and 7333.3 t per hour, consistent with ch-16-04 and ch-16-05); the typical shift (each intervention's loss, 3006.3 t in total, 2.41 truck-hours of DLY-OD-04, Operating Efficiency 0.979 to 0.9729); the life cycle of the R03 lane zone (overdue and EXTENDED at its planned end, RELEASED only after handback and inspection); the placement of I05 (0.0, 1100.0, 660.0 t); the plan conflict when I06 is moved; the sweep and the 200 synthetic shifts; the authority cases.

Validation needs data the fictional mine does not have: real counts and durations of clean-ups per loading unit, measured queue behaviour at EXnn-Q during a work zone, shovel stop schedules, and the haulage peaks of a real shift pattern. A site validates the cost model by comparing its per-intervention records (REQ-14-01-008) with the FMS's production record over several weeks. Whether ISO 17757:2019 (SRC-STD-ISO17757), the code of practice Safe mobile autonomous mining in Western Australia (SRC-COP-WA-SMAM-2015) or the code of practice on road and traffic management (SRC-COP-WA-RTM-2026) set requirements for ancillary work in autonomous areas is not verified here [TBV]; all three are verified at catalogue level only, and no requirement is restated from them.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • A permission for everything. A dozer on a loading floor gets "a small permission", and its release follows a report out that says nothing about the floor.
  • No plan object. Requests arrive one by one and nobody sees that two of them together isolate the pit.
  • Releasing a zone at its planned end. Convenient, and wrong: a planned end is a forecast.
  • Cost shown only for permissions. Work zones and closures look free on the HMI.
  • Grader not registered in the AMS. GR01 arrives on site and nobody knows which devices it carries.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • "It's only five minutes." A dozer enters a loading area without a work zone because the job is short.
  • Clean-up right after the meal break. Joining two stops into one long one costs more than either alone.
  • Granting the unplanned clean-up by habit. It holds a circuit; it belongs to the Pit Supervisor.
  • Letting WT03 in "because the radio works". One failed device is a failed access check.
  • Scheduling a ramp survey without looking at the other ramp. R24 is free only while R05 is open.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — classifying the night-shift list. Floor at EX02: work zone (machine in a loading area). Water on the pit-floor roads: permission, moving (ch-14-05). Windrow on R06 reported soft: permission for LV07 to inspect, then — if repair is needed — a road closure or lane work zone for the repair. Survey on R24: permission.

Example 2 — cost of the DZ03 zone. The zone holds EX02 from T+120 s to T+900 s: 780 s. LAB-14-01-01: 660 t (three loads of 220 t) and 0.58 truck-hours of DLY-OD-04; EX02 itself is without trucks for 780 / 3600 = 0.22 h.

Example 3 — LV07's patrol. Lock of R05 and R06 from T+25 s to T+1715 s, 1690 s, at 311.7 t per hour: 311.7 × 1690 / 3600 = 146.3 t, no truck held (as ch-16-04).

Example 4 — the moved survey. I06 moved to 07:30 overlaps I01. R05, R06 and R24 locked together: 15 931.7 t per hour. Over the 1690 s of LV07's permission that would be 15 931.7 × 1690 / 3600 = 7479.0 t instead of 146.3 t — a difference made by moving one entry in the plan.

Example 5 — one long or three short. Two zone-hours per shift spread over the four loading units: one 30-minute clean-up each costs 6600.0 t; three 10-minute clean-ups each cost 5060.0 t, 1540.0 t less, and 3.51 instead of 9.08 truck-hours of DLY-OD-04 (LAB-14-01-01 sweep).

15. Key takeaways

  • Every ancillary activity in an autonomous area needs one AMS construct: permission (moving to a task), work zone (working in a place trucks use) or closure (road changed or validated before return).
  • The AMS needs identity, device health, position and age, task, area, duration, driver and construct state for every manned vehicle; a missing item means no grant.
  • All 19 manned vehicles carry PRU, radio and FT; a failed device at the access point means no entry.
  • A typical shift of ancillary work costs 3006.3 t (1.63 %) in the model, almost all of it loading-area work zones; a clean-up inside a shovel's own stop costs nothing extra.
  • Harmless constructs can be harmful together: the plan check catches a survey of R24 during a lock of R05.
  • The Pit Supervisor agrees the plan once; anything outside it goes back to the Pit Supervisor.
