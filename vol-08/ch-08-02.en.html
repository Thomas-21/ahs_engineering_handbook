1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

10:12, day shift. DT17, loaded on M-20481, is climbing R05 towards P1 when its icon on the AHS Controller's HMI turns grey: no report. The controller's first thought is the network: last month a comms-site outage on lower R05 stopped eight trucks at once (SCN-COMMS-R05). But the trucks in front of and behind DT17 are still reporting. Fifteen seconds later DT17 is back, now red: stopped — VCS safe stop, ADS command loss, holding. Whatever DT17 lost, it was not the wireless link.

Twenty minutes later the Field Technician reaches DT17 under a permission and isolation, with a laptop and a diagnostic cable. The Maintenance Planner has asked for "the bus log from before the stop", and ADS support has asked for "the ADS logs, not the VCS ones". The technician is about to plug the cable into the diagnostic port behind the cab when they remember last quarter's security incident with a field terminal (SCN-CYBER-FT). Which device may they connect, and to what?

The scene raises this chapter's questions:

  • What travels between the ADS, the VCS and the truck's other electronic control units, and how does a bus decide who may send?
  • How busy may a vehicle bus be, and what happens to the ADS's commands when another node floods it or falls silent?
  • How does the VCS know that a command is fresh, complete and from a working ADS — and what does it do when it is not?
  • How is a J1939-style log read, and what does it prove about the stop on R05?
  • How does the control room tell an on-vehicle communication loss from a wireless network loss, and who should get the call?
  • Who may connect what to the vehicle bus, and how is the evidence preserved?

This chapter covers:

  • the CAN frame, its 29-bit identifier, bit stuffing and fixed-priority arbitration, and the J1939-style identifier fields — priority, parameter group number, destination and source address (FIG-08-02-01);
  • bus load and worst-case response time for a synthetic DT17 message set, and what a fault flood, a babbling node and a bus-off node do to the ADS command (LAB-08-02-01, FIG-08-02-02);
  • the timeout, alive counter and checksum checks on ADS–VCS command frames and the VCS's fail-safe reaction, including why the alive check must tolerate a lost frame;
  • decoding a synthetic J1939-style log around the stop of SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17 and reconstructing the timeline for the incident record (LAB-08-02-02);
  • diagnostic access, log preservation, and the triage that separates on-vehicle communication loss from wireless loss.

Every identifier, parameter group, signal, period, timeout and rate in this chapter is an illustrative assumption [A] computed with ahs_sim 0.16.1. The HT220 is a generic handbook class [H]: no manufacturer's bus, signal set or ECU is described. SAE J1939 is verified in this handbook at catalogue level only (SRC-STD-SAEJ1939); its parameter tables are not reproduced and the field layout used here is the one commonly described [TBV].

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Explain CAN arbitration, frame structure and error handling, and calculate worst-case bus load for a given synthetic message set [A].
  2. Decode J1939-style parameter groups (29-bit identifier fields, source address, parameter group and parameter concepts) from a synthetic DT17 log; SAE J1939 document set and edition [TBV].
  3. Design timeout, alive-counter and checksum checks for ADS–VCS command messages and state the receiver's fail-safe reaction.
  4. Analyse the effect of a bus-off or babbling-node fault on ADS–VCS communication and specify how it is detected, isolated and reported.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Reconstruct from a decoded DT17 bus log the sequence of speed, brake and fault signals around a stop event for the incident record.
  2. Explain why unapproved devices must not be connected to vehicle buses and follow the site process for diagnostic access (SCN-CYBER-FT).
  3. Collect and preserve bus and event logs after a stop event following a defined procedure, so that ADS support and the Maintenance Planner analyse the same evidence.
  4. Recognise from the HMI and fault summary when a DT17 stop was caused by on-vehicle communication loss (SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17) rather than wireless network loss, and route it to maintenance rather than network support.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine runs 40 HT220 trucks in autonomous operation. Every one of them is a network on wheels. Inside each truck the ADS computer, the VCS and the controllers of the drive, brakes, steering, engine and onboard safety-related systems exchange several hundred messages per second over vehicle buses (ch-08-01). The control room never sees those messages. It sees their consequences: a truck that stops, a fault code, a derate.

Three things go wrong with on-vehicle communication, each with an operational signature people must learn to read.

The ADS stops talking to the VCS. In SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17 the ADS computer of loaded DT17 resets while it climbs R05 [A]. The VCS no longer receives valid commands. It cannot know why, and it must not wait to find out: it stops the truck by itself and keeps it stopped. Because the truck's report to the AMS runs through the ADS computer in the baseline architecture A of ch-08-01, the control room first sees a truck with no report, then — when the ADS computer is back — a VCS safe stop.

The bus is busy, or someone makes it busy. A faulty controller can flood the bus; a faulty or unapproved device can transmit without pause. Depending on its priority, the ADS's commands arrive late or not at all.

Someone connects something. A laptop at the wrong bit rate, a tool with the wrong software, or a device that should not be there reaches the same bus as the ADS's commands. ch-04-09 places maintenance access inside the truck's security zone (Z4, boundary TB5); this chapter explains what the bus itself does and does not protect.

Each signature needs a different response. A wireless loss is a network problem with its own triage (ch-11-07); an on-vehicle communication loss is a vehicle fault for the Maintenance Planner, with ADS support involved if the ADS computer is the source. Routing them correctly matters for reliability statistics (ch-08-08) and for the safety case.

3. Terminology and foundations

CAN (Controller Area Network) (glossary controller-area-network). A serial bus on which every node can transmit and every node receives every frame. Classic CAN frames carry 0 to 8 data bytes. The data link layer is specified by ISO 11898-1:2024 (edition 3), Road vehicles — Controller area network (CAN) — Part 1: Data link layer and physical coding sublayer, whose scope covers classical, FD and XL frames [F] (SRC-STD-ISO11898, catalogue level). The handbook uses the frame layout as commonly described for classic CAN with 29-bit identifiers; clause-level details are [TBV].

Frame. One transmission on the bus: identifier, control bits, data, a 15-bit CRC, acknowledgement and end of frame (FIG-08-02-01). A periodic frame is sent every period T; the handbook's message set is periodic.

Arbitration. The way a CAN bus decides who transmits when several nodes start together. Each node sends its identifier bit by bit; a node that sends a recessive bit but reads a dominant one stops and tries again later. The frame with the numerically lowest identifier wins and is not delayed by the contest. Arbitration is non-preemptive: a frame already on the bus is never interrupted, even by a higher-priority one.

Bit stuffing (glossary bit-stuffing). After five equal bits a transmitter inserts one opposite bit so that receivers stay synchronised. Stuff bits make the length of a frame depend on its content; a timing analysis uses the worst case.

Bus load (glossary bus-load). The share of time the bus is busy: the sum over all frames of frame time divided by period. 100 % means that the bus can carry nothing more.

Worst-case response time (glossary worst-case-response-time). The longest time from a frame being queued to the end of its transmission, including waiting for higher-priority frames and for one lower-priority frame already on the bus.

J1939-style identifier (glossary sae-j1939). SAE J1939 is a family of documents defining a CAN-based network for heavy-duty on- and off-road vehicles; its top-level document is J1939_202603, and the family includes J1939-21 Data Link Layer and J1939-71 Vehicle Application Layer, with parameter definitions largely in the J1939DA Digital Annex [F] (SRC-STD-SAEJ1939, catalogue level). The handbook uses the commonly described division of the 29-bit identifier into priority (3 bits), extended data page and data page (1 bit each), PDU format (8), PDU specific (8) and source address (8) [TBV] (SRC-STD-SAEJ1939-CLAUSES). Nothing in this chapter depends on a J1939 parameter definition.

Parameter group number (PGN) (glossary parameter-group-number). The number that identifies what a frame contains. In the commonly described layout it is formed from the data pages, the PDU format and — only when the PDU format is 240 or more (PDU2, broadcast) — the PDU specific byte. When the PDU format is below 240 (PDU1), the PDU specific byte is the destination address [TBV].

Source address. The 8-bit address of the sending node. On the handbook's synthetic DT17 bus: ADS 0x10, VCS 0x20, drive 0x21, brakes 0x22, steering 0x23, onboard safety-related systems 0x30–0x32 [A].

Signal (parameter). One physical quantity inside a parameter group, with start byte, length, scaling, offset and unit. A raw value of all ones means not available in the handbook's dictionary [A].

Heartbeat, alive counter, checksum (glossary heartbeat, alive-counter, cyclic-redundancy-check). A periodic frame whose absence reveals a lost sender is a heartbeat. An alive counter is a field the sender increments in every frame, so that the receiver sees a frozen or repeating sender. An application checksum — here an 8-bit CRC over the payload — lets the receiver detect corruption anywhere between the sender's software and its own, not only on the wire.

Bus-off and babbling node (glossary bus-off, babbling-node). A CAN controller that detects too many errors of its own goes bus-off and stops transmitting; the error-counter rules are commonly described in the CAN standard [TBV]. A babbling node transmits valid frames without pause. Error confinement does not stop it, because its frames are not errors.

Diagnostic port (glossary diagnostic-port). The connector through which a maintenance tool reaches the vehicle buses. In the handbook it is a controlled access point inside zone Z4 (ch-04-09).

4. Technical explanation

4.1 Buses on the HT220

ch-08-01 decomposes the HT220 into subsystems. For this chapter the relevant fact is that the ADS, the VCS, the drive, brake and steering controllers and the onboard safety-related systems share at least one vehicle bus [A]. The handbook's model uses one 250 kbit/s bus [A] with fourteen periodic frames (Table 1). Real trucks often use several buses joined by gateways; the analysis below applies to each bus separately, and a gateway adds a delay that belongs in the latency budget of ch-08-04.

Frame Sender (SA) Priority PGN Period Content [A]
fire onboard fire system (0x32) 2 0xFF22 100 ms fire detection and suppression state
ads_cmd ADS (0x10) → VCS (0x20) 3 0xEF00 (PDU1) 50 ms speed, curvature, brake, hoist requests; alive counter; CRC-8
vcs_motion VCS (0x20) 3 0xFF10 50 ms speed, service-brake pressure, parking brake
steer_fb steering (0x23) 3 0xFF13 20 ms steering feedback
brake_fb brakes (0x22) 3 0xFF14 20 ms brake feedback
vcs_power drive (0x21) 4 0xFF11 100 ms propulsion power limit, drive temperature
vcs_mode VCS (0x20) 4 0xFF12 100 ms operating mode, fault count, stop latched
inv1_fb, inv2_fb inverters (0x24, 0x25) 4 0xFF15 10 ms wheel-motor feedback
engine engine (0x26) 5 0xFF16 50 ms engine state
ads_status ADS (0x10) 6 0xFF18 100 ms ADS state
payload payload monitor (0x30) 6 0xFF20 1000 ms payload
tyre tyre monitor (0x31) 6 0xFF21 1000 ms highest tyre temperature
dtc gateway (0x40) 6 0xFF30 1000 ms fault summary

Table 1 is a teaching message set. The parameter groups 0xFFxx are chosen in the PDU2 (broadcast) format and are synthetic; whether and how a real truck uses that range is not a handbook claim. Only the command is a PDU1 frame, addressed from the ADS to the VCS, because a command has one intended receiver.

4.2 The frame and its identifier

FIG-08-02-01 shows the classic CAN extended data frame and the J1939-style identifier.

An extended frame carries 67+8n bits for n data bytes before stuffing: the start-of-frame bit, two identifier parts (11 and 18 bits), three control bits, a 4-bit length code, the data, a 15-bit CRC, delimiters, acknowledgement, seven end-of-frame bits and three bits of interframe space. Stuffing applies from the start of frame to the end of the CRC: g=54 bits plus the data. A commonly used worst-case bound [F] (SRC-PUB-DAVIS-CAN-2007) adds one stuff bit for every four bits after the first:

b(n)=67+8n+⌊g+8n−14⌋,g=54

For eight data bytes b=131+29=160 bits, which ahs_sim.platform.frame_bits returns. At 250 kbit/s one frame takes 0.64 ms. The same frame without stuffing takes 131 bits; the difference, 22 %, is the margin a design loses if it budgets with nominal lengths.

The identifier does two jobs at once: on the wire it is the arbitration priority, and to the application it says what the frame is and who sent it. The top three bits are a priority field, so priority 3 frames (0x0C……) beat priority 6 frames (0x18……) whatever their content; within one priority the parameter group and then the source address decide. A frame's priority is therefore set by its whole identifier, and two nodes must never send the same identifier — unique source addresses guarantee that, which makes address management part of the configuration baseline (ch-18-06).

Decoding follows directly. 0x0CEF2010 has priority 3, PDU format 0xEF (below 240, so PDU1), PDU specific 0x20 — the destination, the VCS — and source address 0x10, the ADS: it is the ADS command, PGN 0xEF00. 0x0CFF1020 has PDU format 0xFF (PDU2), so 0x10 is a group extension and the PGN is 0xFF10; the source is the VCS: it is the VCS's motion status.

4.3 Bus load

For a set of periodic frames with frame times Ci and periods Ti the bus load is

U=∑iCiTi,Ci=b(ni)R

with bit rate R. Table 1 sends 403 frames per second. LAB-08-02-01 gives:

Bit rate Stuffing Bus load
250 kbit/s worst case 25.8 %
250 kbit/s none 21.1 %
500 kbit/s worst case 12.9 %

Most of the load comes from the fast frames: the two inverter frames at 10 ms alone are 12.8 % of the bus at 250 kbit/s, half of the total. A design budget for bus load [A] keeps room for diagnostics, for later additions (retrofit, ch-08-07) and for error recovery; the budget's value is a site and supplier decision, not a handbook threshold.

Bus load alone says little about timing: one misbehaving node can starve a frame on a bus whose average load looks harmless (section 4.5).

4.4 Worst-case response time

Arbitration makes CAN a fixed-priority, non-preemptive system. A frame i waits at most for one lower-priority frame that has already started (blocking Bi, the longest lower-priority frame time) and for every higher-priority frame released while it waits. The classic bound [TBV] iterates

wi=Bi+∑j∈hp(i)(⌊wiTj⌋+1)Cj,Ri=wi+Ci

until wi stops changing. The lab implements this simple form, without release jitter and for the first instance only; published refinements check several instances when the load is high, and that is the version a real analysis should use [TBV].

Frame Bound Ri Simulated worst (all released together)
fire (highest priority) 1.28 ms 0.64 ms
ads_cmd 1.92 ms 1.28 ms
vcs_motion 2.56 ms 1.92 ms
dtc (lowest priority) 8.96 ms 8.96 ms

The bound is at or above the simulation for every frame, as it must be: releasing everything at once misses the blocking by a lower-priority frame already on the bus. The ADS command is through within 1.92 ms — a large margin against a 100 ms timeout, on a healthy bus.

4.5 Fault flood, babbling node and bus-off

LAB-08-02-01 runs Table 1 for 6 s and injects three faults between 2 and 4 s (FIG-08-02-02) [A]:

  • low-priority fault flood: inverter 1, after an internal fault, sends a diagnostic frame at priority 6 every 0.5 ms;
  • high-priority babbling node: a device sends identifier 0x00000001 back to back every 0.5 ms;
  • ADS node bus-off: the ADS's controller stops transmitting; its two frames disappear.
Condition Peak offered load ads_cmd worst response Other effect
Nominal 28 % per 100 ms window 1.28 ms —
Low-priority fault flood 156 % 1.92 ms the gateway's dtc frame starved: 3454.08 ms
High-priority babbling node 156 % 2577.28 ms every frame starved, also vcs_motion 2611.84 ms
ADS node bus-off 28 % never sent load falls to 23.9 %

The two floods offer exactly the same load, and the outcomes could hardly differ more. The low-priority flood fills the bus, yet every frame with a lower identifier — the command, the VCS status, even payload and tyre at priority 6 — goes through with at most one extra blocking frame. Only frames whose identifiers are above the flood's are starved. The babbling node with the lowest possible identifier wins every arbitration; the command is delayed by 2577.28 ms — far beyond the VCS's 100 ms timeout — and so is everything else. The bus is not overloaded in any sense that error confinement would notice: every babbled frame is a valid frame.

Three design lessons follow.

  1. Priority assignment is a safety decision. The command and the safety-related status frames must sit above anything a single faulty node can flood. A diagnostic or retrofitted device must not send identifiers above them — a configuration rule checked against the baseline's identifier list.
  2. The bus cannot protect itself against a babbler. Detection is at the receivers: the VCS sees its command time out (section 4.6) and other receivers see their own timeouts. Isolation needs something outside the CAN protocol — a bus guardian that disconnects a node transmitting beyond its schedule, a gateway that separates the command bus from maintenance access, or a node that monitors its own transmissions [R]. Which a truck has is a supplier question [TBV].
  3. A silent node looks like a timeout. The VCS cannot distinguish an ADS whose CAN controller went bus-off from an ADS whose computer reset: in both cases nothing arrives. The distinction matters for repair and comes from the ADS's own logs, from whether other ADS frames disappeared too, and from whether the ADS comes back (section 4.9).

A node in bus-off may return after a recovery sequence [TBV]. A design must decide whether the application accepts it back automatically. For the ADS–VCS command the handbook's answer is no: the VCS's stop is latched, and a returning ADS cannot cancel it (section 4.6). The same rule appears in the arbitration of ch-08-04: a latched stop is released only by its own source, and the VCS's own safe stop is not the ADS's to release.

4.6 Timeout, alive counter and checksum on ADS–VCS commands

The command frame carries, besides the setpoints of ch-08-03, a 4-bit alive counter and an 8-bit CRC over the payload (polynomial 0x1D, initial value 0xFF, final XOR 0xFF — a teaching choice [A], ahs_sim.platform.crc8). The VCS's receiver (CommandReceiver) checks every 50 ms cycle [A]:

Check Fails when Detects
Timeout more than 100 ms since the last accepted frame lost sender, bus-off, babbling node, broken harness
Alive counter counter did not advance by exactly one frozen sender repeating an old frame, replay, reordering
Checksum CRC over the payload does not match corruption in software, memory, gateways or on the wire
Range and rate a setpoint outside its range, or a speed step above 2 km/h per frame implausible content (ch-08-03)

The reaction is the handbook's fail-safe rule [H]: missing or invalid data is never read as normal. One failed frame is tolerated by holding the last accepted command; a timeout, or three consecutive failures, starts the VCS's own controlled stop and latches it. The ADS cannot cancel the latched stop. Only a reset after the cause is understood ends it — at the truck or as an authorised remote reset if the fault class is on the site's list (ch-16-08).

LAB-08-02-01 drives the receiver with ten good frames and then a fault:

Fault after T+0 First faulty cycle Controlled stop latched
Nothing arrives (heartbeat loss) hold last 100 ms later
The last frame repeated with a valid CRC (frozen sender) hold last 100 ms later
Every frame with a wrong CRC hold last 100 ms later

In each case the stop latches 100 ms after the first faulty cycle — the registry's T+0.1 s in SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17 (T+0.11 s in the model of ch-08-04). The frozen sender is the instructive case: its frames are perfectly formed, on time and pass the CRC; only the alive counter reveals that nothing new has been computed. A receiver without an alive counter would execute the same old command until something else noticed.

What a CRC adds to CAN's own CRC. The CAN frame already has a 15-bit CRC, checked by every controller. An application CRC protects what the CAN CRC cannot see: corruption before the frame is built or after it is received — in a gateway, in memory, in the software that packs and unpacks signals. LAB-08-02-01 corrupts a 7-byte command payload: all 56 single-bit errors and all 1540 double-bit errors are detected, and of 100,000 random multi-byte corruptions 0.0036 pass undetected, close to the 1/256 expected of an 8-bit check. That residual is why the CRC is one layer among several: the alive counter, the timeout and the range checks catch most of what a CRC misses. How much diagnostic coverage the combination must achieve is a question for the functional-safety analysis (ch-04-03); ISO 19014 Part 4 (2026) addresses the design and evaluation of software and data transmission for safety-related parts of machine control systems, by its title [F] (SRC-STD-ISO19014, catalogue level); its requirements are not paraphrased here.

Why the alive check must tolerate a lost frame. A receiver that accepts only an advance of exactly one, and keeps its last accepted frame, turns a single lost frame into a stop: the next frame's counter has advanced by two and fails the alive check, the one after has advanced by three, and within 100 ms the timeout latches. The default receiver of ahs_sim.platform accepts an advance of 1 to max_alive_step (default 4) and re-synchronises, while a repeated (frozen) or jumping counter is still an alive error. LAB-08-02-01 compares the exact-advance variant (max_alive_step=1) with the default, with independent random frame losses over one hour (72,000 frames) [A]:

Loss probability per frame Exact advance only (max_alive_step=1) Default (advance 1–4 accepted)
0.001 77 stops (about Np = 71.9) 0 stops (about Np2 = 0.07)
0.01 753 stops (about 712.8) 6 stops (about 7.13)

With the default a stop needs two consecutive losses, so the expected number per hour is

Nstop≈Np2(1−p)

with N frames per hour and loss probability p. The exact-advance receiver behaves like Np(1−p). Neither number is a prediction for a real truck: CAN frame loss on a healthy bus is rare, and "loss" here also stands for an ADS that misses its cycle. The point is the form. With a 100 ms timeout at a 50 ms period, the design tolerates exactly one lost frame — the timeout, not max_alive_step, is the binding limit here; a receiver that does not resynchronise tolerates none. Every nuisance stop has a cost in Operating Delay and in trust, and a design that produces them will be pushed towards longer timeouts — which lengthen the distance a truck travels on a stale command (ch-08-04). REQ-08-02-004 requires the re-synchronising behaviour.

4.7 Choosing the timeout

The timeout is a trade between detection time and nuisance stops. Three quantities bound it from below: the command period (50 ms), the worst-case response time on the bus (1.92 ms on the healthy bus, section 4.4) and the jitter of the ADS's computation [A]. From above it is bounded by what the truck may do on a stale command. At 40 km/h, the empty descent of R05, a truck travels 1.1 m in 100 ms on its last setpoint; ch-08-04 turns that into a latency budget and an added stopping distance. The handbook's 100 ms [A] is two periods: one lost frame is ridden out, two are not. A frame every 20 ms would tolerate four losses at 3.2 % of the bus instead of 1.3 %.

4.8 Reading a J1939-style log

A bus log is a list of frames with a receive time, an identifier and the data bytes. LAB-08-02-02 generates a seeded synthetic log of DT17 from T−10 s to T+60 s around SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17, on the timeline of ch-08-04 [A]. Its first lines read:

  -10.000  0CEF2010  00 72 00 10 27 00 00 75
   -9.995  0CFF1323  00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
   -9.990  0CFF1020  5A 0B 00 00 FF FF FF FF

Decoding is mechanical once the dictionary is known. The identifier gives priority, PGN, destination and source; the dictionary gives, for each signal in that PGN, start byte, length, scaling, offset and unit. In the third line, PGN 0xFF10 from the VCS, bytes 0–1 little-endian (0x0B5A = 2906) times 1/256 give 11.35 km/h; byte 2 times 4 kPa gives a service-brake pressure of 0 kPa; byte 3 says the parking brake is not engaged; bytes 4–7 are 0xFF, not available — unused. The command frame decodes with the lab-local additions to the dictionary: alive counter 0, speed request 11.4 km/h, curvature 0, brake 0, CRC 0x75.

Three rules:

  1. Unknown is not zero. Of 6691 frames in the log, 1400 belong to a PGN the dictionary does not define (the steering feedback). They are counted and reported, not dropped silently.
  2. "Not available" is not normal. From T+20.080 s the tyre monitor sends 0xFF — not available — 40 values in total. A decoder that turned that into 215 °C, or into "no alarm", would mislead (ch-08-05 reads missing tyre data as UNKNOWN, never as NORMAL).
  3. The dictionary is versioned. The same PGN may mean something else in another software version. The dictionary used for an incident is part of the evidence, recorded with the log (REQ-08-02-007).

4.9 What the log proves about the stop

LAB-08-02-02 reconstructs the following from the log alone:

Time Evidence in the log Reading
T+0.000 s last ADS command; alive counters continuous before it; every CRC valid the commands were healthy until they stopped
T+0.050 s first missing command; no other ADS frame (ads_status) until T+15 s the whole ADS node fell silent, not one frame
T+0.120 s propulsion power limit 0 % the VCS cut the drive
T+0.140 s VCS: stop latched, fault count 2 → 3 the VCS detected the timeout and latched its stop
T+3.810 s service-brake pressure at hold level the VCS holds the truck against rolling back
T+3.860 s speed reads 0 DT17 at standstill on the R05 grade
T+5.010 s parking brake engaged the hold handed to the parking brake
T+15.000 s ADS commands again; alive counter restarts at 0 a restart, not a delayed frame
T+15.030 s ADS status frames again the ADS computer is back
after T+15 s VCS stop still latched; operating mode AUTONOMOUS throughout the ADS cannot cancel the VCS's stop

The log follows ch-08-04's times [A]: detection at T+0.11 s, drive torque removed after 1.0 s of actuation (ch-04-05), then on a 9 % grade with 3 % rolling resistance the grade alone decelerates the truck at 1.18 m/s² — more than the 1.0 m/s² class C target, so no service-brake force is needed until it is nearly stopped. It is at rest at T+3.8 s, 7.8 m beyond the last command, as in ch-08-04 (the speed signal reads zero from T+3.860 s because of sensor noise). At T+15 s the ADS computer is back and reports the stop to the AMS.

The log excludes a wireless cause (the VCS kept sending status on the vehicle bus while the AMS saw no report), places the fault (the whole ADS node went silent and restarted: the ADS computer or its supply, not the bus or the VCS) and confirms the fail-safe chain (detection in 0.1 s, drive cut, hold, latch, no motion when the ADS came back). Why the ADS reset is in the ADS's own logs and the supply data of ch-08-01 — which is why both logs are collected together.

4.10 Diagnostic access and the bus as a security boundary

CAN has no sender authentication: any node can send any identifier, and every node receives everything. A device on the diagnostic port is therefore a node on the same footing as the ADS, able to babble (section 4.5, SCN-DIAGPORT-DT17), to send frames with a command's identifier, or to change parameters through diagnostic services. ch-04-09 assigns this to boundary TB5 inside the truck's zone, protected by physical access control and a configuration check at start-up, and requires that every path by which a change can enter Z4 is approved per use and recorded (REQ-04-09-007). For the bus that means [H]:

  • the diagnostic port is behind a lockable cover and, where the architecture allows, behind a gateway that separates it from the bus carrying ADS–VCS commands [R];
  • only approved tools with recorded software versions are connected, by named people, each connection logged;
  • a connection that writes parameters or software is a change under change control (ch-18-06);
  • the checks of section 4.6 protect against faults, not forgery: a CRC and an alive counter are easy to compute for anyone who has seen a few frames. Cryptographic message authentication, for which classic CAN frames have little room, is a design question for the supplier [TBV].

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

On a healthy bus the ADS command reaches the VCS within 1.92 ms of being queued, every 50 ms, with a continuous alive counter and a valid CRC. The VCS executes it, reports its motion and mode status, and the AMS shows DT17 with fresh reports. The bus runs at about a quarter of its capacity [A].

6.3 Degraded operation — a flooding controller

Inverter 1 develops a fault and floods diagnostic frames at priority 6 [A]. The command and the VCS status are unaffected (section 4.5); the gateway's fault summary is starved, so the fault count the AMS shows may be stale. The truck keeps working. The signature in the control room is subtle: fault reports from DT17 arrive late or not at all while its motion reports are normal. The handbook's rule [H] is that a fault summary older than its expected period is shown as stale, not as "no faults".

6.4 Abnormal operation — a babbling device (SCN-DIAGPORT-DT17)

An unapproved or faulty device on DT17's diagnostic port transmits at the highest priority [A]. Every frame is delayed by seconds; the VCS latches a controlled stop 100 ms after its last command (T+0.1 s); the VCS's own status may also be delayed, so the AMS may see DT17 fall silent shortly after the stop. From the control room this looks like SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17: one silent truck among reporting neighbours, triaged at T+90 s as an on-vehicle fault, not a network loss (section 8.2). At T+1200 s the Field Technician finds and removes the device; the security incident process of SCN-CYBER-FT starts.

6.5 Abnormal operation — bus logs in SCN-RECOVER-DT17

In SCN-RECOVER-DT17 DT17 stops on R03 with a drivetrain fault (ch-16-05). The bus log around that stop is the evidence: the same collection procedure (section 8.3) applies, but the log points at the drive rather than at the ADS.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Bit rate. 500 kbit/s halves frame times and bus load (12.9 % instead of 25.8 %) but shortens the permissible cable length and makes the bus more sensitive to wiring quality on a vibrating, dirty machine [TBV]. The handbook keeps 250 kbit/s in its model [A]; the choice belongs to the platform supplier.

One bus or several. Separating the ADS–VCS command path from maintenance access and from high-rate feedback reduces the number of nodes that can disturb it, at the cost of gateways, their delays and their failure modes. The babbling case (section 4.5, SCN-DIAGPORT-DT17) argues for separation [R].

Command period and timeout. Section 4.7: two periods ride out one lost frame. A faster frame with the same timeout tolerates more losses but costs bus time.

Exact-advance or re-synchronising alive check. An exact-advance check stops on every lost frame; re-synchronising within the timeout keeps detection of frozen and repeating senders and avoids nuisance stops (section 4.6).

Where the VCS reports. In the baseline architecture A of ch-08-01 the truck's report to the AMS runs through the ADS computer, so an ADS failure hides its own consequence: from T+1 s to T+15 s the AMS sees no report. Architecture B adds an independent VCS telemetry path, and the stop and its cause would reach the AMS within seconds even if the ADS never came back (ch-08-04 section 6.3). The handbook recommends it [R] (REQ-08-02-006).

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17

  • T+0 to T+0.1 s — detection. The last good command was received at T+0; the VCS holds it while the timeout runs and latches the stop at T+0.1 s (T+0.11 s in ch-08-04's model). At 11.44 km/h DT17 covers 0.35 m on the held command.
  • T+0.1 to T+3.8 s — the stop. The VCS removes drive torque (effective after 1.0 s of actuation) and lets the grade decelerate the truck, adding service brake as it nears standstill to prevent rolling back. On a descent the same logic needs brake force from the start; ch-04-05 covers both cases.
  • T+1 to T+15 s — no report. Parking brake by T+5 s [A]; on the HMI DT17 is grey. With architecture A nothing on the truck can tell the AMS why until the ADS computer is back.
  • T+15 s — the ADS returns. Its first commands have alive counter 0; the VCS answers them with its latched controlled stop. Nothing in the ADS's restart can move the truck. The AMS sees a report again and a resume may be tried; the vehicle-side stop refuses it (ch-16-05: a vehicle-side stop is ended only by the vehicle).
  • The log window. The lab's record covers 10 s before and 60 s after T+0 [A]. It must show healthy commands before the trigger and the restart after it; ch-08-06 specifies the event record.
  • Time base. The bus log uses the vehicle's clock. To line it up with the AMS's T+15 s and T+20 s the vehicle's clock must follow the site time reference (ch-02-02), or the investigation starts by arguing about offsets.

Two questions separate the two: are the other trucks on the same comms site still talking to us, and when this truck talks again, what does it say? A truck that comes back with a VCS safe stop is not a network problem, however much its reason mentions "communication". If the ADS does not come back, DT17 stays a single silent truck — ch-11-07's "single truck: vehicle or local link" — and only attendance or architecture B's telemetry tells the two apart.

8.2 Who decides what

Decision tree for a truck that stops with a communication-related alarm [H]:

  1. Is the truck's report fresh? If not, treat it as stopped with unknown state and follow the comms-lost triage of ch-11-07 (central, then sites, then exceptions); a single silent truck with its site up is presented alone. Do not dispatch anyone to a truck whose state cannot be confirmed (ch-16-05).
  2. Fresh again, with an on-vehicle communication stop reason (VCS safe stop on command loss, bus-off, node missing): treat as a vehicle fault. Code DLY-DN-01. Inform the Maintenance Planner. Do not request a resume: the VCS's stop is latched and is not on the remote-reset list unless the site has put it there (ch-16-08).
  3. Has the same truck had the same fault before? Check the fault history (ch-08-06). A repeat points at the truck — harness, connector, computer supply — and the Maintenance Planner decides whether the truck is withdrawn after recovery.
  4. Is the ADS involved? A command timeout with the ADS restarting (its status reappearing) involves ADS support [A] for the ADS logs. A bus-off or a missing node on the VCS side is maintenance alone.
  5. Traffic. The AMS holds trucks behind DT17. Whether trucks are rerouted over the west ramp R24 while R05 is blocked is the Pit Supervisor's decision (ch-08-04).

8.3 Procedure excerpt — diagnostic access and log preservation after a stop event

Applies to SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17 at T+90 s to T+1200 s and to any fault stop whose cause is not yet known [H].

  1. AHS Controller (T+90 s): classify the stop by the decision tree of 8.2. Record the alarm text, fault code, report age and the state of other trucks nearby. Code DLY-DN-01 from T+0 s. Open the fault case.
  2. AHS Controller: inform the Maintenance Planner with the fault code; if the ADS status shows a restart, inform ADS support. Do not request a resume or a remote reset.
  3. AHS Controller: before any reset, ask for the truck's event record to be locked against overwriting, if the system allows it remotely [A]; otherwise note that the Field Technician must do it first.
  4. Field Technician (before leaving MIA): take the approved diagnostic tool from the controlled store; check its software version against the approved list; record tool, version, truck and time.
  5. Field Technician (T+1200 s, at DT17, under permission and isolation per ch-16-05): before any reset or power cycle, connect the tool to the diagnostic port; read and export the event record (bus log window, fault memory, operating mode, stop state) and the ADS logs as ADS support specifies.
  6. Field Technician: compute and record a checksum (hash) of each exported file; note the vehicle's clock against the site time shown on the field terminal.
  7. Field Technician: do not write parameters, clear fault memory or update software. If ADS support or maintenance asks for a change, it is a change under change control (ch-04-09, ch-18-06).
  8. Field Technician: report to CR1: evidence captured, file names and checksums; then proceed with the reset or recovery decided with the Maintenance Planner.
  9. Maintenance Planner: store the files in the fault case with the dictionary version used for decoding; give ADS support and maintenance the same files.
  10. Field Technician (on leaving): disconnect the tool, close and secure the port cover, return the tool and record the disconnection.

If the tool or anything else connected to the port is not on the approved list, or the port shows signs of an unknown device, the Field Technician does not connect, reports to CR1, and the AHS Controller starts the security incident process of SCN-CYBER-FT (ch-04-09 section 8.3).

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-08-02-001 [H] Each vehicle bus carrying ADS–VCS commands shall have a documented message set with identifiers, data lengths and periods, and its bus load computed with worst-case bit stuffing shall not exceed a design budget [A] set by the platform supplier and the site. Rationale: nominal frame lengths understate the load by about a fifth (section 4.3). Verification: analysis (LAB-08-02-01 load table); measurement on the truck

REQ-08-02-002 [H] The worst-case response time of every safety-relevant frame, computed with a published CAN response-time analysis including blocking [TBV], shall be shorter than the receiver's timeout by a stated margin [A]. Rationale: bus load does not show timing; priority does (sections 4.4, 4.5). Verification: analysis (LAB-08-02-01 bound against simulation); HIL measurement

REQ-08-02-003 [H] The VCS shall check every ADS command frame for timeout, alive counter, checksum and plausibility, shall hold the last accepted command for at most one failed cycle, and shall start and latch its own controlled stop on a timeout or on three consecutive failures; the ADS shall not be able to cancel the latched stop. Rationale: missing or invalid data is never read as normal; the ADS may be the failed element (sections 4.6, 4.9). Verification: test (LAB-08-02-01 receiver cases; LAB-08-02-02 latched after the ADS returns); HIL fault injection

REQ-08-02-004 [H] The alive-counter check shall accept an advance consistent with frames lost within the timeout and re-synchronise on it, while still rejecting repeated, reversed or frozen counters. Rationale: an exact-advance check turns every lost frame into a stop (section 4.6). Verification: test (LAB-08-02-01 exact-advance versus default receiver, random-loss runs)

REQ-08-02-005 [H] No node other than the ADS and the onboard safety-related systems shall be able to transmit identifiers of higher priority than the ADS command on the command bus, and the design shall state how a node transmitting beyond its configured schedule is detected and isolated. Rationale: a babbling node at high priority starves the command for seconds and is not stopped by error confinement (section 4.5). Verification: inspection of the identifier list against the configuration baseline; test with an injected babbling node

REQ-08-02-006 [H] The VCS shall record its stop state and the stop's cause, including on-vehicle communication faults, and the truck shall report them to the AMS as soon as a report path is available; the AMS shall display a silent truck as no report with report age, distinct from a reported stop and from a comms-site loss. An independent VCS telemetry path (ch-08-01, architecture B) is recommended [R]. Rationale: the control room must tell on-vehicle loss from wireless loss; in architecture A the reason reaches the AMS only when the ADS computer is back (sections 7.1, 8.1). Verification: test (SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17 in SIL: HMI at T+1 s, T+15 s and T+20 s); inspection of the report path

REQ-08-02-007 [H] On every stop event the truck shall record the bus traffic from at least a configured time before to a configured time after the trigger [A], time-stamped against the site time reference, protect it from overwriting until exported, and export it with the signal-dictionary version and a checksum. Rationale: the reconstruction of section 4.9 needs the time before the stop, the time after it, and the right dictionary. Verification: test (LAB-08-02-02 reconstruction from an exported record); inspection of export files

REQ-08-02-008 [H] Connections to vehicle diagnostic ports shall be limited to approved tools with recorded software versions, made by named people, and recorded per use; any write of parameters or software shall be a change under change control. Rationale: CAN has no sender authentication; a device on the port is a node on the bus (section 4.10, REQ-04-09-007). Verification: inspection of connection records and tool list; audit

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Budgeting bus load without stuffing. 21.1 % looks like 25.8 % only after the worst case is added.
  • Assigning priorities by habit. A diagnostic frame above the command identifier, or a retrofitted device with a low identifier, can starve the command.
  • Trusting error confinement to stop a babbler. Valid frames are not errors.
  • An exact-advance alive check. Every lost frame becomes a stop; nuisance stops push timeouts up.
  • An application CRC treated as security. It detects faults, not forgery.
  • Reporting the VCS's stop only through the ADS. An ADS failure then hides its own consequence.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Calling every "communication" fault a network problem. A truck that reports its own fault is not lost.
  • Requesting a resume on a latched command-timeout stop. The vehicle holds its stop.
  • Resetting or power-cycling before the logs are exported. The pre-trigger window may be overwritten.
  • Plugging in the nearest laptop. An unapproved device is a node on the command bus.
  • Coding DLY-OD-05 for an on-vehicle fault. Downtime disappears into Operating Delay.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — one frame. Eight data bytes, extended identifier: 67+64=131 bits; stuffing over 54+64=118 bits gives at most ⌊117/4⌋=29 stuff bits; 160 bits at 250 kbit/s take 160/250000=0.64 ms.

Example 2 — the command's response bound. Only the fire frame (priority 2) has a lower identifier than ads_cmd. Blocking by one lower-priority frame: 0.64 ms. First iteration: w=0.64+0.64=1.28 ms; the fire frame's period is 100 ms, so w stays 1.28 ms, and R=1.28+0.64=1.92 ms.

Example 3 — babble arithmetic. A frame every 0.5 ms offers 160/0.5=320 bits per ms against a capacity of 250 (128 %). In 2 s the bus carries 3125 of the 4000 babbled frames; the 875 left over keep it busy for another 560 ms, which is why the command's worst response, 2577.28 ms, exceeds the 2 s window.

Example 4 — nuisance stops. N=72000 frames per hour, p=0.01: default receiver 72000×0.0001×0.99=7.13 per hour (the seeded run gave 6); exact-advance receiver 72000×0.01×0.99=712.8 (the run gave 753: a loss directly after a stop also counts).

Example 5 — the stop on the grade. Detection at T+0.11 s and 1.0 s of actuation at 11.44/3.6=3.18 m/s: 3.5 m; deceleration 9.81×(0.0899+0.03)=1.18 m/s², time 3.18/1.18=2.70 s, distance 3.182/(2×1.18)=4.3 m; at rest at T+3.8 s, 7.8 m beyond the last command, as in ch-08-04.

15. Key takeaways

  • On a CAN bus the lowest identifier wins and nothing is interrupted; priority, not bus load, decides timing.
  • Budget bus load with worst-case stuffing: 160 bits, not 131, for an 8-byte extended frame.
  • A low-priority flood leaves the command untouched; a high-priority babbler starves everything and is not stopped by error confinement.
  • The VCS checks timeout, alive counter, CRC and plausibility; on failure it holds once, then latches its own stop, which the ADS cannot cancel.
  • An exact-advance alive check makes every lost frame a stop; re-synchronise within the timeout.
  • A bus log shows what happened and when; it needs a versioned dictionary, a common time base and export before reset.
  • A truck that reports its own communication fault is not a network problem: code DLY-DN-01, call maintenance.
  • A diagnostic port is a door onto the command bus: approved tools, named people, recorded connections.
