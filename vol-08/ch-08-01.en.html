1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

13:20, day shift. DT17 stops on R03 in the X-bound lane, 600 m from Intersection X, empty and on its way back from the waste dump D01. Five seconds later the AHS Controller in CR1 reads the alarm row: DT17 — fault stop — drivetrain — R03 (SCN-RECOVER-DT17, ch-16-05). The row also says mode autonomous, parking brake holding, body down, no onboard alarm, remote stop not latched.

The Field Technician asks which box on the truck has failed and whether they can reset it at the truck. The Pit Supervisor asks whether "parking brake holding" on a screen can be trusted — who measures it, and what would the screen show if the truck could not tell. The Maintenance Planner asks whether the fault is in the base truck or in the autonomy equipment fitted to it. And at the morning briefing the Emergency Response Team had asked where the isolation points, emergency stops and manual fire-suppression actuators of an autonomous truck are, and what they do.

Each question is about the architecture of the vehicle: which subsystems exist, which architectural element each belongs to, what each depends on for power and data, and what the truck can still do — and still report — when one fails.

This chapter covers:

  • the decomposition of the generic HT220 into subsystems and their allocation to base machine, VCS, ADS (the autonomy kit), independent safety function and onboard safety-related systems (FIG-08-01-01), consistent with ch-02-03 and ch-02-04;
  • the power and data architecture of the autonomy kit, with supply, protection and environmental assumptions (FIG-08-01-02);
  • a dependency model and a search for single points of failure across the ADS–VCS boundary, with mitigations and what they cannot remove (LAB-08-01-01);
  • the status set a stopped truck must report to the AMS, and the subsystem that owns each signal;
  • what a Field Technician may do in the field, what the kit adds to planned maintenance, and a briefing for the Emergency Response Team.

The HT220 is a generic handbook class [H]. No manufacturer's architecture, bus, electronic control unit or interface is described; every element, dependency, supply and timing value is an illustrative assumption [A].

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Decompose the HT220 diesel-electric truck into subsystems (engine–alternator, traction drive, braking, steering, hoist, electrical, autonomy kit, onboard safety-related systems) and allocate each to VCS, ADS, independent safety functions or onboard safety-related systems.
  2. Specify, as an interface block diagram, the power and data architecture for autonomy hardware (compute, sensors, radios; no position-reporting-unit receiver on the truck — manned-vehicle devices never connect to a truck, ch-02-04) including supply, protection and environmental assumptions [A].
  3. Identify single points of failure across the ADS–VCS boundary and propose architectural mitigations.
  4. Define the status signals a stopped truck must report to the AMS (mode, parking-brake hold, hoist position, isolation state, onboard safety-related system state) and allocate each to the subsystem that owns it.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Identify which truck components a Field Technician may isolate, inspect or reset in the field and which require workshop attendance, using the subsystem map.
  2. Explain to the Maintenance Planner which inspection tasks the autonomy kit and onboard safety-related systems add to planned maintenance.
  3. Read the truck status view on the HMI for a stopped DT17 (mode, isolation, parking brake, onboard systems) and confirm the information required before a Field Technician approaches in SCN-RECOVER-DT17.
  4. Brief the Emergency Response Team on the generic location and function of external isolation points, emergency stop devices and manual suppression actuation on an autonomous truck, so that they can act safely at a stopped truck [A].

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine runs 40 HT220 trucks, DT01 to DT40 (equipment.yaml). Each is three things bolted together. There is the base machine: a 220 t class rigid-frame truck with a diesel engine driving a traction alternator, electric wheel motors, hydraulic steering, brakes and body hoist, a 24 V electrical system and a cab. There is the autonomy kit: perception sensors, positioning, a compute unit and radios that let the truck drive without a person. And there are the devices that sit beside both: fire detection and suppression, payload monitoring, tyre pressure and temperature monitoring, the emergency stop at the machine (SF-02) and the receiver of the independent remote stop (SF-01).

Volumes 02 and 04 decided who decides what: the ADS perceives, plans and stops for objects; the VCS actuates and protects the machine; the onboard safety-related systems request a safe state on their own; the remote stop acts through the VCS without the ADS (ch-02-03, ch-02-04, ch-04-04). They did not need to say which physical parts carry each function, and what those parts share. The gap shows up in three ways.

The same stop looks different depending on which box failed. When DT17's traction drive control fails, the ADS and the site radio still work: CR1 sees a fault stop with a complete status row within seconds. If the kit's power distribution fails instead, the VCS stops the truck on loss of commands and the AMS hears nothing more — on the HMI, a communication loss. The architecture made two different failures look alike.

A status on a screen is only as good as the path that carries it. "Parking brake holding" is measured by the VCS, passed to the ADS, sent over the site network and drawn by the AMS. If any link is missing, the screen must not show the last good value. ch-16-05 made "nobody approaches a truck whose state cannot be confirmed" an operating rule; this chapter makes it a design property.

The people who touch the truck need a map of what they may isolate, inspect or reset in the field, what the kit adds to planned maintenance, and — for the ERT — what each safety device does when nobody is in the cab. Volume 02's diagrams are about decisions, not parts. A wrong reading of the status row sends a person towards a truck whose state is unknown, or holds R03 — the only road to D01, closed for 1710 s in SCN-RECOVER-DT17 (ch-16-05) — for a reason a better architecture would have reported.

3. Terminology and foundations

HT220 [H]. The handbook's generic 220 t class diesel-electric haul truck (equipment.yaml): empty mass 165 t, rated Payload 220 t, net power 1800 kW, maximum speed 55 km/h — all [A]. It is not a model of any manufacturer's truck.

Subsystem [H]. A group of parts with one purpose that can be isolated, tested and replaced as a unit.

Allocation groups [H]. Every subsystem of the HT220 belongs to exactly one of five groups:

  • Base machine — power, drivetrain hardware, hydraulics, frame, cab and the 24 V electrical system. It decides nothing; the VCS commands it and everything draws power from it.
  • VCS (glossary vehicle-control-system) — the controllers that command propulsion, braking, steering and hoist, protect the machine and own the machine state (ch-02-03 F13).
  • ADS — the automated driving system, physically the autonomy kit (glossary autonomy-kit): perception sensors, positioning, compute and the site radio (ch-02-03 F11, F12, F14).
  • Independent safety function (ISF) — here the stop receiver and its antenna, which execute the remote stop SF-01 through the VCS and not through the ADS (ch-02-04, ch-04-04). The emergency stop at the machine, SF-02, is a device of the same kind; equipment.yaml records that the HT220 has one, without number, location or performance [A].
  • Onboard safety-related systems (OSS) — fire detection and suppression, payload monitoring, tyre pressure and temperature monitoring (equipment.yaml; ch-02-04 section 4.4).

Dependency model [H]. A directed graph in which each element lists what it needs in order to work. A requirement written A|B is met if either A or B works: that is how redundancy is written. A function — "remote stop", "status report to the AMS" — is an element whose requirements are the parts it needs.

Single point of failure (glossary single-point-of-failure). An element whose loss alone makes a function unavailable. Common-cause failure (glossary common-cause-failure): one element on which two functions depend.

Stopped-truck status set [H]. The signals a stopped truck reports so that people can decide whether and how to approach it (section 4.6). Each signal has one owner — the subsystem that measures or decides it.

Fail-safe interpretation [H]. A signal not received within its timeout is unknown, never normal (ahs_sim.platform.OnboardSignal reads UNKNOWN).

Machine mode [H]. The state of the machine as the VCS holds it: ahs_sim.platform.Mode has MANUAL, AUTONOMOUS, MAINTENANCE, REMOTE and ISOLATED (energy isolated for people to work on or near the truck). The first three are the operating modes of ch-16-05; ISOLATED is the state ch-16-05 enters at T+897 s in SCN-RECOVER-DT17; REMOTE is not discussed here. Transitions need interlocks (MODE_TRANSITIONS, ch-08-03).

Isolation point [H]. A device on the truck that removes or locks out one energy source — electrical, hydraulic, stored mechanical — so that people can work on or near it. Which devices a given truck has, where they are and in which order they are used is the manufacturer's and the site's information [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AUTONOMOUS-ISOLATION, SRC-REG-AU-ISOLATION-LOCKOUT). The handbook names their functions, not their locations.

Three features of the table carry the rest of the chapter.

The base machine decides nothing, but everything stands on it. The 24 V distribution, the machine data bus and the stopping hardware appear in every function's dependencies (section 4.4).

The autonomy kit owns no stopped-truck signal; it forwards them. Mode, brake state, hoist position and isolation state are measured or decided on the VCS side, so they stay true when the kit fails, even if they can no longer be sent.

The ISF and the OSS are beside the chain, not inside it. Their arrows go to the VCS. The OSS also send events to the ADS, but the safe state does not wait for it (ch-02-04, REQ-02-04-006; ch-04-07; section 6.4).

4.2 Allocation rules for the platform

The allocation of ch-02-03 is written at the level of functions (F01–F19). Mapping it onto parts adds five rules [H].

  1. One group per element. An element that two groups claim is split. The ADS–VCS command link is allocated to the ADS side because the ADS sends on it and the VCS treats it as untrusted input (ch-08-02, ch-08-03).
  2. Safe-state paths end at the VCS and do not pass through the ADS. The stop receiver's stop demand and the fire system's safe-state request reach the VCS on their own paths (FIG-08-01-02): ch-02-04's independence condition, applied to wires.
  3. The VCS owns the machine state. Mode, stop source, parking-brake state, body position, isolation state and active faults are produced on the VCS side. The ADS may add a stop reason or a position, but does not own the state.
  4. Manual operation does not need the kit. Driving from the cab — the manual recovery of ch-16-05 — depends only on base-machine and VCS elements (REQ-08-01-002).
  5. Central systems reach the truck only through the ADS's radio, and only with information. The AMS sends granted paths and holds; it never commands an actuator. No manned-vehicle device talks to the truck (ch-02-04 section 4.1).

Rule 5 is why engineering objective 2 lists no receiver for position reporting units: the HT220 carries none. Manned vehicles are kept apart from trucks by plan — permissions and granted paths from the AMS — not by trucks reacting to their broadcasts (ch-02-04). Whatever the truck needs to know about them reaches it over the site radio, which is the element the model contains.

One point of published scope matters for the allocation: the 2026 edition of ISO 19014-2 states that its scope excludes awareness-only systems and fire suppression unless they interact with another safety control system [F] (SRC-STD-ISO19014, catalogue level). Whether a fire system's safe-state request to the VCS makes it such an interacting system is a safety-analysis question (ch-04-03) that the handbook does not answer.

4.3 Power and data architecture of the autonomy kit

FIG-08-01-02 draws the paths along which the kit receives power and exchanges data. It is an interface block diagram: every line is an interface that someone must specify, test and maintain.

The 24 V distribution is fed by the batteries or by the charging alternator (batt24|chg24). Three feeds leave it: the kit feed to the kit power distribution, which feeds compute, positioning, sensors and site radio through fused branches; the VCS feed; and an own feed to the stop receiver, so that the remote stop does not depend on the kit. The data side has four links — sensors to compute, positioning to compute, the ADS–VCS command link, and the site radio to the AMS. The onboard systems sit on the machine data bus; the fire system also has a hard-wired safe-state request to the VCS [A]. The table gives the assumptions; they are teaching values [A], not a specification.

Interface [H] Supply and protection [A] Data [A] Environment [A] Why it matters
24 V distribution → kit power distribution one protected feed; fused branch per consumer; hold-up long enough for the compute to stop in order — engine-bay heat avoided; vibration-rated mounting a kit power fault must not reach the VCS feed
Kit power distribution → ADS compute fused branch; hold-up local network to sensors; command link to the VCS sealed enclosure, dust and water ingress, wide temperature range, shock and vibration the compute is the kit's single biggest dependency
→ perception sensors fused branch per sensor local network exposed: dust, water, mud, sun, cleaning water a sensor fault should cost one sensor, not the kit
→ GNSS receiver, inertial unit fused branch serial or network antenna clear of the body, cabling protected positioning feeds every driving decision
→ site radio fused branch network to the compute antenna placement, cable routing the only path for the status set to the AMS
24 V distribution → stop receiver own feed, not through the kit stop demand to the VCS as for the radio independence of SF-01 from the kit
Fire system → VCS own back-up supply (`bus24 fire_batt`) hard-wired safe-state request; events on the machine data bus engine-bay temperatures
ADS ↔ VCS command link — commands with heartbeat, alive counter and checksum; machine state back cable route protected the boundary where ADS faults must be caught (ch-08-02, ch-08-04)

Hold-up keeps the compute alive briefly after its supply fails, so that it ends in a known state; it does not keep the truck driving. Fusing per branch turns a short in one sensor cable into the loss of one sensor, not of the kit. The environment — dust, washdown water, vibration, engine heat — is harsh; the 2026 edition of ISO 19014-3 addresses environmental performance and test requirements for electronic components in safety-related parts of earth-moving machinery [F] (SRC-STD-ISO19014, title and scope only). Which tests apply to which kit component is not stated here.

4.4 The dependency model and the search for single points of failure

LAB-08-01-01 writes the HT220 as a dependency map. For a function f and a candidate failed element x, the function still works if every one of its requirements is met by at least one alternative that still works:

ok(e∣x)=[e≠x]∧⋀r∈R(e)⋁a∈rok(a∣x)

where R(e) is the list of requirements of element e and each requirement r is a set of alternatives (r={A} for a plain dependency, r={A,B} for A|B). An element without requirements works unless it is the failed one. The single points of failure of f are the elements whose loss alone breaks it:

SPOF(f)={x≠f:¬ok(f∣x)}

ahs_sim.platform.dependency_spof implements exactly this recursion. The search is over single failures only; two simultaneous failures are the subject of reliability analysis (ch-08-08).

The model defines eight functions [A]. Their single points of failure in the baseline architecture A:

Function SPOFs Single points of failure (architecture A)
Autonomous driving 16 ADS compute, traction alternator, brake control, brakes, 24 V distribution, command link, traction drive control, engine, GNSS, inertial unit, kit power distribution, machine data bus, site radio, steering valve, traction, VCS controller
ADS-commanded controlled stop 8 ADS compute, brake control, brakes, 24 V distribution, command link, kit power distribution, machine data bus, VCS controller
VCS safe stop on command loss 5 brake control, brakes, 24 V distribution, machine data bus, VCS controller
Remote stop (SF-01) 7 brake control, brakes, 24 V distribution, machine data bus, stop-radio antenna, stop receiver, VCS controller
Fire-triggered safe state (SF-05) 6 brake control, brakes, 24 V distribution, fire system, machine data bus, VCS controller
Hold at standstill 1 parking brake
Status report to the AMS 6 ADS compute, 24 V distribution, command link, kit power distribution, site radio, VCS controller
Manual driving (recovery) 11 traction alternator, brake control, brakes, 24 V distribution, cab controls, traction drive control, engine, machine data bus, steering valve, traction, VCS controller

Four readings give the architecture's character.

A single point of failure of a driving function costs availability; one of a stop function costs safety. An autonomous truck that loses its GNSS receiver should stop driving; 16 single points of failure for autonomous driving are not a flaw in themselves. The perception sensors are not on the list, because lidar_f|radar_f lets either see ahead [A] — redundancy against loss, not against error (Volume 06).

Holding at standstill depends on one element and needs no power. The model's parking brake is spring-applied and released by pressure [A]: when power or pressure is lost, it applies — the right direction for a failure. A brake that has failed mechanically is invisible to software redundancy; it is inspected (section 4.8).

The 24 V distribution, the VCS controller and the stopping hardware are in every stop function. In the lab's inverse view, the VCS controller and the 24 V distribution each remove 7 of the 8 functions; the machine data bus, the brake control and the brakes each remove 6. At the other end, 11 of the 32 elements are a single point of failure of nothing: the batteries and charging alternator back each other up, as do steering pump and accumulator, and lidar and radar; the fire system's back-up supply, the body position sensor, the hoist valve and the tyre and payload monitors are needed by no function of the model.

Manual driving does not depend on the kit. Its 11 single points of failure are base-machine or VCS elements, and a search of everything it depends on, directly or not, finds no kit element.

4.5 Shared items across the ADS–VCS boundary, and what mitigation can buy

ch-02-04 wrote the independence of the remote stop as a condition on component sets. With the dependency model the overlap can be computed. The elements whose single loss removes both the ADS-commanded stop and the remote stop are, in architecture A:

SPOF(ADS stop)∩SPOF(remote stop)={brake control, brakes, 24 V distribution, machine data bus, VCS controller}

None of them is in the autonomy kit: the remote stop's independence from the ADS holds. What both stop paths share is the truck's own stopping capability and what it stands on — the conclusion ch-02-04 (section 4.3) and ch-04-04 (section 4.1) reached from the other side. Its integrity is ch-08-04's subject.

The lab compares architecture A with a mitigated architecture B [A] that makes four changes:

  1. Two kit power distributions (A and B), with the compute able to run from either and the lidar and radar fed from different ones.
  2. Two ADS compute units and two command links, either of which can drive and stop the truck.
  3. A hard-wired stop demand from the stop receiver and the ADS to the brake control, beside the machine data bus.
  4. A second status path: the VCS can send the status set to the AMS through its own telemetry radio when the ADS cannot.
Function SPOFs, A SPOFs, B What is left in B
Autonomous driving 16 13 machine elements, GNSS, inertial unit, site radio
ADS-commanded controlled stop 8 4 brake control, brakes, 24 V distribution, VCS controller
VCS safe stop on command loss 5 4 the same four
Remote stop (SF-01) 7 6 the same four, stop receiver, antenna
Fire-triggered safe state (SF-05) 6 5 the same four, fire system
Hold at standstill 1 1 parking brake
Status report to the AMS 6 2 24 V distribution, VCS controller
Manual driving (recovery) 11 11 unchanged

The shared elements of the two stop paths fall from 5 to 4: the hard-wired stop demand removes the machine data bus from the intersection. Everything else that remains is the stopping capability and its supply.

Three lessons apply to any truck, not only to this model.

  • Redundancy in the kit buys availability and reporting, not stopping. Doubling compute and command link removes four single points of failure of the ADS stop, but the VCS safe stop on command loss already covered them. What B buys is that the truck keeps driving after one compute fails and keeps reporting after the kit fails.
  • The status path is where operations gain most. B reduces the status report's single points of failure from 6 to 2. The gain is at the approach: the controller can confirm the truck's state instead of treating it as unknown (section 6.3).
  • Irreducible single points must be named. The VCS controller, its supply and the stopping hardware are in every stop function in both architectures; their integrity, diagnostics and inspection carry the stops (REQ-08-01-003).

Whether B is worth more parts, more configuration and a second radio is a reliability and cost question (ch-08-08).

4.6 The stopped-truck status set

When a truck has stopped, the people who may go to it need to know what state it is in. The handbook defines eleven signals [H], each owned by one subsystem:

Signal [H] Owner (element) Group Before an approach it answers
Machine mode VCS controller VCS Is the truck autonomous, manual, in maintenance or isolated?
Stop state and stop source VCS controller VCS Which source holds the stop — VCS, ADS, onboard system, remote stop, person at the truck (ch-16-05 section 4.1)?
Parking brake applied and holding parking brake (switch read by the VCS) VCS Will the truck stay where it is without power?
Propulsion inhibited VCS controller VCS Is propulsion blocked by the VCS?
Body down and hoist position body position sensor VCS Is the body raised, and can it come down?
Isolation state at the truck VCS controller VCS Has someone applied the truck's isolation (ch-16-05 section 3)?
Fire system state fire system OSS Fire, suppression activated, discharged, fault, not armed?
Tyre monitoring state tyre monitor OSS Is a tyre hot or under-inflated (SCN-TYRE-DT17)?
Payload state payload monitor OSS Is the truck loaded, and is the load within limits?
Remote stop latched and receiver health stop receiver ISF Is a remote stop in force? Can the receiver hear the stop radio?
Active fault codes VCS controller VCS What has failed, and is it on the remote-reset list (ch-16-08)?

The ADS adds the truck's position and its own stop reason to each report. The AMS adds what only it knows: whether a mission is held, whether a path is granted and which permissions cover the truck. Those central fields are the central isolation of ch-16-05; the eleven signals include the isolation at the truck.

Every signal reaches the AMS along the same path: owner → VCS controller → command link → ADS compute → site radio → AMS. Each report carries its measurement time [H]. The rule that matters most is the fail-safe one: a signal that has not been received within its timeout is shown as unknown, with the time of the last report, never as its last value (REQ-08-01-004). The truck applies the same rule inside itself (section 12, example 3).

The lab computes, for any failed element, which signals the AMS can still show. Two cases matter for SCN-RECOVER-DT17:

  • Traction drive control fails (the scenario's drivetrain fault, placed there [A]): all 11 signals are reported. The owner of every signal and the whole reporting path still work. The truck has lost autonomous and manual driving; it has lost nothing it needs to tell.
  • Kit power distribution fails (a contrast [A]): all 11 signals become unknown at the AMS. The truck itself still knows its mode, its brake, its body and its isolation — the owners are on the VCS side and still powered — but it cannot send them. In architecture B, with a second kit distribution and the VCS telemetry path, all 11 are reported.

Same position on R03, same "stopped" icon: in one case the controller can confirm the truck's state, in the other nobody can. That difference is the core of section 8.

4.7 What a Field Technician may isolate, inspect or reset in the field

The subsystem map is also a work map. The handbook's rule [H]: in the field, a Field Technician works only on what the site's procedure lists, on an isolated truck (ch-16-05), and leaves lifting, pressurised or high-voltage systems and recommissioning tests to the workshop. The table is a teaching example [A]; a site's list comes from the manufacturer's manuals and its own risk assessment.

Subsystem In the field (truck isolated) [A] Workshop [A]
Autonomy kit — sensors inspect, clean, check mounting; replace a sensor only if the site allows field calibration checks (ch-06-03) bracket repair, sensor replacement with calibration and verification (ch-18-06)
Autonomy kit — compute, radio, positioning read fault codes; check connectors and fuses; power-cycle the kit if the procedure allows replace units; software changes (configuration register, ch-18-06)
VCS and traction drive read fault codes; request the vehicle's self-test; reset faults that the vehicle allows after the check traction inverters, wheel motors, alternator; any fault the vehicle does not clear
Braking, steering, hoist visual inspection for leaks and damage any repair to hydraulics or brakes; functional tests
Electrical (24 V) check batteries, isolators, fuses wiring repairs
Onboard safety-related systems read the state; never reset a fire system or recharge it at the roadside (ch-16-06, ch-18-06) inspection, recharge and recommissioning test
Remote-stop receiver read its health repair; any latch is released only at its source (ch-04-05)

Two limits apply whatever the site's list says. A latch is released by its owner. The remote stop is released at its source, a fire-triggered safe state only after the ERT and with logs secured, and a machine emergency stop at the machine. A Field Technician never "clears" a stop they do not own. A reset is a request to the vehicle. Resetting a fault asks the vehicle to re-run its checks; it ends the fault stop only if the vehicle finds the fault gone (ch-16-05 section 3). The subsystem map tells the Field Technician who owns the fault before they press anything.

4.8 What the autonomy kit and the onboard systems add to planned maintenance

A manned HT220 already has a maintenance plan for its engine, drive, hydraulics, tyres and frame. Conversion to autonomous operation adds two groups of planned work, and the Maintenance Planner needs both in the same plan (ch-18-06 builds it).

Added item [H] Planned task [A] Why it is planned rather than left to fault codes
Perception sensors inspection, cleaning, mount check; calibration check after work near a sensor (ch-06-03, REQ-06-03-007) dirt and disturbed mounts degrade detection before any code appears
Positioning (GNSS, inertial unit) antenna and cable inspection; comparison of fix quality with other trucks (ch-18-06) slow degradation is visible only by comparison
Site radio and antennas antenna and cable inspection; link quality compared with other trucks same
ADS compute and kit power distribution connector, fuse and enclosure inspection; record of software and calibration versions failures are sudden; the inspection finds damage and wrong configuration
ADS–VCS command link cable route inspection a chafed cable shows up as intermittent heartbeat faults (SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17, ch-08-02)
Stop receiver functional check of the remote stop (ch-04-10 evidence EV-16) a latent receiver fault is invisible until a stop is needed
Machine emergency stop (SF-02) functional check same
Fire detection and suppression periodic inspection (500 h in ch-18-06's model [A]); intervals and content per the site's fire-protection maintenance [TBV] latent faults are found only by inspection (ch-18-06)
Tyre and payload monitoring calibration records (ch-04-10 evidence EV-36) wrong values look like normal values
Parking brake holding test the single point of failure of "hold at standstill" (section 4.4)

The common thread is that the added items are mostly protective. A protective item that has failed latently costs nothing until it is needed; that is why it is inspected on a schedule rather than repaired when it reports a fault (ch-18-06 section 4.1).

4.9 Briefing the Emergency Response Team at a stopped truck

The ERT may reach a stopped autonomous truck in a fire (SCN-FIRE-DT17, ch-16-06) or after an incident. On a manned truck an operator would say what has been done; on DT17 nobody can. The ERT therefore needs a briefing, before they ever attend, on the safety devices an HT220 carries and what each does. The handbook gives its structure [H]; the devices and their locations come from the manufacturer's documentation and the site's procedure [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AUTONOMOUS-ISOLATION, SRC-PUB-UNMANNED-FIRE-ACTUATION).

Device [H] Function on an autonomous truck What the ERT must know [A]
Emergency stop at the machine (SF-02) stops the truck through the VCS and latches; works with or without the ADS and the AMS where the controls are reachable from the ground and from the cab; that pressing one does not isolate energy
Electrical isolation (24 V) removes power from the VCS, the kit and the radios that the truck then stops reporting — CR1 loses the status set; the spring-applied parking brake holds (section 4.4)

Three facts belong in every briefing because they differ from a manned truck [H]. The truck may move on its own logic or on a central grant; neither is in the ERT's hands, and both are removed only by isolation at the truck together with the AMS's central isolation (ch-16-05). Electrical isolation makes the truck silent: CR1 then loses its state, so the ERT reports by radio what they isolated and when. Nobody assumes the cab is empty; a truck in manual recovery has a person in it. Whether any regulator or standard prescribes the briefing's content is not asserted here [TBV].

4.10 Trade-offs and limits

  • Granularity. 32 elements show the architecture's character; a real model has hundreds, built from the manufacturer's and the kit supplier's documentation.
  • Single failures only. Double failures are for the reliability models of ch-08-08.
  • Loss, not error. A sensor reporting wrong values, a weak brake or a status bit stuck at "holding" are invisible to the graph; they need diagnostics (ch-08-06) and inspection (section 4.8).
  • Redundancy has a price. Architecture B shows where redundancy acts; it is not a recommendation to fit it.
  • Shared location. Two redundant cables in one loom are one element for a fire or a rock strike; routing and segregation are a separate review.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — DT17 running on the waste circuit

In normal running DT17's status set travels with every state report: mode autonomous, no stop, parking brake released, body down, not isolated, onboard systems normal, payload empty on the way back from D01, remote stop not latched, no fault. The architecture earns its keep only when something fails — and the first check is always whether the row is current.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-RECOVER-DT17, traced through the subsystems (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

T+0 s is 13:20:00. The registry gives T+0, 60, 900 and 2400 s; the other times are those of ch-16-05, used here without change (its lab reproduces the registry's skeleton within 3 s, so isolation is confirmed at T+897 s and the fault cleared at T+2397 s). The drivetrain fault is placed in the traction drive control [A]. The subsystem and status columns are added by this chapter.

The fault took away driving, not reporting. The traction drive control is a VCS-side element on which no status signal depends, so CR1 had a complete, current row from T+5 s — which let the controller classify the stop at T+60 s as a fault stop with nobody at the truck and no onboard alarm. The mode interlock repeats the procedure's order. ModeMachine refuses AUTONOMOUS → ISOLATED while the AMS has not released the truck (missing interlock: ams_released) and accepts it once it has.

ch-16-05's manual-recovery variant is open only if the fault leaves DT17 drivable: in the model the traction drive control is a single point of failure of manual driving too [A]. The diagnosis at T+1497 s must answer two questions: can it be repaired here, and can it be driven at all.

6.3 Degraded operation — the same stop, but the kit's power distribution fails

SCN-KITPOWER-DT17, a variant of SCN-RECOVER-DT17 (times [A]): at 13:20 on the same spot DT17's kit power distribution fails instead. At T+0 s the VCS stops receiving commands and performs its own controlled stop — the VCS safe stop does not depend on the kit — and applies the parking brake. On the truck nothing is unknown; at the AMS everything is. All 11 signals are unknown: their owners still work, but the report has lost its compute and radio. At T+10 s, after the AMS's timeout, DT17 is shown communication-lost at its last position.

ch-16-05's rules apply as written; the architecture explains why:

  • The cause is on the truck (T+60 s). Other trucks served by the same comms site keep reporting; one silent truck among reporting neighbours points to the truck, and the Maintenance Planner, not network support, is informed (the same distinction as SCN-HEARTBEAT-DT17, ch-08-02).
  • Nobody approaches DT17 until its state is confirmed (ch-16-05 section 4.1); no signal arrives to confirm it.
  • The confirmed state comes from the stop path, not the screen (T+120 s). The controller remote-stops the area, or waits for a renewed report (ch-16-05 section 6.6). The stop receiver has its own feed, so the remote stop acts on DT17 whatever the kit is doing; the trucks it holds are coded DLY-OD-13.
  • Attendance (T+900 s). The Field Technician arrives under permission and isolation and finds the kit power fault.

In architecture B the failure costs one of two kit distributions and the status set still arrives: the attendance starts from a confirmed state instead of from a remote stop of the area.

6.4 SCN-FIRE-DT17 — an onboard safety-related system beside the chain

The fire scenario shows how an OSS acts without the ADS–VCS chain. The registry times are T+0, 5, 10, 15, 60 and 600 s; the platform paths are this chapter's model.

Time Event (registry) Platform path
T+0 s Fire detection alarm on DT17, loaded on R05 fire system detects; safe-state request to the VCS on its hard-wired path; event to the ADS on the machine data bus
T+5 s DT17 begins controlled stop; stop-location logic evaluated VCS acts on the request; the ADS chooses where within the safe-state logic (ch-04-07); if the ADS were unavailable the VCS would stop at once

T+600 s ERT at the exclusion-zone boundary under positive communication the ERT briefing (section 4.9) applies

The fire-triggered safe state has 6 single points of failure in architecture A, none in the kit. A fire that destroys the kit's compute removes only the event to the AMS — the coordinating part, which ch-04-07 already treats as slower and less certain. With its back-up supply [A] the fire system keeps detecting and actuating even if the 24 V distribution is lost, although the VCS can then no longer act on the request.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Allocate the status set to the VCS side. If the ADS assembled the truck's state, the kit would stand between the truck and the truth about it. With the VCS as owner, a kit failure loses only the transmission of a correct state; a second status path (architecture B) recovers even that. The cost is a VCS that time-stamps eleven signals.

Give the stop receiver its own feed. A wire and a fuse make SF-01 independent of the kit's power distribution — the element that, in section 6.3, takes away everything the AMS can see. The independence is checked in the harness (section 10).

Hard-wire the stop demands beside the machine data bus. In architecture A a bus-off (ch-08-02) is in every stop function. A hard-wired demand to the brake control removes the bus from the intersection; the price is wiring that can fail latently unless it is exercised.

Accept that the stopping capability is shared, and say so. The answer is integrity and monitoring of the VCS and the brakes (ch-08-04) and inspection of the parking brake, not more boxes.

Keep manual driving free of the kit. A display or a mode selector read only by the ADS can creep into the manual path; rule 4 is checked by program in the lab and by review in a real design.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

Four interfaces carry the SCN-RECOVER-DT17 timeline, each with its own timing class [A]:

  • ADS → VCS commands and heartbeat, checked by the VCS for timeout, alive counter and checksum (ch-08-02, ch-08-03); in section 6.3 this turns the kit failure into a VCS safe stop.
  • Onboard systems → VCS, read UNKNOWN after a timeout (500 ms in ahs_sim.platform [A]).
  • VCS → ADS → AMS status set, periodic and on change, time-stamped by each owner. At T+747 s and T+897 s, when the crew asks CR1 to confirm, the controller reads the age as well as the value.
  • Stop radio → stop receiver → VCS, independent of the others; it acts whether or not it can be reported.

Two rules follow [H]: no status is shown older than its timeout without being marked unknown; and the timeout exceeds the report period plus the worst normal network delay, or healthy trucks flicker to unknown on marginal roads such as R05 and R04 (as ch-14-02 shows for PRU reports). The values are design choices [A].

8.3 Procedure excerpt — reading the status set before an approach

Keyed to the SCN-RECOVER-DT17 timeline. It adds the architecture's checks to steps 1, 2, 5 and 6 of ch-16-05's procedure; it does not replace them.

  1. AHS Controller (T+5 s): open DT17's detail view; check the age of every line before any value. If a line is unknown, go to step 7.
  2. AHS Controller (T+5 s): read the stop source: VCS with a fault code → fault stop; onboard system → ch-16-06 for fire; remote stop → released only at its source; ADS → safety stop (ch-16-05).
  3. AHS Controller (by T+60 s): read parking brake, body and propulsion; a raised body or a brake not reported holding is passed to the crew as a hazard, with the onboard alarms (ch-16-05 section 6.5).
  4. AHS Controller (T+60 s): read the fault code against the subsystem map. A VCS-side drivetrain fault → field diagnosis, and the Maintenance Planner told that manual recovery depends on the truck being drivable; a kit fault → connectors and fuses in the field, replacement in the workshop.
  5. Field Technician crew (T+747 s, at the stand-off point): ask CR1 to read back the status set with ages: mode, stop source, parking brake, body, onboard states, remote stop, central isolation.
  6. Field Technician crew (T+897 s): apply SF-02 and the site's isolation at the truck; confirm with CR1 that the status set now shows isolation applied and that the mode reads ISOLATED. If the line does not change, stop: the status path or the isolation is not what it seems.
  7. AHS Controller (any time a line is unknown): do not authorise an approach on that truck. Confirm the stop by the remote stop of its area, or wait until the truck reports again (ch-16-05 section 4.1). Call the Maintenance Planner if the truck is silent while its neighbours report.
  8. AHS Controller (T+2457 s to T+2684 s): before the release, confirm isolation removed from the truck's own line, not only from the crew's radio report — both are needed (ch-16-05 step 12).

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • AHS Controllers learn the status set line by line and to read the age before the value; they practise the section 6.3 case until "confirm the stop, then attend" is automatic.
  • Field Technicians learn the subsystem map, that a reset is a request to the vehicle, and — from the manufacturer's documentation — which isolation device removes which energy.
  • Maintenance Planners learn the added inspection items and why protective items are inspected on a schedule.
  • ERT members are briefed with the card of section 4.9 and practise reporting by radio what they isolated.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-08-01-001 [H] Every subsystem and element of an autonomous truck class shall be allocated to exactly one of the groups base machine, VCS, ADS, independent safety function and onboard safety-related systems, and the allocation shall be recorded under configuration control. The stop paths of the independent safety function and the safe-state requests of onboard safety-related systems shall reach the VCS without passing through any ADS element. Rationale: allocation by part makes ch-02-04's independence conditions checkable (section 4.2). Verification: inspection of the allocation record; analysis with the dependency model (LAB-08-01-01).

REQ-08-01-002 [H] Manual operation of the truck from the cab shall not depend on any element of the autonomy kit. Rationale: recovery must remain possible when the kit is what has failed (section 4.2, rule 4). Verification: analysis (LAB-08-01-01 kit_in_manual_path returns an empty list); test with the kit isolated.

REQ-08-01-003 [H] A dependency model of each truck class shall be maintained, and for each stop function (ADS-commanded stop, VCS safe stop, remote stop, fire-triggered safe state, hold at standstill) every single point of failure shall be listed with either a mitigation or a named justification; elements shared by the ADS-commanded stop and the independent stop path shall be identified as common-cause items. Rationale: the shared stopping capability cannot be removed and must be visible (section 4.5). Verification: analysis; review of the list at each change to the truck's configuration.

REQ-08-01-004 [H] A stopped truck shall report to the AMS the status set of section 4.6, each signal with its source and measurement time. The AMS shall show any signal not received within its configured timeout as unknown with the time of the last report, and shall never show it as its last value. Rationale: a stale value read as current is a fail-open at the approach (section 4.6). Verification: test (interrupt the reporting path; LAB-08-01-01 status_at_ams); inspection of the HMI.

REQ-08-01-005 [H] The AMS shall not show a truck as available for attendance while any line of its status set is unknown, and the procedure shall require the stop to be confirmed by the independent safety function or by a renewed report before any approach. Rationale: section 6.3; ch-16-05 section 4.1. Verification: test of the HMI state; inspection of the procedure; drill.

REQ-08-01-006 [H] The autonomy kit shall be supplied through a protected feed of its own with fused branches; the stop receiver and the VCS shall be supplied by feeds that do not pass through the kit's power distribution. Rationale: a kit power fault must not remove the independent stop or the VCS (sections 4.3, 6.3). Verification: inspection of the wiring diagram and the harness; test (kit feed opened, remote stop exercised).

REQ-08-01-007 [H] For each truck class the site shall keep a field-work list stating, per subsystem, what a Field Technician may isolate, inspect or reset in the field and what requires the workshop. No latch shall be released except by its own source. Rationale: section 4.7. Verification: inspection of the list against the manufacturer's documentation; audit of field records.

REQ-08-01-008 [H] The site shall keep, for each truck class and configuration, a briefing for the Emergency Response Team stating the function and location of the emergency stop controls, isolation points and manual fire-suppression actuators, and the facts of section 4.9; it shall be reviewed whenever the truck's configuration changes. Rationale: nobody is in the cab to tell the ERT what has been done (section 4.9). Verification: inspection; exercise with the ERT.

REQ-08-01-009 [H] The planned maintenance of each autonomous truck shall include the inspection and functional-check tasks of section 4.8 for the autonomy kit, the stop receiver, the machine emergency stop, the onboard safety-related systems and the parking brake. Rationale: their failures are mostly latent (section 4.8; ch-18-06). Verification: inspection of the maintenance plan.

10. Verification and validation

  • Allocation and independence by analysis. The lab checks that the intersection of the two stop paths' single points of failure contains no kit element and that manual driving depends on none (REQ-08-01-001 to -003); the model is reviewed against the wiring diagrams.
  • Independence by test, because the harness, not the diagram, is what fails. With the kit's feed opened: the VCS stops the truck on command loss, the remote stop still acts, and the status set turns unknown at the AMS within its timeout (REQ-08-01-004, -006).
  • Status set by fault injection. Each owner is disconnected in turn; only its signal may turn unknown. A stuck signal — a parking-brake switch reading "holding" whatever the brake does — needs a plausibility check on the VCS side and the holding test of section 4.8.
  • Field-work list and ERT briefing by inspection and drill (REQ-08-01-007, -008). Whether any regulation or standard sets requirements for isolation of autonomous trucks or for manual suppression on unmanned machines is not asserted here [TBV] (SRC-REG-AU-ISOLATION-LOCKOUT, SRC-PUB-AUTONOMOUS-ISOLATION, SRC-PUB-UNMANNED-FIRE-ACTUATION).
  • Commissioning. The static tests of the truck commissioning checklist (ch-18-04 section 4.9, item 5) include the status set and the onboard systems' reporting; this chapter adds the kit-feed test above.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Letting the ADS own the machine state. A kit failure then turns a known truck into an unknown one.
  • Feeding the stop receiver from the kit's distribution. The independent stop then shares the kit's most likely single failure.
  • Showing the last value of a lost signal. "Parking brake holding" from ten minutes ago looks exactly like a current "holding".
  • Counting redundancy in the kit as redundancy of the stop. Two compute units do not make the brakes redundant.
  • Drawing redundancy that shares a loom. Two cables in one harness are one element for a rock strike or a fire.
  • Putting a kit element into the manual path. A mode selector read only by the ADS can make manual recovery impossible when the kit fails.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Reading the value before the age. The single most common misreading of a status row.
  • Calling network support for a silent truck among reporting neighbours. The cause is on the truck.
  • Resetting a stop at the truck that belongs to another source. A remote stop is released at its source; a fire safe state after the ERT and with logs secured.
  • Assuming electrical isolation is harmless for CR1. It removes the status set; the ERT or the crew must say by radio what they isolated.
  • Assuming a stopped autonomous truck is unmanned. In manual recovery it is not.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — single points of failure of the ADS-commanded stop by hand. The function needs ads, cmd_bus, vcs and brakes [A]. Following the dependencies: ads needs kit_pdu, which needs bus24; vcs needs bus24; brakes needs brake_ctl, which needs vcs, bus24 and mbus; bus24 needs batt24|chg24. The direct requirements give 4 elements; the chains add kit_pdu, brake_ctl, mbus and bus24: 8 single points of failure. batt24 and chg24 are not on the list, because each covers the loss of the other. The lab gives the same 8.

Example 2 — what CR1 can confirm. The status report needs vcs, cmd_bus, ads and radio, with kit_pdu and bus24 behind them: 6 single points of failure. A traction drive control fault is neither among them nor the owner of any signal: all 11 signals are reported. A kit power fault is among them: all 11 are unknown. With architecture B the status report's requirement becomes ads_report|vcs_telemetry; the two alternatives share only vcs and bus24, which become the only 2 single points of failure, and losing one kit distribution leaves all 11 signals reported.

Example 3 — the onboard view. The fire system reports every 100 ms; the tyre monitor's last report is at 200 ms (its reports stop 300 ms after the start). At 2000 ms the VCS reads the fire system NORMAL (last report 0 ms old) and the tyre monitor UNKNOWN (1800 ms old, more than the 500 ms timeout) [A]. A display of the tyre's last value would show NORMAL.

15. Key takeaways

  • The HT220 is a base machine, a VCS, an autonomy kit, an independent stop path and onboard safety-related systems; each element belongs to one group, and the safe-state paths reach the VCS without the ADS.
  • A dependency model with A|B redundancy turns independence claims into checks: autonomous driving has 16 single points of failure, the ADS stop 8, manual driving 11 — none of the last in the kit.
  • The two stop paths share the truck's stopping capability and its supply. Kit redundancy cannot remove it; it must be named and made reliable.
  • The VCS owns the stopped-truck status set; the kit only forwards it. A kit failure therefore loses the transmission of a correct state — which is why a silent truck is unknown, not "probably fine".
  • The status row is read age first, value second. An unknown line blocks the approach until the stop is confirmed by the independent stop path or a renewed report.
  • The subsystem map is also a work map for Field Technicians, a maintenance plan for the Maintenance Planner and a briefing for the ERT.
