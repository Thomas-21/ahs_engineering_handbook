1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

DT09, empty, is on R17 approaching the EX01 loading area at 15 km/h. Its perception reports an object classified as a person 60 m ahead, near the toe of the face, where nobody has a permission. The truck stops and holds (ch-07-04 records it at rest 51.5 m from the person). Ten seconds later the AHS Controller has the alarm and finds no matching position report; at fifteen seconds the area remote stop of AA-North follows (SCN-PERSON-EX01).

The next morning the Pit Supervisor reads the event record and asks three questions that nobody in the review can answer from the supplier's brochure. "It saw the person at 60 m on R17. Would it have seen them at 50 km/h on R03? In the dust EX01 raises when it loads? In the spray WT02 leaves on R03?" The perception engineer asks the same question from the other end: "How far ahead does the truck need to see — on which road, at which speed, for which object?"

The questions this chapter answers:

  • How is a required detection range derived from the truck's stopping distance, the time perception and the decision take, and a stand-off margin, and which stopping model is the right input?
  • How does the required range vary across the reference-mine network — by speed limit, grade, payload and road surface — and where does a controlled stop stop being possible?
  • Which objects must the truck detect in which part of the mine, and which object sets the requirement on a road?
  • How do dust, spray, rain, night, low sun, vibration and mud limit each sensing modality, and how does that relate to the operational design domain (ODD) of ch-04-02?
  • What coverage does the truck need around itself — when reversing into EX02-L and the C01 tipping bay, and at Intersections X and V?
  • What is a truck not designed to detect, and what does that mean for people on foot and light vehicles in AA-North?

This chapter covers:

  • the required detection range as latency, decision, stopping distance and margin, computed with the stopping models of ch-04-05 (class C) and ch-07-02 (full braking), used here unchanged, for every AHS segment, both directions, loaded and empty (LAB-06-01-01; FIG-06-01-01, FIG-06-01-02);
  • object classes by area and the handbook's design class per road [H];
  • conditions against modalities and the ODD, with the speed each condition supports under the illustrative detection model of ahs_sim.perception;
  • coverage around the truck: the near-field blind zone, reversing, and side coverage at X and V;
  • SCN-PERSON-EX01 walked through from the detection to the area remote stop, and SCN-WATER-WT02 as the recurring environmental limit;
  • the operations consequences: what trucks are not designed to detect, and the rules and reports that follow.

Every latency, margin, detection probability, sensor parameter and speed in this chapter is an illustrative assumption [A] computed with ahs_sim 0.15.1. No sensor product, vendor capability or perception performance is described, and no range computed here is a requirement for any real system.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Derive the required detection range on R05, R06 and the EX02 loading-area link from DT17's stopping distance, perception and decision latency and the segment speed limits, with every parameter labelled [A].
  2. Specify the object classes the perception system must handle at the reference mine (haul trucks, light vehicles, people, dozers, shovels, windrows, rocks and spillage) and their relevance by area and segment.
  3. Analyse the environmental conditions (dust, rain, night, low sun, vibration, mud) that limit each sensing modality, and relate them to the ODD defined in ch-04-02.
  4. Specify sensing coverage around the truck (forward, rear for reversing into EX02-L and the C01 tipping bay, sides at Intersections X and V) from the manoeuvres trucks perform at the reference mine.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Explain which objects an autonomous truck is and is not designed to detect, and how this shapes rules for people on foot and light vehicles in AA-North.
  2. Identify the site conditions the operations team must control to keep perception within its ODD, such as WT02's watering pattern on R03 and clearing spillage on R05.
  3. Explain why reversing into loading spots and tipping bays places particular demands on perception, and what Shovel Operators and people near C01 must do as a result.
  4. Report conditions that exceed sensing assumptions (heavy rain, smoke from a truck fire, unusual objects on the road) so that the controller can restrict operation.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine asks a lot of a truck's perception, and asks it in different ways on different roads.

  • Speeds from walking pace to 50 km/h. The site limits in mine.yaml run from 15 km/h on the loading-area, crusher and dump links (R17–R22) to 50 km/h on the central roads R01, R03 and R12. The main ramp R05 descends 9.0 % at 40 km/h for an empty truck; a loaded truck climbs it at its power-limited 11.44 km/h.
  • Three surfaces. Dry roads most of the time; roads WT02 has just watered; roads after rain. The braking a truck can rely on changes with each (ch-07-02).
  • Many kinds of object. Forty autonomous haul trucks share AA-North, AA-Central and AA-South with four manned shovels, four dozers, three water trucks, a grader and twelve light vehicles (equipment.yaml); Field Technicians walk to stopped trucks; Survey works on the benches; rocks fall from loads; windrows are damaged.
  • Reversing every cycle. Each truck reverses into a loading spot beside a shovel and into the C01 tipping bay or a D01 tip point — manoeuvres in which the truck moves towards the area its forward sensors do not see.
  • Intersections with manned traffic. Light vehicles use Intersection V to reach the MIA gate, the park-up and the fuel bay; when R05 is closed, pit traffic is rerouted over R24 and V (ch-04-08).
  • Air that is not clear. Dust from loading and from dry haul roads, residual spray after WT02 has watered R03 (SCN-WATER-WT02), rain, smoke if a truck catches fire (SCN-FIRE-DT17), night for half of every day.

Perception is not the first protection against any of this. Access control, position reporting, permissions and work zones keep people and manned vehicles away from moving trucks (ch-04-08, ch-14-02). But perception is the layer that remains when those fail. In ch-04-08's illustrative event tree, an uncovered presence without a working position reporting unit (PRU) falls to perception 90 % of the time; a person on foot has no PRU at all. The required detection range is therefore not an engineering curiosity. It is the quantitative link between the speeds the mine wants, the stopping the truck can do and the protection that people in the pit actually have.

Operations people meet the same link from the other side, as three practical questions:

  1. Why does the truck slow down in dust when the road is perfectly good? — because the distance it can see a person has shrunk below the distance it needs to stop (section 4.5).
  2. Why are we not allowed to walk across R03 to look at a truck, when the trucks "can see people"? — because on a 50 km/h road the truck cannot see a person far enough ahead to stop (section 4.4).
  3. Why must nobody stand behind a truck at the shovel, even when it has rear sensors? — because the near field of a large truck has blind zones, and the reversing truck relies on the area being clear (section 4.6).

3. Terminology and foundations

Detection range (glossary detection-range). The range up to which a perception system reports an object of a given class in a given condition with a stated probability. In this chapter: the largest range at which the per-frame detection probability of ahs_sim.perception.p_detect is at least 0.9 [A]. A detection range without a class, a condition and a probability is meaningless.

Required detection range (glossary required-detection-range). The range at which an object must be detected for the truck to stop short of it with a stated margin: distance travelled during perception latency and the stop decision, plus the stopping distance, plus the margin.

Perception latency tp. Time from the object coming into view to a confirmed detection being available — sensor frames, processing, confirmation over several frames. Here 0.3 s [A].

Decision time td. Time from a confirmed detection to a stop being commanded by the behaviour layer. Here 0.2 s [A].

Stop margin dm. Distance the truck should still have to the object when it is at rest; here the 10.0 m stop margin of ahs_sim.planning.BehaviourParams [A] (V07), read from the model rather than retyped.

Stop class (glossary stop-class). ch-04-05's handbook classes: class C (controlled, planned deceleration 1.0 m/s² [A]) and class I (immediate, all available braking). The perception stop changes class with distance: if a class C stop would end beyond the object, braking harder is the only choice (ch-04-05 section 4.1).

Object class (glossary object-class). A category of object the perception system distinguishes because the truck must behave differently for it: person, light vehicle, haul truck, dozer, rock, windrow in the model; shovels, spillage and other equipment in the site's list.

Design class [H]. The hardest object class a moving truck is designed to stop for on a given road. This chapter's rule: the light vehicle on roads limited to 40 km/h or more, a person elsewhere (section 4.4).

Blind zone (glossary blind-zone). A region around the truck in which a sensor cannot detect an object of a given size, because no beam or pixel reaches it — typically close to the truck and below the sensor.

Missed detection and false detection. A missed detection is a real object that is not reported (a safety problem); a false detection is a report of an object that is not there, or not a hazard (a productivity problem, seen as a nuisance stop, glossary nuisance-stop). The two are budgeted, measured and reported separately (ch-06-07).

Operational design domain (glossary operational-design-domain). The conditions the system is designed for (ch-04-02). Visibility is one of its attributes; in ch-04-02's illustrative ODD, reduced visibility is inside only derated to 30 km/h and poor visibility is outside.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 From stopping distance to required detection range

A truck that first detects an object at range R must cover, before it is at rest, the distance it travels while perception confirms the object, the distance it travels while the stop is decided and commanded, and its stopping distance. To stop with a margin dm it needs

Rreq=v(tp+td)+sstop(v,G,μ,m)+dm

with v the speed, G the grade in the direction of travel, μ the road adhesion and m the mass. This is ahs_sim.perception.required_range_m; the stopping distance is supplied by the caller. Two models supply it, both used here unchanged, and LAB-06-01-01 first reproduces their published numbers so that the requirement inherits them unchanged:

  • V04, class C (ahs_sim.safety.stopping_distance, ch-04-05): latency and actuation at constant speed, then the braking force that holds 1.0 m/s² with grade and rolling resistance, from 1000 kN capped by adhesion 0.5 [A]. For DT16's case in ch-04-05 — empty, descending R05 at 40 km/h — the lab reproduces 84.0 m. That figure contains ch-04-05's 1 s remote-stop latency: the radio path from the console to the truck. A perception stop has no radio path, so the stopping part here uses latency 0, which gives 72.8 m — the value ch-04-05 itself reports for zero latency.
  • V07, full braking (ahs_sim.dynamics.stop_on_grade, ch-07-02): reaction with the drive cut, retarder and service brakes building up, adhesion-limited braking. The lab reproduces 25.2 / 35.9 / 56.2 / 122.2 m on dry, damp, wet and slippery surfaces for the same case. Its 0.5 s reaction is kept as the command-to-brake reaction. If a design's reaction already contains the decision, the budget double-counts 0.2 s (2.2 m at 40 km/h) on the safe side [A].

4.2 Two ranges, one switch

The case of FIG-06-01-01 gives three numbers:

R05, empty, descending, 40 km/h Stop Required range
Class C (controlled) 72.8 m 88.4 m
Full braking, dry 25.2 m 40.8 m
Full braking, wet 56.2 m 71.8 m

They answer different questions. 88.4 m is the range at which the truck can still choose a controlled stop: an object first confirmed further away than that is met with the steerable, load-friendly class C stop of ch-04-05. 40.8 m dry and 71.8 m wet are the ranges inside which a stop is no longer possible at all: an object first confirmed nearer than that will be reached however the truck stops. Between the two lies the band in which the ADS must switch to full braking — exactly ch-04-05's rule that the perception stop changes class with distance.

A perception requirement therefore has two parts [H] (a handbook convention). The safety part asks that the design class be detected beyond the full-braking range, on the worst surface the ODD allows, at the speed the truck is driving. The operational part asks that it usually be detected beyond the class C range, because each full-braking stop costs stability margin, spillage and sometimes a blocked ramp (ch-04-05 section 4.5). Engineers who quote one number for "the" required range have hidden one of the two questions.

Four results matter.

Speed dominates. The class C range grows with the square of speed: 24.9 m at 15 km/h, 57.2 m at 30 km/h, 88.4 m at 40 km/h, 127.3 m at 50 km/h. The class C stop does not depend on grade or payload as long as its target can be met (ch-04-05), so all 40 km/h cases share 88.4 m and all 30 km/h cases 57.2 m.

Surface and grade act on full braking. On R05 the full-braking requirement grows from 40.8 m dry to 71.8 m wet; on the level R06 at the same speed from 36.1 m to 50.4 m. Grade amplifies poor adhesion, as ch-07-02 showed: an empty truck on wet R05 at 40 km/h needs slightly more range than an empty truck on wet R03 at 50 km/h (71.8 m against 70.8 m).

The climb is not the problem. The loaded truck climbing R05 at 11.44 km/h needs 19.1 m even for a class C stop. The demanding direction on a ramp is down — the empty truck at 40 km/h — which is also the direction ch-07-02 restricts in wet weather.

The EX02 link is short-range but close-quarters. At 15 km/h on R18 the requirement is 24.9 m for a controlled stop and 16.2 m for full braking. What makes the loading area hard is not range but what is there: a shovel, a dozer cleaning the floor (SCN-DOZER-DZ03-EX02), sometimes a person, and a reversing manoeuvre (section 4.6).

Two conclusions follow. First, people on foot are exceptions everywhere — by design of the access rules (ch-04-08, ch-14-06) — but they are the hardest object to detect. Second, the class a truck must be able to stop for on a road can be chosen with the access rules, and must be recorded with them. The handbook's design-class rule [H], a handbook convention, is:

  • on roads limited to 40 km/h or more, people on foot are present only inside a work zone or permission that holds the trucks (ch-14-03, ch-16-05), so the design class is the light vehicle;
  • on approaches, loading-area links, destinations, the park-up, the fuel bay and the water-fill spur the design class is the person;
  • rocks and spillage are handled separately, by road maintenance (below).

The rule is only as good as the access discipline behind it. It turns a perception limit into an operating rule, and operations must know that it has done so (section 8.3).

LAB-06-01-01 evaluates the illustrative detection model of ahs_sim.perception (LiDAR, clear air) at a per-frame detection probability of 0.9 [A]:

Detection range, clear [A] 44.2 m 81.0 m 117.8 m 95.7 m 51.5 m 33.1 m

With the design-class rule every one of the 96 cases has its full-braking requirement inside the detection range of its design class in clear air. A class C stop is possible for the design class in only 44 of them. Applied to a person everywhere, 13 cases would fail — the 50 km/h roads and the 45 km/h dump access road, where the full-braking range (up to 51.8 m) exceeds the person's 44.2 m.

Rocks and spillage are a road-maintenance requirement. A 0.5 m rock is reliably detected at 33.1 m in the model. That supports a full-braking stop up to 37.0 km/h on a level road and 33.3 km/h descending R05 — below R05's 40 km/h. A rock fallen on R05 is therefore not something the truck can be relied on to stop for at the limit; clearing spillage promptly, and reporting it, is part of the protection (section 8.3). Whether a truck should stop for a small rock or drive round or over it is a design decision with its own risks (tyre damage, a sudden manoeuvre on a ramp); the point here is only that the sensing requirement alone does not cover it.

For the light-vehicle design class the model supports 37.9 km/h on R03 in dust and 35.8 km/h in spray, so the 30 km/h derate covers it. On the 30 km/h roads whose design class is the person — R07, R08, R10, R11, R13 — the model supports 19.7 km/h in dust, and 18.0 km/h on the bench ramp R09, below the 30 km/h of the ODD rule. Both sets of numbers are illustrative; the lesson is the method.   3. Rain closes more than the ramp. In rain the design class is not covered at the limit on R01, R03 and R12 (50 km/h; 46.7–47.1 km/h supported), R09 (person class, loaded descent; 25.2 km/h) and R05 (36.9 km/h). ch-04-02 already puts R05 outside the ODD in rain on grade alone and caps eight segments at 30 km/h, which is more restrictive than the model requires.

The model treats a condition as uniform. In reality dust is patchy and drifts with the wind, spray lies where WT02 has just passed, and the worst case is the patch the truck enters at speed. That is why the truck's own estimate of its sensing (ch-06-02) and the operational controls on dust and watering (sections 6.4 and 8.3) belong to the same design.

4.6 Coverage around the truck

A required range answers "how far ahead". Coverage answers "where around the truck", and the answer depends on the manoeuvre.

Forward, and the near-field blind zone. The illustrative forward LiDAR (DEFAULT_LIDAR: mounted 4.0 m high, beams from −25° to +5° in 0.4° steps [A]) looks far ahead but cannot look straight down. Its lowest beam reaches the ground 8.6 m ahead; a target closer than that is seen only if it is tall enough to reach a beam. LAB-06-01-01 finds the nearest range at which a target gets any return:

Target height 1.8 m (standing person) 1.0 m (crouching person, small object) 0.5 m (rock, person lying)
Blind within 4.72 m 6.44 m 7.51 m

A truck that has stopped cannot see with this sensor someone who has walked up to its bumper. Before it moves off, the near field must be covered by other sensors or confirmed clear by procedure. The handbook rule of ch-04-05 — the ADS checks its surroundings before moving after a restart — therefore needs near-field coverage to be meaningful (REQ-06-01-005).

Rear, for reversing into EX02-L and C01. A truck spots at 5.0 km/h (the Spotting speed of ahs_sim.planning.BehaviourMachine, as in ch-07-05). With the same latency and decision budget, a class C stop and a 2.0 m stand-off [A], the rear requirement is only 5.05 m — at EX02-L (empty) and at C01 (loaded) alike. The difficulty is not range but geometry and context:

  • the rear range must be covered close to the truck, where a high-mounted forward-type sensor is blind; the lab's generic near-field sensor (2.0 m high, beams down to −60° [A]) sees a person from 0.12 m;
  • at EX02-L the truck reverses towards the shovel, an object it is meant to approach; perception must keep the shovel and its bucket from triggering a stop while still detecting a person or a light vehicle next to them;
  • at C01 the truck reverses towards the bund and the crusher wall, with people sometimes working on the crusher pad.

The operational side is simple and strict: nobody stands or parks behind a truck that may reverse, and the Shovel Operator controls the loading area (section 8.3).

Sides, at Intersections X and V. Traffic at X and V is coordinated by AMS reservations: a truck enters an intersection only with the full grant (ch-10-05, ch-07-04). Could side perception do the same job? A truck crossing from a standstill must cover the road width (30 m, mine.yaml) plus its own length (13.0 m, equipment.yaml) at its comfort acceleration of 0.6 m/s² (ahs_sim.dynamics): 11.97 s. During that time a vehicle on the crossing road travels

These ranges are far beyond what side sensors on a truck can be relied on for, especially for a light vehicle seen end-on or in dust. Crossing decisions must therefore come from reservations and permissions — a light vehicle crosses X only under a transit permission that locks the intersection (ch-14-06) — and side perception is a last layer: it detects a vehicle or person that has entered the conflict area without a permission, close enough to stop for, and it covers the swept path of a turning truck (ch-07-05). That is a coverage requirement on the near and middle field at the sides, not a long-range one.

4.7 Missed detections, false detections and what the range does not say

A required range is a geometric statement. It assumes that an object inside the detection range is detected and an object that is not there is not reported. Neither is true with certainty.

  • Misses. The detection range uses a per-frame probability of 0.9. Several frames per second raise the chance of a timely detection only if misses are independent from frame to frame. In dust they are not: the same cloud hides the object in consecutive frames. This is the dependence ch-04-08 found between perception and the observer in poor visibility, now inside one sensor. The safety argument must use the probability of detection in the condition, not an average over conditions (ch-06-07).
  • False detections. A truck tuned to see a person at 44.2 m in clear air will also report things that are not there — returns from spray and dust. In SCN-WATER-WT02 one pass in five over wet R03 ends in a nuisance stop (ch-01-05, ch-14-05, ch-16-07); ahs_sim.perception.rate_from_pass_probability turns that into 0.159 false stops per kilometre on R03 (1403.6 m). That number is a productivity loss, not a safety credit.

The two errors are linked by the same threshold, which is why they must be reported separately: reducing nuisance stops by raising the threshold shortens the detection range, and the required range does not shrink with it. The safety argument is never weakened to reduce nuisance stops (ch-06-07).

4.8 Model limits

Simplification Effect Treated in

None of these changes the structure of the result: the requirement is set by speed, surface and the design class, and the detection range is set by class and condition.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

In clear air, day or night, every case of the network has its full-braking requirement inside the detection range of its design class, and on the 15 km/h links even a person is detected beyond the class C range. Perception stops are then mostly true detections of expected objects — a truck ahead, a dozer at EX02 — handled by following and hold-off (ch-07-04), and occasional nuisance stops.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-PERSON-EX01 (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

The registry gives T+0 s (detection, stop), T+10 s (alarm, no matching PRU or permission) and T+15 s (escalation to the area remote stop). The detection distance (60 m) and the truck (DT09 on R17 at 15 km/h) are those of ch-07-04. Rows marked [A] are added by this chapter from LAB-06-01-01.

T+0 to T+5.7 s [A] Class C stop: 0.5 s latency and decision, 1 s actuation, braking at 1.0 m/s² 14.9 m travelled; at rest 45.1 m from the person (ch-07-04's behaviour model, which brakes at once: 51.5 m) —

The detecting truck had a comfortable margin: 60 m against 24.9 m. The other trucks are the reason for T+15 s. In clear air a truck on the 30 km/h approaches R07 or R08 detects a person reliably at 44.2 m against a full-braking requirement of 28.2 m, so its own perception is a real layer during those fifteen seconds. In dust it detects a person at 19.9 m and is not.

6.3 Degraded operation — the same person in dust [A]

Suppose EX01 has been loading on a dry, still morning and the approach is in dust (a variant, not a registered scenario). The per-frame detection probability of the person at 60 m is then practically zero. It rises to 0.65 at 24.9 m — the class C range of R17 — and to 0.97 at 16.2 m, the full-braking range. The person is reliably detected (p ≥ 0.9) only from 19.9 m: inside the class C range, outside the full-braking range. The truck would still stop short, but by full braking, without the 45 m of margin of the clear-air case, and only if the misses in the dust are not correlated from frame to frame (section 4.7). The derate the model supports on R17 in dust is the 15 km/h limit itself; on the R07 approach, 19.7 km/h.

6.4 Degraded operation — SCN-WATER-WT02 on R03

WT02 waters R03 on a hot, dry shift. Residual spray lies on the road for minutes; a truck passing through it stops for an obstacle that is not there (registry: stop T+0 s, review T+30 s, resume T+45 s; ch-14-05, ch-16-07). From the requirements side two things happen at once:

  • the detection range shrinks — for the light-vehicle design class of R03 from 81.0 m to 44.5 m in the model, which supports 35.8 km/h on R03 and is why the ODD derate to 30 km/h of ch-04-02 is consistent here;
  • false detections rise — 0.159 per kilometre on wet R03, one pass in five.

The first is a safety requirement on speed; the second is a productivity loss. They are handled by different people: the derate by the ODD and the controller; the nuisance stops by the classification rule of ch-16-07 and the watering pattern decided by the Pit Supervisor (ch-14-05). ch-06-02 shows why spray produces returns that clustering turns into obstacles.

6.5 Conditions beyond the sensing assumptions

Some conditions are not covered by any number in this chapter: heavy rain beyond what the ODD calls rain; smoke from a truck fire (SCN-FIRE-DT17) drifting across a road; a sun low in the east over the R01 exit in the morning [A]; unusual objects — a fallen light tower, a tyre, a large boulder, an animal. The perception system was not shown to work in them. The operational answer is a report and a restriction, not a guess (section 8.3 B).

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Choose the stop model per question. The class C model answers "when can we still stop gently"; the full-braking model answers "when can we no longer stop". Requirements that use only class C ask for detection ranges no sensor achieves for a person at 50 km/h (127.3 m); requirements that use only full braking hide the cost of hard stops.

Count the latency where it is. ch-04-05's 84.0 m contains a radio path that a perception stop does not have; using it unchanged would add 11.1 m at 40 km/h that does not exist, and hide the 5.6 m that does (latency and decision).

Fix the design class with the access rules. The design-class rule makes the requirement achievable on 50 km/h roads and makes the access rules safety-relevant. If the site changes the rules — people walking on haul roads to inspect windrows, say — the requirement changes too.

Derive ODD speeds from detection, not the other way round. The comparison with ch-04-02 (section 4.5) shows how easily a single "reduced visibility" speed is right for one design class and wrong for another.

Accept the cost of conservatism, and make it visible. Every margin in the budget — the 10 m stand-off, the double-counted 0.2 s with V07, a detection probability of 0.9 — lengthens the requirement and therefore lowers the speed allowed in poor conditions. Those choices belong in the safety case with their rationale, not in tuning parameters.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along SCN-PERSON-EX01

  • T+0 s. The stop decision is vehicle-side and immediate; nothing central can delay or override it. The ADS sends the AMS: truck, stop reason person, class C or full braking, distance at detection, object position, latch state and a snapshot.
  • T+0 → T+2 s [A]. The report reaches the HMI. The AMS holds trucks whose granted paths lead towards the detecting truck; it does not stop the others — only the remote stop does that.
  • T+2 → T+10 s. The controller compares the object position with PRU positions and permissions. The comparison needs the object position in the mine grid, not just "60 m ahead of DT09".
  • T+15 s. The area remote stop acts through the independent stop radio (ch-04-05); the person latch on DT09 stays set underneath it.
  • Release. Two independent conditions: the remote stop reset at its source, and the person latch released by the controller's recorded decision after the person is accounted for (ch-07-04).

8.3 Procedure excerpts

A — Person detected (SCN-PERSON-EX01), keyed to section 6.2.

  1. AHS Controller (T+0 to 10 s): open the snapshot; confirm the truck is stopped and latched; check PRU positions and permissions near the object position; call the Shovel Operator of EX01.
  2. AHS Controller (T+10 to 15 s): no match — escalate to the area remote stop of AA-North (ch-04-05); inform the Pit Supervisor and the ERT standby.
  3. Shovel Operator: keep the bucket still; report what can be seen from the cab; do not wave anyone through.
  4. Pit Supervisor: establish who the person is, why they are there and how they entered (ch-04-08); record it.
  5. AHS Controller: release the latch only when the person is accounted for and out of the area; reset and staged restart per ch-04-05. A snapshot that now "shows nothing" is not a release condition.

B — Reporting conditions beyond sensing assumptions.

  1. Anyone (Shovel Operators, water-truck and light-vehicle drivers, Field Technicians): report by radio to CR1: what (heavy rain, smoke, low sun, dust you cannot see through, an object on the road), where (segment, nearest marker), since when.
  2. AHS Controller: record the condition on the segment; for smoke or an unknown object on a road, hold traffic short of the place at once; for dust, spray, rain or sun, apply the ODD restriction and tell the Pit Supervisor.
  3. Pit Supervisor: decide watering, road maintenance, a lower speed or a closure; the AHS Controller applies it.
  4. AHS Controller: remove the restriction only on a report that the condition has cleared (not on the clock).

C — What trucks are not designed to detect: rules for people on foot and light vehicles in AA-North [H].

A truck is not designed to … Therefore
stop for a person on foot on a 40–50 km/h road at the limit People walk on haul roads only inside a work zone or permission that holds the trucks (ch-14-03, ch-16-05)
see a person within about 5 m of its front bumper with its forward sensors, or a small object within 7.5 m Never approach a truck that is not isolated; nobody stands in front of or behind a stopped truck (ch-16-05)
detect a person reliably at range in dust or spray Stay in the light vehicle in dust; report dust you cannot see through

The light vehicle is the design class on the haul roads, not an invitation to drive close: a light vehicle is detected at 81.0 m in clear air in the model, at 36.4 m in dust.

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • Controllers and supervisors understand the two required ranges and why a derate in dust is a safety measure, not a software quirk.
  • Everybody who enters AA-North knows the table of section 8.3 C, in particular the near-field blind zone and that a truck at speed on a haul road cannot stop for a person.
  • Shovel Operators know they are the eyes in the loading area and how to report a person, dust or an object.
  • Water-truck operators know that their spray shortens what trucks can see, and why the watering pattern is a perception control (ch-14-05).
  • Engineers can rebuild the required-range table from the stopping models and explain every [A] in it.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-06-01-001 [H] The sensing requirement for each road shall state two ranges per direction and payload: the range within which a stop is no longer possible (full braking, with measured perception and decision latency) and the range beyond which a controlled stop is possible, each computed from the segment's speed limit and grade with a stated margin. Rationale: one range hides either the safety question or the cost of hard stops (section 4.2). Verification: analysis (LAB-06-01-01 experiment B: 96 cases, e.g. R05 empty descending 88.4 m and 40.8 / 71.8 m)

REQ-06-01-002 [H] The full-braking range shall be computed for the lowest adhesion the ODD allows on that road and direction. Rationale: on R05 the wet requirement (71.8 m) is 31.0 m longer than the dry one (section 4.3). Verification: analysis; inspection of the requirement table against the ODD

REQ-06-01-003 [H] The design object class of each road shall be recorded together with the access rule that justifies it, and changed only through the change process that changes the rule. Rationale: the light-vehicle design class on 40–50 km/h roads is valid only while people on foot are excluded there (section 4.4). Verification: inspection of the AHS map attributes, access rules and change records

REQ-06-01-004 [H] Any ODD speed restriction for a visibility condition shall be shown to keep the full-braking range of each affected road inside the detection range of its design class in that condition. Rationale: a single visibility speed can be adequate for one class and not for another (section 4.5). Verification: analysis (LAB-06-01-01 experiment C); test of the restriction in the AMS configuration

REQ-06-01-005 [H] The ADS shall not move off after any stop unless the near field around the truck, including the zone in which its long-range sensors are blind, has been observed clear for a configured time [A]. Rationale: the forward sensor of the model is blind to a standing person within 4.72 m (section 4.6). Verification: test (static person targets around a stopped truck, front, rear and sides); inspection

REQ-06-01-006 [H] While reversing into a loading spot or tipping bay, the ADS shall keep detecting persons and vehicles within the rear required range, including next to an expected object such as the shovel or the crusher bund. Rationale: expected-object handling must not suppress the objects the reversing truck can hurt (section 4.6). Verification: test (person target beside the bucket at EX02-L and on the C01 pad during Spotting)

REQ-06-01-007 [H] Entry into Intersections X and V shall be decided by AMS reservations and permissions; side perception shall act as a stop layer for objects entering the conflict area and the truck's swept path, and shall not be credited for crossing decisions. Rationale: seeing 50 km/h crossing traffic in time would need 166.3 m of side range (section 4.6). Verification: inspection of the intersection logic; test with a vehicle entering the conflict area without a grant

REQ-06-01-008 [H] The AMS shall record condition reports (dust, spray, rain, smoke, low sun, unknown object) per segment with time and source, and shall apply the configured restriction or hold until a report that the condition has cleared. Rationale: conditions beyond the sensing assumptions are known to people before any truck (section 6.5). Verification: test (condition report → restriction → clearance); inspection of records

10. Verification and validation

  • Analysis. The required-range table is rebuilt from the stopping models and the measured latency; LAB-06-01-01 shows the structure with [A] values and reproduces the stopping distances of ch-04-05 and ch-07-02 (84.0, 72.8, 25.2, 35.9, 56.2, 122.2 m).
  • Measurement of the inputs. Perception latency and decision time from logged detections and stop commands; stopping distances from site stop tests (ch-18-03), on dry and wet surfaces.
  • Detection range by class and condition. Static and moving targets of each design class at known ranges, in clear air, at night and in dust or spray, with enough trials to state the probability (ch-06-07, ch-18-03). Whether standards or guidance prescribe such tests for mining machines is not verified [TBV] (SRC-PUB-AHS-SITE-TEST-PRACTICE).
  • Coverage. Mapping of the blind zones around a stopped truck with targets of 0.5 m and 1.8 m; reversing tests at the shovel and the tipping bay with a person target beside the expected object.
  • Operational checks. Inspection that the design class per road matches the access rules; exercises of procedures 8.3 A and B.

The safety of autonomous machine systems is the subject of ISO 17757:2019, Earth-moving machinery and mining — Autonomous and semi-autonomous machine system safety [F] (SRC-STD-ISO17757, catalogue level). Whether and how it addresses detection requirements is not verified, and no requirement in this chapter is taken from it. ISO 21448:2022, Road vehicles — Safety of the intended functionality [F] (SRC-STD-ISO21448), is a road-vehicle standard; treating the limits of sensing as "insufficiencies of the intended function" in a mine is the handbook's analogy [H] (ch-06-06).

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Quoting one "detection range" for the truck. Without class, condition and probability it cannot be compared with anything.
  • Using the remote-stop latency for a perception stop, or forgetting perception latency altogether.
  • Designing for dry roads. Wet R05 needs 71.8 m, not 40.8 m.
  • Assuming people can be seen on every road. At 50 km/h the model's person range (44.2 m) is shorter than the full-braking requirement.
  • Ignoring the near field. A truck that cannot see its own bumper must not be allowed to move off on the strength of a long-range sensor.
  • Averaging detection over conditions. The person in dust is the case that matters, not the annual mean.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • "The trucks can see us." True in some places, for some objects, in some conditions — never a reason to be near a moving truck.
  • Walking up to a stopped truck without permission and isolation.
  • Treating the dust derate as a nuisance and asking for it to be lifted for production.
  • Leaving spillage on R05 for the next grader pass.
  • Not reporting smoke or low sun because "the trucks have sensors".
  • Releasing a person latch because the snapshot no longer shows anybody.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — R05 by hand. Empty, descending, 40 km/h = 11.11 m/s. Latency and decision: 11.11 × 0.5 = 5.6 m. Class C stop without remote-stop latency: 11.1 m actuation + 61.7 m braking = 72.8 m. Margin 10.0 m. Class C range: 88.4 m. Full braking dry: 5.6 + 25.2 + 10.0 = 40.8 m; wet: 5.6 + 56.2 + 10.0 = 71.8 m.

Example 2 — R03 at 50 km/h. 13.89 m/s; latency and decision 6.9 m; class C stop 110.3 m; margin 10 m: 127.3 m. A person in clear air is reliably detected at 44.2 m; the full-braking requirement on R03 (Y→X, empty) is 47.3 m. Hence the light-vehicle design class (81.0 m) and the rule for people on foot.

Example 3 — dust on the EX02 approach. R08 at 30 km/h, loaded towards Z: full-braking requirement 28.2 m dry. Person in dust: 19.9 m. The speed whose requirement fits is 19.7 km/h (lab, the lower of the four cases on R08). The ODD rule of 30 km/h leaves R08 unrestricted.

Example 4 — reversing at C01. 5.0 km/h = 1.39 m/s; latency and decision 0.7 m; class C stop 2.4 m (1.39 m actuation, 0.96 m braking); stand-off 2.0 m: 5.05 m. Short — but entirely inside the forward sensor's blind zone if a forward-type sensor were used at the rear.

Example 5 — side range at X. 2×43/0.6 = 11.97 s to clear 30 m of road and 13 m of truck from standstill; at 50 km/h a crossing vehicle travels 166.3 m in that time.

15. Key takeaways

  • Required detection range = speed × (perception latency + decision) + stopping distance + margin. Two stops give two ranges: 88.4 m for a controlled stop and 40.8 / 71.8 m for full braking (dry / wet) on R05 at 40 km/h.
  • Speed dominates: 24.9 m at 15 km/h, 57.2 m at 30, 88.4 m at 40, 127.3 m at 50 for a controlled stop.
  • The object the truck must stop for depends on the road, and is fixed by the access rules: light vehicle on 40–50 km/h roads, person elsewhere. Rocks and spillage on fast roads are a road-maintenance task.
  • Dust and spray halve the model's detection ranges; ODD speeds for visibility must be derived from them, per design class.
  • A large truck is blind close to its bumper; reversing needs near-field coverage and a clear area, not range.
  • Side perception cannot decide crossings at X and V; reservations and permissions do.
  • Missed and false detections share a threshold but not a budget; the safety argument is never traded for fewer nuisance stops.
