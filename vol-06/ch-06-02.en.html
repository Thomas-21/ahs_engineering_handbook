1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is early afternoon on a hot, dry day shift. Two minutes after WT02 has finished its pass on R03, DT24, loaded for D01, comes to rest in residual spray (SCN-WATER-WT02). On the HMI the AHS Controller opens the stop's snapshot: a round, pale blob of points a few metres across in the lane ahead, nothing with a straight edge, nothing with the shape of a vehicle. The detection has cleared, the watering log shows R03 watered at that place, and at T+45 s the controller authorises the resume (ch-14-05, ch-16-07).

Later in the shift DT24 starts to slow on R03 and R01 where nobody else does. The HMI shows Derated — sensing for DT24 alone. When it is parked for fuel, the Field Technician finds a film of dried mud on the forward LiDAR window — the spray that stopped the truck at lunchtime has left its mark.

Two people ask questions. The controller: "What exactly was I looking at in that snapshot? Could there have been a person inside the blob?" The Maintenance Planner: "How often do we have to clean these windows, and how do we know a truck has to come out of service rather than just slow down?"

The questions this chapter answers:

  • How does a LiDAR measure range and build a point cloud, and how do scan pattern and angular resolution decide how many points land on a person at a given range?
  • Are there enough points on a person at the required detection ranges of ch-06-01?
  • How do window contamination, dust and spray reduce what the LiDAR sees, and how can a truck detect its own degraded sensing?
  • How does the processing chain — ground segmentation, clustering, dust filtering — turn points into obstacles, and why does residual spray become an obstacle while thin dust does not?
  • Why is the dust filter a safety decision and not a tuning knob?
  • What can a point-cloud snapshot on the HMI tell a controller, and what can it not?

This chapter covers:

  • time-of-flight ranging, scan pattern and angular resolution, and the number of returns on target versus range against ch-06-01's required ranges (LAB-06-02-01; FIG-06-02-01);
  • a simplified range equation for target reflectivity, window transmission and dust or spray extinction;
  • ground segmentation, clustering and dust filtering on a synthetic scene at EX02-Q with LV07, a person-sized object and dust (FIG-06-02-02, FIG-06-02-03), including the case where a person at the edge of a dust cloud is thrown away with it;
  • self-checks by which a truck estimates its own sensing and derates or stops [A];
  • SCN-WATER-WT02 walked through from the first spray return to the resume; SCN-PERSON-EX01 for sparse returns on a person;
  • the operations side: cleaning routine, fault indications, the decision to keep a truck in service or withdraw it, cleaning frequency, and what a snapshot can and cannot show.

Every sensor parameter, reflectivity, transmission, extinction coefficient, threshold and speed in this chapter is an illustrative assumption [A] computed with ahs_sim 0.15.1. No LiDAR product, scan pattern of any product or vendor capability is described, and no value is a sensor specification or a requirement.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Explain LiDAR time-of-flight ranging, scan patterns and angular resolution, and how they determine the number of returns on a person-sized target at a given range.
  2. Calculate returns on target versus range for [A] sensor parameters and compare them with the required detection ranges from ch-06-01.
  3. Implement basic point-cloud processing — ground segmentation, clustering and filtering of dust and rain returns — on a synthetic scene.
  4. Analyse how window contamination, dust and spray attenuate LiDAR returns, and design self-checks by which a truck detects its own degraded sensing.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Recognise causes of LiDAR performance loss (dirty or damaged windows, mud, dust build-up) and the inspection and cleaning routine carried out by Field Technicians.
  2. Interpret LiDAR-related fault indications on the HMI and decide, with maintenance, whether the truck can stay in service or must be withdrawn.
  3. Plan sensor-cleaning frequency with maintenance from site conditions (dry season, wet roads after watering) and nuisance-stop data.
  4. Describe what a point-cloud snapshot shown on the HMI after a stop can and cannot tell a controller.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

ch-06-01 set the requirement: on each road, the design object class must be detected beyond the full-braking range, and preferably beyond the controlled-stop range — 24.9 m and 16.5 m on the EX02 link R18, 57.2 m and 28.2 m on the approach R08, 88.4 m and 40.8 m (71.8 m wet) on R05 (largest per segment, LAB-06-01-01). It used a detection model that says nothing about how a truck sees. This chapter opens that box for the sensor that does most of the seeing in the handbook's generic truck: a scanning LiDAR.

At the reference mine three things make that sensor interesting to operations as well as to engineers.

  1. It sees spray and dust. A LiDAR measures every surface its pulse meets, including water droplets and dust particles. That is why residual spray after WT02 has watered R03 produces nuisance stops — one pass in five for two hours after watering, 27.3 stops in two hours, 13.6 per hour and 0.34 truck-hours of DLY-OD-03 (ch-01-05, ch-14-05, ch-16-07).
  2. It gets dirty. Spray dries to mud on the window, dust settles on it, a wet road throws slurry up from the tyres. A dirty window does not fail outright; it shortens the range a little at a time. Someone must notice.
  3. It is what the controller sees after a stop. The snapshot attached to a perception stop is typically a picture of points. The controller's classification of the stop — nuisance, genuine, unresolved (ch-16-07) — starts from it.

And one thing is not interesting at all until it goes wrong: a person standing in dust. The processing that removes dust returns to avoid nuisance stops is the same processing that can remove a person with them.

3. Terminology and foundations

LiDAR (glossary lidar). A laser ranging sensor that sends short light pulses and measures the time until each reflection returns; scanning LiDARs sweep many beams to build a point cloud (glossary point-cloud).

Time of flight (glossary time-of-flight). Round-trip time t of a pulse to a surface and back. The range is r=ct/2.

Return. One measured reflection: range, the beam's two angles, and an intensity — how strong the reflection was. A pulse may produce several returns (first and last); whether a given sensor reports them is product-specific and not assumed here.

Scan pattern and angular resolution (glossary angular-resolution). The set of directions the beams cover and the angle between neighbouring beams, horizontally and vertically. The illustrative sensor of ahs_sim (DEFAULT_LIDAR) has 0.2° horizontally, 0.4° vertically, from −25° to +5°, mounted 4.0 m above the ground [A].

Returns on target (glossary returns-on-target). The number of returns that fall on an object in one scan. It falls with range because the beams spread apart.

Ground segmentation (glossary ground-segmentation). Labelling each point as ground or not ground. Here: a point is ground if it lies within 0.25 m of the lowest point of its 2 m grid cell [A].

Clustering (glossary clustering). Grouping non-ground points that belong together. Here: connected occupied cells of a 0.5 m plan grid, at least 5 points per cluster [A].

Dust filter. A rule that discards clusters judged to be dust or spray. Here: mean intensity ≤ 0.2 and fewer than 20 points per cubic metre of the cluster's bounding box [A] (ahs_sim.perception.dust_filter).

Window contamination (glossary window-contamination). Dirt, mud, water film or damage on the sensor's optical window that reduces the light passing through it; expressed here as a one-pass transmission T (1.0 clean) [A].

Extinction. Loss of light in the air between sensor and target by scattering on particles or droplets; expressed as a coefficient α per metre [A].

Sensing self-check (glossary sensing-self-check). A check by which the truck estimates the quality of its own sensing from its own data — for example how far away it still receives ground returns — and acts on it.

Snapshot. The data attached to a stop report for the controller: in this handbook, the point cloud (or image) of the frame that caused the stop, with the detected object marked [H].

4. Technical explanation

4.1 Time-of-flight ranging

A pulse leaves the sensor, hits a surface and comes back. Its round-trip time gives the range:

r=ct2

with c the speed of light. A target at 50 m returns after 333.6 ns, one at 200 m — the illustrative maximum range of DEFAULT_LIDAR — after 1334.3 ns. Timing to a fraction of a nanosecond is what gives a LiDAR its centimetre-level range precision; it is also why a LiDAR measures droplets and dust as faithfully as it measures a person: a droplet in the beam reflects light at its own range like any other surface.

Each return also carries the beam's direction (azimuth and elevation) and an intensity. The three coordinates of the point follow from range and angles in the sensor frame; the extrinsic calibration (ch-06-03) moves them into the truck frame, and the truck's pose (Volume 05) into the mine grid.

4.2 Scan pattern and angular resolution

The illustrative sensor fires 76 beams spaced 0.4° apart vertically, from −25° to +5°, and samples every 0.2° of azimuth: 1800 columns per revolution, 136 800 points per scan [A]. The spacing between neighbouring returns grows with range: at 50 m it is 0.175 m horizontally and 0.349 m vertically. A target of width w and height h standing on flat ground at range r therefore receives about

N(r)≈⌊wrΔθh⌋×nv(r,h)

returns, where nv is the number of beams whose elevation lies between the angle to the target's foot and the angle to its top (ahs_sim.perception.returns_on_target). Both factors fall with range, so N falls roughly with the square of the range — and in steps, because beams are discrete.

FIG-06-02-01 (a) shows the beams in side view. The lowest beam meets the ground 8.6 m ahead; everything closer and lower than the beams is the near-field blind zone of ch-06-01 (4.72 m for a 1.8 m target). Above the horizon most beams look into the sky — useful on a ramp, wasted on the flat.

  2. On the fast roads a person is a few points. At R05's class C range (88.4 m) a person gets 2 returns — below the smallest cluster. At R03's full-braking range, 15. This is the geometric side of ch-06-01's design-class rule: a light vehicle still gets 21 returns at 88.4 m and 8 at 127.3 m.
  3. Points are necessary, not sufficient. Having 10 or 15 returns on a person says that a cluster can form; whether the detector reliably calls it a person, in which conditions, is the detection model's question. ch-06-01's model puts the person's 90 % detection range at 44.2 m, well inside the 85.9 m that geometry allows. The gap is the work detection, classification and confirmation have to do (ch-06-04).

4.4 How far the light gets: reflectivity, window and air

Geometry says where beams go. Whether a reflection is strong enough to be measured depends on how much light comes back. LAB-06-02-01 uses a simplified range equation for a target larger than the beam footprint [A]:

P∝ρT2e−2αRR2

with target reflectivity ρ, one-pass window transmission T (the light crosses the window twice), extinction coefficient α of the air and range R. The reference is DEFAULT_LIDAR's 200 m maximum range for a target of reflectivity 0.8 through a clean window in clear air [A]. The form of the equation is textbook radiometry; it is not verified against a source here [TBV] (SRC-PUB-LIDAR-PRINCIPLES), and the model ignores beam footprint, detector noise and multiple returns.

Three consequences follow, all [A]:

  • Dark targets are the limit. A person in dark work clothing (reflectivity 0.10) returns enough light up to 70.7 m through a clean window — shorter than the 85.9 m of geometry. High-visibility clothing and retroreflective strips raise the reflectivity; mud on the clothing lowers it.
  • The window acts linearly. Without extinction the range scales with T: a window that passes half the light (T = 0.5) halves the range, to 35.4 m for the dark person.
  • Dust and spray act exponentially. The lab chooses α so that the clear-air range shrinks by the condition factors of ahs_sim.perception (0.45 in dust, 0.55 in spray): α = 0.0251 per metre for dust and 0.0154 per metre for spray. In dust the dark person's returns end at 31.8 m.

The same scale factors apply to the detection range of ch-06-01 (44.2 m for a person, clean window, clear air). With a calibrated α, a clean window reproduces ch-06-01's 19.9 m in dust and 24.3 m in spray exactly; a dirty window compounds them:

(Supported speed as in ch-06-01: the highest speed whose full-braking requirement fits within the detection range; dry road in clear air and dust, wet road in spray.) A window that passes 70 % of the light — dirty enough to see, not dirty enough to look alarming — takes a truck that could run 47.5 km/h for a person on the level down to 34.7 km/h; in dust, from 20.8 to 14.7 km/h. Dirt and dust multiply. That is the engineering reason for cleaning, and the operational reason why a truck that has been through spray may slow down later in the shift.

4.5 From points to obstacles: a synthetic scene at EX02-Q

FIG-06-02-02 shows the processing chain of the handbook's teaching model: self-check, point cloud in the truck frame, ground segmentation, clustering, dust filter, object candidates, then detection, classification and tracking (ch-06-04) and the ADS behaviour. The ground points go to terrain and free-space estimation (ch-06-05).

LAB-06-02-01 builds a synthetic scene (ahs_sim.perception.synthetic_scene, seed 602) as seen from a truck at EX02-Q looking towards EX02-L [A]: a ground grid with 5 cm noise, LV07 parked 25 m ahead and 3 m to the left, a person-sized box 35 m ahead and 4 m to the right, and a dust cloud 3 m in radius 15 m ahead (300 low-intensity returns, as raised by loading or by DZ03 cleaning the floor). Scene C1 gives 11 632 points:

  • Ground segmentation keeps 11 292 points as ground. Ten points at the feet of the objects are taken for ground and six noisy ground points for objects — the price of a simple cell-minimum rule.
  • Clustering of the non-ground points finds three clusters: the dust (298 points), LV07 (30) and the person (6).
  • The dust filter discards the dust cluster: mean intensity 0.085, 3.2 points per cubic metre. LV07 (intensity 0.664, 84.0 per m³) and the person (0.747, 83.3 per m³) remain obstacles.

The scene generator thins the faces of objects with range by its own simple rule, not by the beam geometry of section 4.2: it puts 6 points on the person at 35 m where returns_on_target gives 28. The scene exercises the processing chain; its point counts are not the sensor's resolution. Since ahs_sim 0.15.1 synthetic_scene(beams=…) can thin the faces by the scanner geometry instead; the lab keeps the default, so its numbers stay as computed here.

Residual spray becomes an obstacle (C2). Replace the thin dust by a dense patch of residual spray — 2000 returns within 2.5 m [A]. The cluster (1985 points) has the same low intensity (0.084) but 29.1 points per cubic metre: above the density threshold, so it is not filtered. It is an obstacle, and a truck that meets it stops. That is the mechanism behind the nuisance stops of SCN-WATER-WT02: dense spray looks to the filter like something solid. FIG-06-02-03 (centre) shows it.

4.6 The dust filter is a safety decision

A designer who wants fewer nuisance stops will be tempted to make the filter more aggressive: lower the density threshold, or filter on intensity alone. LAB-06-02-01 shows what that costs.

Scene [A] What happens Result
C3: person 16 m ahead, 2.5 m to the left, at the edge of the thin dust The person's 21 points and the dust's 298 fall into connected cells and form one cluster; mean intensity 0.127, 3.4 points per m³ The whole cluster is filtered as dust: the person is discarded
C4: C3 with a protective filter — keep a dust cluster that contains a compact sub-cluster of bright returns (intensity ≥ 0.3, 0.25 m grid, ≥ 5 points) The person's returns form such a sub-cluster The cluster is kept as an obstacle
C5: C1 with the person in dark, wet clothing (intensity × 0.25) Person cluster intensity 0.187 (below 0.2) but density 83.3 per m³ Kept: the density criterion protects it
C6: C5 with a filter on intensity alone Intensity 0.187 ≤ 0.2 The person is discarded

FIG-06-02-03 (right) shows C3. Three lessons:

  1. A filter acts on clusters, and clusters merge. A person next to a cloud is not a separate object to the clustering step unless something keeps them apart. Any filter that deletes whole clusters must be tested with people at the edge of and inside dust, not only beside it.
  2. Each criterion protects against a different failure. Intensity alone loses dark people; density alone loses people in thin clouds; together they lose a person merged into a cloud. The protective sub-cluster check of C4 is one mitigation [A]; others are finer clustering inside dust, or treating any dust cloud in the path as an obstacle until it clears.
  3. The thresholds are part of the safety argument. ahs_sim.perception.dust_filter says so in its own documentation: the thresholds are a safety decision, not a tuning knob. Changing them changes the missed-detection rate, which must be shown again (ch-06-07); it is not a production setting. The nuisance stops of C2 are the price of the conservative filter and are paid by operations through watering and classification, not by engineering through a looser filter (ch-14-05, ch-16-07).

4.7 Window contamination, dust and self-checks [A]

A truck cannot clean its own windows in the handbook's generic design, but it can notice that it needs to. The self-check of LAB-06-02-01 uses the road itself as a test target [A]:

  • On a straight, level stretch, the furthest ground return depends on the ground's reflectivity (0.2 [A]) and on what the light passes through. With a clean window in clear air it is 100.0 m; the range equation makes it proportional to T. The truck therefore estimates T^ = observed furthest ground return ÷ 100.0 m.
  • T^ scales the detection range of the road's design class (ch-06-01), and the truck computes the speed that range supports with full braking.
  • Action [H] with thresholds [A]: NORMAL if the supported speed is at least the current speed; DERATE to the supported speed if it is lower; STOP and report if T^ < 0.5.
Window T Furthest ground return R08 (person, 30 km/h) R03 (light vehicle, loaded 46.9 km/h)
1.0 100.0 m 44.2 m → 47.5 km/h, NORMAL 81.0 m → 60.0 km/h, NORMAL
0.7 70.0 m 30.9 m → 34.7 km/h, NORMAL 56.7 m → 54.0 km/h, NORMAL
0.5 50.0 m 22.1 m → 23.9 km/h, DERATE 40.5 m → 41.8 km/h, DERATE
0.3 30.0 m 13.3 m → 8.9 km/h, STOP 24.3 m → 25.6 km/h, STOP

(60.0 km/h is the search cap.) The self-check has two weaknesses that the design must address:

  • It cannot tell the window from the air. Dust between the truck and the road shortens the ground range just as a dirty window does. The answer is to look at the pattern: dust is local — it comes and goes with place and time, and other trucks see it too; contamination is persistent — it follows one truck everywhere. Many LiDARs also see returns from the window itself at almost zero range; a growing count of such returns points to the window [TBV] (SRC-PUB-LIDAR-PRINCIPLES). Either way the truck derates for what it can see now; the cause decides what people do.
  • It needs a known scene. On a crest, a curve or a ramp the ground range is set by geometry, not by the window. The check runs only where the AHS map says the road ahead is straight and level for long enough [A], and its result is held between such places.

A complete design adds checks that do not need a scene: no data, stale data, a frame rate below nominal, an internal fault reported by the sensor, a mismatch between sensors that should see the same object (ch-06-03, ch-06-06). Each ends in a defined state and a report to the AMS (REQ-06-02-005).

4.8 What a point-cloud snapshot can and cannot show

After a perception stop the controller sees a snapshot — here, the point cloud of the frame that caused the stop with the detected object marked [H]. It is evidence for the classification rule of ch-16-07, and it has limits that follow directly from sections 4.2–4.6.

A snapshot can show A snapshot cannot show
Where the detected object was, how large, how far Whether a person is inside or behind a dust or spray cloud (C3)
Its shape: straight faces of a vehicle, a diffuse round cloud of spray What is below the lowest beam near the truck (the 4.72 m blind zone for a standing person)
Its intensity, if displayed: spray and dust are faint, vehicles and clothing brighter What is behind a windrow, a crest or another vehicle
Whether the object is on the road or at the edge Motion — one frame has none; "the detection has cleared" comes from later frames, not from the snapshot
That the frame was degraded — few ground returns, short range A small or distant object: a person at 80 m is 6 points, at R05's class C range 2

Two rules follow [H]. A snapshot that shows spray is evidence for a nuisance stop only together with a cleared detection and the watering record (ch-16-07 REQ-16-07-007). And a snapshot that shows nothing is not evidence that nothing is there (ch-04-08): the person may be below, behind or inside.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

Clean window, clear air: the furthest ground return is 100.0 m in the model, every road's design class is detected beyond its full-braking range (ch-06-01), the dust filter removes thin dust from loading and haul roads, and perception stops are mostly genuine — trucks ahead, a dozer at EX02, LV07 parked where it should not be.

6.3 Abnormal variant — a person at the edge of the cloud (SCN-PERSON-SPRAY-R03) [A]

Suppose a person had been standing at the edge of the patch — a contractor who walked across R03 against the rules. In scene C3 the person's returns merge with the cloud and the whole cluster is discarded by the ahs_sim filter: the truck would not stop for spray, and not stop for the person. With the protective filter of C4, the truck stops — for what looks like spray in the snapshot. Two things then protect the person: the detection does not clear (a person stays, spray drifts and settles), and the classification rule of ch-16-07 does not allow a nuisance classification while the detection persists. A snapshot that "shows spray" is not enough; the person is visible in the record of persistence, not in the picture. This is why the protective filter is required (REQ-06-02-003) and why the rule needs the cleared detection (section 4.8).

The variant is registered as SCN-PERSON-SPRAY-R03 (variant of SCN-WATER-WT02), with times [A]: T+0 s DT24 stops for an object in the patch and the snapshot reaches CR1; T+30 s the snapshot shows spray but the detection has not cleared, so the stop is not classified a nuisance; T+60 s no remote resume — the controller requests a field check under permission and holds the trucks short; T+600 s the Pit Supervisor finds the person, escorts them off R03 and reports the incident, and DT24 is released after the check.

6.4 Degraded operation — the dirty window

After passing through the mist at T+50 s, DT24's window carries droplets that dry into a film [A]. Over the following hours its self-check finds the furthest ground return falling. At 70 m (T^ = 0.7) the truck stays NORMAL on R08 and on R03 at its operating speed. At 50 m (T^ = 0.5) it derates — to 23.9 km/h on R08 and 41.8 km/h on R03 — and reports Derated — sensing, suspected window, because no other truck shows the same at the same places. The controller informs the Maintenance Planner; the Field Technician cleans the window at the next fuelling stop under the isolation procedure (ch-16-05), and the truck's ground range returns to 100 m. Had the estimate fallen below 0.5, the truck would have stopped and reported, and been withdrawn for cleaning (section 8.3 B).

6.5 Degraded operation — sensor fault

A sensor that reports an internal fault, stops sending data or sends stale data is not degraded but lost. The ADS stops the truck (it cannot verify its path) and reports Sensor fault — LiDAR front [H]. The truck stays stopped until a Field Technician has inspected it under isolation; restart follows the sensor checks after maintenance (section 8.3 B, ch-06-03 for recalibration).

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Range from geometry and from light, whichever is shorter. Beam spacing decides how many points a target can get; the light budget decides whether they come back. For a dark person the light limit (70.7 m) is shorter than the geometric one (85.9 m); for a light vehicle neither binds before 200 m.

Treat the dust filter as part of the safety function. The thresholds trade nuisance stops against missed persons. They are fixed in the safety case, verified with persons at the edge of and inside dust, and changed only through the change process (REQ-06-02-003, REQ-06-02-004).

Prefer stopping for spray to missing a person in it. Scene C2 costs one 45 s stop; scene C3 without protection could cost a life. The design takes C2's cost and gives operations the tools to reduce it — watering patterns, the watering log on the HMI, classification evidence — rather than reducing it in the filter.

Make the truck know its own sensing. A self-check that estimates its range and derates closes the gap between a clean-window design and a dirty-window reality. Its thresholds [A] are themselves safety parameters.

Report the suspected cause. The truck derates for what it sees; operations needs to know whether to call maintenance (window) or the Pit Supervisor (dust).

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along SCN-WATER-WT02

  • T−9.8 s → T−9.3 s [A]. Perception latency and decision (0.5 s): the patch is confirmed and the stop commanded. The stop class is chosen by distance (ch-04-05): 60 m is beyond the 57.2 m class C range, so the stop is controlled.
  • T+0 s → T+2 s. The stop report carries the snapshot frame, the object's position in the mine grid, its size and mean intensity, and the truck's sensing state. The AMS adds WT02's last position and the watering log entry for the place.
  • T+2 s → T+30 s. "Detection cleared" is a statement about the frames after the snapshot. The ADS must report the persistence of the detection, not only the first frame.
  • T+45 s. The resume is an authorisation; the ADS still verifies its path with fresh frames before moving (ch-07-04).
  • Later. Sensing-state reports (estimated range, suspected cause) are sent on change and at a low periodic rate [A]; the AMS groups them by truck and by place (ch-05-05 and ch-16-08 use the same pattern logic for localisation).

8.3 Procedure excerpts

A — Snapshot review after a perception stop (keyed to section 6.2; consistent with ch-16-07 playbook C).

  1. AHS Controller (T+2 to 30 s): open the snapshot. Look for shape (straight faces or a diffuse cloud), size, place in the lane, and whether the frame is degraded (few ground points, short range).
  2. AHS Controller: check whether the detection persists in later frames — the HMI's persistence flag, not the picture.
  3. AHS Controller: check WT02's position and the watering log for the place.
  4. AHS Controller (T+30 s): nuisance only if the snapshot shows spray or dust, the detection has cleared and the log explains it. A cloud with a persisting detection is unresolved, whatever the picture looks like: no remote resume, field check under permission.
  5. AHS Controller (T+45 s): authorise the resume and record the evidence; code DLY-OD-03 with the watering reference.

B — Degraded sensing or sensor fault: stay in service or withdraw.

  1. AHS Controller: on Derated — sensing, look at the pattern: one truck at many places (window) or several trucks at one place (environment).
  2. AHS Controller: environment — inform the Pit Supervisor (dust or spray); apply condition restrictions (ch-06-01 procedure B). Window — inform the Maintenance Planner.
  3. AHS Controller with the Maintenance Planner: a derated truck may stay in service to its next planned stop if the derate holds and the estimate is stable [A]; withdraw it at once if the estimate keeps falling, if the truck has stopped on the threshold, or on a sensor fault.
  4. Field Technician: clean or inspect only on an isolated truck, at the park-up PK1, the fuel bay FB1 or in the workshop (ch-16-05). After cleaning, confirm that the truck's ground range has returned to its clean value on the next straight stretch, and record it.
  5. Maintenance Planner: record the event against the truck; three events on the same sensor within a period [A] make it a reliability item (ch-06-03, Volume 18).

C — Inspection and cleaning routine (Field Technician) [H], with intervals [A].

When What
Pre-start of each shift, at PK1 Visual check of every perception window and lens: cracks, chips, mud, water film; clean with the approved method and materials (manufacturer's instructions [TBV], SRC-PUB-PERCEPTION-SENSOR-MAINTENANCE)
Each fuelling stop at FB1 in the dry season Forward and rear windows wiped
After washdown or work near a sensor Check for damage and alignment; sensor checks before return to service (ch-06-03)
On Derated — sensing, suspected window Clean at the next planned stop; at once if the estimate keeps falling
On Sensor fault or a cracked window Inspection; repair or replacement; recalibration and check before return to service

D — Planning the cleaning frequency (Maintenance Planner with the Pit Supervisor).

  1. Plot each truck's estimated sensing range over the shift and the times of cleaning.
  2. Overlay the watering log and the nuisance-stop record by time since watering (ch-14-05, ch-16-07): trucks that pass through fresh spray foul faster.
  3. Set the interval so that the estimate rarely reaches the derate threshold [A]; shorten it in the dry season (dust) and on days with heavy watering (spray film).
  4. Review monthly with perception engineering: a falling interval is a design question (window position, protection), not only a maintenance one.

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • Controllers can read a point-cloud snapshot: shape, size, intensity, degraded frame — and know its limits (section 4.8).
  • Controllers and supervisors know that a derate for sensing is the truck protecting its stopping distance and that the suspected cause decides whom to call.
  • Field Technicians know the cleaning routine, the approved materials, the isolation procedure and the check after cleaning.
  • Maintenance Planners can use the sensing trend and the nuisance-stop record to set and review cleaning intervals.
  • Engineers can explain why the dust filter's thresholds are safety parameters and show the C3 case.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-06-02-001 [H] For each road, the LiDAR (alone or with the other sensors it is fused with) shall provide at least the returns needed to form a cluster on the design object class at its full-braking range, for the lowest target reflectivity assumed for that class [A] and through the cleanest and the dirtiest window state allowed in service. Rationale: geometry and light both limit the range; at R05's class C range a person gets 2 returns (section 4.3), a dark person's returns end at 70.7 m (section 4.4). Verification: analysis (LAB-06-02-01 experiments A and B); test with low-reflectivity targets at the required ranges

REQ-06-02-002 [H] Point-cloud processing shall be verified against scenes containing dust, spray and rain returns together with the design object classes, and shall report for each test the clusters formed, filtered and kept. Rationale: the dense spray of scene C2 is kept, thin dust is filtered (section 4.5); both must be known, not assumed. Verification: test (LAB-06-02-01 scenes C1 and C2; field data with labelled spray)

REQ-06-02-003 [H] A dust or spray filter shall not discard a cluster that contains returns consistent with a person or vehicle, including when they are merged with dust returns; its thresholds shall be recorded in the safety case and changed only through the change process. Rationale: scene C3 discards a person merged with dust; an intensity-only filter discards a dark person (C6) (section 4.6). Verification: test (LAB-06-02-01 scenes C3–C6; persons at the edge of and inside dust in field tests); inspection of the change record

REQ-06-02-004 [H] Changes to perception thresholds intended to reduce nuisance stops shall be accompanied by a new demonstration of the missed-detection performance for each design object class in dust and spray. Rationale: the threshold that removes spray stops is the one that removes people in spray (section 4.6; ch-06-07). Verification: inspection of change records; test

REQ-06-02-005 [H] The ADS shall estimate its sensing range during operation, derate to the speed that range supports for the road's design object class, stop and report when the estimate falls below a configured threshold [A], and stop and report on any sensor fault, loss of data or stale data. Rationale: a window passing 70 % of the light takes the supported speed from 47.5 to 34.7 km/h (section 4.4). Verification: test (LAB-06-02-01 experiment D; field test with calibrated window films [A])

REQ-06-02-006 [H] Sensing-state reports shall state the estimated range and a suspected cause (window or environment), and the AMS shall group them by truck and by place on the HMI. Rationale: dust and contamination look the same to one truck; the pattern across trucks and places separates them (section 4.7). Verification: test (injected reports: one truck at many places, many trucks at one place); inspection of the HMI

REQ-06-02-007 [H] A perception stop report shall include the snapshot frame, the object's position, size and mean intensity, and the persistence of the detection over the following frames; the HMI shall state that a snapshot cannot show objects inside or behind clouds, below the lowest beam or behind obstructions. Rationale: the classification rule of ch-16-07 depends on persistence, which the snapshot does not show (section 4.8). Verification: inspection of the message schema and the HMI; exercise (procedure 8.3 A with scene C3)

REQ-06-02-008 [H] Perception windows and lenses shall be inspected and cleaned according to a plan set by the Maintenance Planner from the sensing-state trend and the nuisance-stop record, and only on isolated trucks; after cleaning, the truck's sensing estimate shall be confirmed before it returns to normal speed. Rationale: dirt and dust multiply (section 4.4); cleaning near a non-isolated truck exposes the Field Technician (ch-16-05). Verification: inspection of cleaning records and isolation records; audit of the plan against the trend

10. Verification and validation

  • Geometry. Returns on target versus range with static targets of the design classes' sizes at known ranges, on level ground, compared with the model of section 4.2.
  • Light budget. Targets of known low reflectivity; window films of known transmission [A] fitted for tests; measurements in natural dust and in spray from WT02 at known times since watering.
  • Processing. Labelled field recordings with spray, dust and rain; scenes with persons and vehicles at the edge of and inside clouds; the clusters formed, filtered and kept, and the missed detections, reported per scene (ch-06-07).
  • Self-check. Drives with calibrated films over the straight stretches used by the check; derate and stop behaviour; false "window" reports in dust.
  • Operations. Exercises of procedures 8.3 A and B; audit of cleaning records.

The effects of dust and spray on LiDAR are not taken from any source here [TBV] (SRC-PUB-PERCEPTION-DUST-SPRAY); LiDAR principles beyond time-of-flight ranging (range equation, multiple returns, window returns) [TBV] (SRC-PUB-LIDAR-PRINCIPLES). Whether standards address sensor cleaning or self-checks for autonomous mining machines is not verified [TBV] (SRC-PUB-PERCEPTION-SENSOR-MAINTENANCE). Treating degraded sensing as a "triggering condition" in the sense of ISO 21448:2022 (SRC-STD-ISO21448, a road-vehicle standard verified at catalogue level) is the handbook's analogy [H] (ch-06-06).

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Counting points without counting light. Geometry gives a dark person 85.9 m; the light budget 70.7 m.
  • Testing the dust filter with people beside the dust, never in it.
  • Filtering on intensity alone. It deletes people in dark clothing.
  • Loosening the filter to cut nuisance stops without re-demonstrating missed-detection performance.
  • No self-check. A truck that cannot notice its own dirty window runs at a speed its sensing no longer supports.
  • Reporting "degraded" without a suspected cause, so that operations cannot decide whom to call.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Reading a snapshot as a photograph. It is one frame of points: it shows shape and place, not what is inside a cloud or below the beams.
  • Classifying a stop as nuisance because the snapshot shows spray, while the detection persists.
  • Wiping a window on a truck that is not isolated.
  • Keeping a truck derated for sensing "until the end of the shift" while its estimate keeps falling.
  • Asking engineering to "make the trucks ignore spray" instead of changing the watering pattern.
  • Not recording cleaning, so that nobody can tell whether the interval is right.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — time of flight. t=2r/c: 50 m → 333.6 ns; 200 m → 1334.3 ns.

Example 2 — points on a person at 50 m. Horizontal spacing 50×0.2°=0.175 m, vertical 0.349 m. A 0.6 m wide person spans 3 columns; the beams between the foot and the head at 50 m give 5 rows: 15 returns (lab).

Example 3 — the dirty window. T = 0.7: the dark person's returns end at 0.7×70.7=49.5 m; the detection range is 0.7×44.2=30.9 m; the supported speed on the level is 34.7 km/h (lab).

Example 4 — why spray is an obstacle. C2: 1985 points in a bounding box of about 5 × 5 × 2.8 m gives 29.1 points per m³ — above the 20 per m³ threshold, so not dust. C1's thin cloud: 298 points, 3.2 per m³ — dust.

Example 5 — the WT02 stop. At 30 km/h (8.33 m/s), latency and decision 4.2 m, class C stop 43.1 m: 47.2 m after the detection at 60 m, so DT24 stands 12.8 m short of the patch; 9.8 s from detection to rest.

15. Key takeaways

  • A LiDAR measures range by time of flight (r=ct/2; 333.6 ns at 50 m) and sees droplets and dust as faithfully as it sees people.
  • Returns on target fall roughly with the square of range: 15 on a person at 50 m, 2 at R05's class C range (88.4 m).
  • The light budget can be shorter than geometry: a dark person's returns end at 70.7 m; a window passing 70 % of the light takes the supported speed on the level from 47.5 to 34.7 km/h; dirt and dust multiply.
  • Dense residual spray is kept as an obstacle — the source of SCN-WATER-WT02's nuisance stops; thin dust is filtered.
  • A dust filter can throw away a person merged with a cloud; its thresholds are safety parameters, not production settings.
  • A truck should estimate its own sensing range, derate for it, stop below a threshold and say whether the window or the air is the suspected cause.
  • A snapshot shows shape, size and place in one frame; it cannot show what is inside or behind a cloud, below the beams or moving. Nuisance needs a cleared detection and the watering record as well.
