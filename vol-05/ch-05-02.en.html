1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

14:20:00, DAY shift. DT17, loaded, is climbing lower R05 beside the highwall when its localisation integrity drops and its ADS derates it to 10 km/h. Forty seconds later DT13, empty and descending, loses integrity at the same place and stops. At 14:21 the AHS Controller restricts R05 to 20 km/h, and at 14:25 calls Survey "to check GB1 and satellite availability" (SCN-GNSS-R05; ch-04-02, ch-04-06, ch-16-07).

The Survey officer who takes the call asks three things before driving anywhere. "What did the trucks' receivers say — fixed, float or standalone, and how many satellites? Were the corrections current at the trucks? And is anything happening on the west ramp or on the pit floor, or only on lower R05?" The controller can read the integrity alarms but not the receiver states behind them. The Pit Supervisor adds a question of their own: "Last Thursday the same thing happened at about the same time. Could we have known this morning?"

These questions are the subject of this chapter:

  • How does a truck turn satellite signals into a position good to a few centimetres, and what does base station GB1 contribute?
  • Which errors dominate on lower R05 — the shape of the visible sky, the reflections off the highwall, the age of the corrections or the distance to GB1?
  • What happens to the solution on a truck when the correction link through the site network is delayed or lost?
  • Why should R05 and the west ramp R24 behave differently, and do they in the model?
  • What should the HMI show about each receiver, who plans base-station work, and what does Survey need in a report?

This chapter covers:

  • pseudorange and carrier-phase positioning and how RTK uses GB1's corrections to resolve integer ambiguities (FIG-05-02-01), as concepts;
  • the error budget — satellite geometry (DOP), multipath, atmospheric residuals, correction latency and baseline length — computed at P1, along R05, at P2, on the pit floor, at EX02-L and on R24 (LAB-05-02-01);
  • the sky mask of the pit and of the highwall, DOP over a shift, and an illustrative satellite-geometry forecast that feeds ch-04-02's operating conditions (FIG-05-02-02);
  • the RTK solution states and their transitions when geometry or the correction link changes, including the network outages of Volume 11 (FIG-05-02-03, SCN-COMMS-R05);
  • the operations side: solution-state indicators on the HMI, GB1 maintenance planning with Survey, and the RTK problem report.

Frames and the local mine grid REFMINE-GRID are ch-05-01's subject; filtering is ch-05-04; integrity, protection levels and the derating decision are ch-05-06. Constellation facts (orbits, signals, frequencies), RTK practice and receiver behaviour are named here as concepts only and tagged [TBV]. Every satellite position, error magnitude, threshold and rate in this chapter is an illustrative assumption [A] computed with ahs_sim 0.14.0; none is a receiver specification, an accuracy requirement or a limit to adopt.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Explain pseudorange and carrier-phase positioning and how RTK uses corrections from base station GB1 to resolve integer ambiguities.
  2. Model the main error sources — satellite geometry (DOP), multipath, atmospheric residuals, correction latency and baseline length — and compute their effect on position error at P1, R05 and the pit floor with [A] parameters.
  3. Analyse how delay or loss of the correction link through the site network changes the RTK solution state on trucks in AA-North.
  4. Compare expected GNSS quality on the two pit ramps — R05 beside the highwall with high multipath risk and the west ramp R24 with medium risk — using their mine.yaml attributes and a simplified sky mask.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Interpret RTK solution-state indicators (fixed, float, standalone) and satellite counts on the HMI, and relate them to truck behaviour.
  2. Plan with Survey how base-station maintenance or outages at GB1 are scheduled and communicated to the control room before they affect production.
  3. Use satellite-geometry forecasts and historical alarm patterns to anticipate degradation periods on R05 and brief the shift at handover.
  4. Report an RTK problem with the data Survey needs: time window, location, trucks affected and solution-state history.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Every autonomous truck at the reference mine positions itself with RTK GNSS. One base station, GB1, stands at the MIA at (−1450, 250, RL 425) in REFMINE-GRID (mine.yaml). Its corrections travel over fibre to the network core in CR1 and from there over the same comms sites that carry the AMS link — CT1 at the MIA, CT2 at the pit crest and CT3, the trailer on the pit floor (network.yaml, ch-11-01). Wherever a truck loses its AMS link, it loses its corrections too; if GB1 or its fibre fails, every truck loses its corrections at once while everything else keeps working (ch-11-01).

The pit adds geometry. North Pit is 120 m deep: the crest P1 is at RL 420, the ramp toe P2 at RL 300 (mine.yaml). The main ramp R05 runs 1339.6 m from P1 to P2 at 8.99 % beside the highwall and carries the highest GNSS multipath risk class on the site; the west ramp R24, 1425.0 m from Intersection W at RL 330 to the crest P3, carries medium risk, as do the EX01 and EX02 approaches and loading links (mine.yaml). Both ramps are pit exits; R05 is the shortest from every shovel, R24 the detour (ch-01-03, ch-10-03).

The distances to GB1 matter less than one might expect and more than nothing. P1 is 2.37 km from GB1, P2 3.64 km and the EX02 loading area 4.07 km (LAB-05-02-01).

What makes the problem operational is that the GNSS conditions change during a shift. The registry describes SCN-GNSS-R05 with "afternoon satellite geometry poor" [A]; ch-04-02 treats satellite geometry as an operating condition with three levels — good, poor, very poor — "that the site can forecast (Volume 05)", and makes R05's place in the ODD depend on it: under poor geometry R05 is inside only derated to 20 km/h, under very poor geometry outside. ch-16-07 prices the options — restricting R05 costs 568.3 t/h in the fleet model, closing it and using R24 311.7 t/h. None of those chapters says what the geometry is, why it is poor in the afternoon, or how the control room would know in the morning. This chapter does, with a deliberately simple model.

3. Terminology and foundations

GNSS and the receiver's two measurements. A GNSS receiver (glossary gnss) measures, for each satellite it tracks, two quantities. The pseudorange is the range to the satellite computed from the signal's travel time, offset by the receiver's clock error — hence "pseudo". The carrier phase is the phase of the received carrier wave, tracked continuously; it changes by one cycle for every wavelength the range changes, but the whole number of cycles between satellite and receiver at the start of tracking is unknown. That unknown whole number is the integer ambiguity. Carrier wavelengths are of the order of decimetres and pseudorange noise of the order of decimetres to metres for the signals in common use [F] (SRC-PUB-ESA-GNSS-DATA-PROCESSING-VOL1); the handbook asserts no figure for any constellation or signal.

RTK. Real-time kinematic positioning (glossary real-time-kinematic) uses the observations of a base station at a known position (glossary base-station) to cancel errors the base and the moving receiver — the rover — have in common, and to resolve the integer ambiguities of the carrier phase. The data the base sends are the corrections; the path they travel is the correction link; the time since the newest correction the rover holds was observed is the correction age. The distance between base and rover is the baseline length.

Solution states. A rover reports how far it got. RTK fixed: corrections current and the ambiguities resolved to integers — centimetre level. RTK float: corrections current but the ambiguities estimated as real numbers, not yet fixed — decimetre level. Standalone: no usable corrections; pseudorange positioning only — metre level. None: fewer than four satellites. The handbook calls these four values the RTK solution state [H]; real receivers use other names and more states [TBV] (SRC-PUB-RTK-AMBIGUITY-PRACTICE).

Satellite geometry and DOP. Satellite geometry (glossary satellite-geometry) is how the visible satellites are spread over the sky. Dilution of precision (DOP) turns it into a number: the factor by which ranging error becomes position error. HDOP refers to the horizontal position, PDOP to three-dimensional position (section 4.3).

Sky mask. The part of the sky a receiver cannot see: below its cut-off elevation, behind the pit shell, behind a highwall. In the lab a mask is an elevation angle per azimuth.

Multipath. Reception of signals reflected by the highwall, a bench face or a truck body (glossary multipath). The reflected path is longer than the direct one, so a range — and, in an RTK solution, sometimes the whole position — is biased. mine.yaml grades each segment's expected multipath as low, medium or high (glossary multipath-risk-class).

UERE. The user-equivalent range error: the combined ranging error per satellite, which DOP converts into position error. The lab assumes one UERE per solution state [A].

Satellite-geometry forecast. A prediction, for the shift ahead, of satellite count and DOP at the places that matter, translated into ch-04-02's condition levels (section 4.4) [H].

4. Technical explanation

4.1 From ranges to a position

FIG-05-02-01 shows the pieces at the reference mine.

A receiver that measures pseudoranges Pk to k=1…n satellites solves for four unknowns: its three position coordinates and its clock offset. The textbook form of the measurement [TBV] is

Pk=ρk+c(δtr−δtk)+Ik+Tk+εPk

with ρk the geometric range, c the speed of light, δtr and δtk the receiver and satellite clock errors, Ik and Tk the ionospheric and tropospheric delays and εPk noise and multipath. Four satellites are the minimum; more give redundancy, and the least-squares solution weighs the geometry (section 4.3). This standalone solution inherits every error in the equation, which is why it is good to metres, not centimetres.

The carrier-phase measurement, in range units, has the same structure with two differences [TBV]:

Φk=ρk+c(δtr−δtk)−Ik+Tk+λNk+εΦk

The noise εΦ is a small fraction of a wavelength λ, far smaller than the pseudorange noise, but the range now contains λNk, where the integer Nk is unknown. A carrier-phase position is excellent once Nk is known and useless until then.

4.2 What the base station adds

GB1 sits on a surveyed point and tracks the same satellites. Differencing the rover's and GB1's measurements of the same satellite removes the satellite clock; differencing again between two satellites removes the receiver clocks. What is left, for a short baseline, is mostly the difference in geometric range — which carries the rover's position relative to GB1 — plus the double-differenced ambiguity, plus whatever does not cancel: atmospheric delay that differs between GB1 and the rover (it grows with the baseline), multipath at either antenna (it does not cancel, because it is local), and noise [TBV] (SRC-PUB-RTK-AMBIGUITY-PRACTICE).

The rover first estimates the double-differenced ambiguities as real numbers — the float solution. When the estimates are close enough to integers, and the integer set is clearly better than its nearest alternative, it fixes them — the fixed solution. How a receiver decides, how long it takes and how often it fixes wrongly are receiver design matters the handbook does not describe [TBV]. Three consequences for the AHS do not depend on them:

  • The rover needs the corrections continuously. Old corrections are differenced against new rover data; the error grows with their age, and beyond some age the receiver stops using them (section 4.6).
  • The result is relative to GB1's coordinate. The rover's grid position is GB1's surveyed coordinate plus the measured baseline. A wrong GB1 coordinate moves every truck by the same vector and is reported by no receiver — the reason ch-12-01 specifies the GB1 position check. How the grid, the antenna and the truck's reference point relate is ch-05-01's frame chain.
  • Multipath is the rover's own problem. Differencing removes what GB1 and the rover share. Reflections off the R05 highwall are not shared.

4.3 Geometry and DOP

With n visible satellites at azimuth ak and elevation ek, the linearised geometry matrix has one row per satellite, [−coseksinak,−cosekcosak,−sinek,1] — east, north, up and clock. With Q=(G𝖳G)−1:

HDOP=QEE+QNN,PDOP=QEE+QNN+QUU

and the per-axis horizontal error is about UERE·HDOP/2. Satellites spread over the whole sky give small DOP; satellites bunched in one part of the sky, or few satellites, give large DOP. A wall that hides one side of the sky does both.

LAB-05-02-01 uses ahs_sim.localisation: a synthetic, seeded sky of 32 satellites moving on tracks that repeat every half sidereal day — not an almanac of any real constellation [A] — a receiver cut-off of 10° [A], the terrain mask of the assumed pit shell, and a simplified benched highwall beside every ramp with medium or high multipath risk: a wall of 45° overall slope rising from 25 m beside the road to the crest RL of the segment, over a 120° sector on the wall side [A]. Low-risk segments get no wall.

FIG-05-02-02 (a) shows the sky at P2 at 14:20.

Thirteen synthetic satellites are above the horizon. The pit shell itself hides almost nothing — the assumed shell smooths benches and walls, and from P2 it rises at most about 9° — so the 10° cut-off removes the two lowest. The highwall removes one more: S01, at azimuth 168.7° and elevation 29.2°, behind the wall's 120° sector. Ten satellites remain, with HDOP 1.30 and PDOP 2.03. At the crest P1, where the wall has no height, eleven are visible, HDOP 0.96, PDOP 1.59.

Going down R05 from P1, the wall grows with depth; at 14:20 it first hides a satellite 355 m below the crest, at RL 388 (LAB-05-02-01; FIG-05-02-02 (c)). That is where, in this model, "lower R05" begins at that moment. On R24 the same satellite disappears 503 m below P3.

4.4 Geometry over a shift, and a forecast

The satellites move, so the masked sky changes through the shift. FIG-05-02-02 (b) shows PDOP at P2, wall included, every five minutes of the DAY shift. With 32 synthetic satellites the solution never loses its fix; PDOP stays between about 1.3 and 4. It has afternoon peaks: 3.68 at 13:10, 3.35 at 14:05 and the worst of the shift, 3.95, at 15:50, when nine satellites are visible. These are the model's version of the registry's "afternoon geometry poor" — produced by the synthetic tracks, not by any real constellation, and therefore an illustration of the mechanism, not a prediction.

To connect this to ch-04-02, the lab maps DOP and satellite count at P2 to the three condition levels with a teaching rule [A]:

Level (ch-04-02) Rule at P2, wall included [A] Share of the DAY shift, 32 satellites Share, 20 satellites
good PDOP ≤ 2.0 and ≥ 10 satellites visible 70.8 % 6.2 %
poor PDOP ≤ 4.0 and ≥ 6 satellites 29.2 % 60.4 %
very poor otherwise (the fixed state of the ahs_sim rule at risk) 0 % 33.3 %

With 32 satellites the forecast for the day has four poor windows: 11:20–11:50, 12:25–12:40, 13:10–14:30 and 15:30–16:55. SCN-GNSS-R05's T+0 at 14:20 falls inside the third, with PDOP 2.03 — poor by a margin of 0.03, which is a reminder that a threshold turns a continuous quantity into a verdict. At 14:30 the level returns to good.

The thresholds are chosen to make the levels meaningful in this model, not derived from any requirement. A real site would derive its forecast from the actual constellation data, a measured sky mask at the critical points, and its own history of integrity events [R]; how such forecasts are produced is a topic here [TBV]. What carries over is the structure: a forecast is computed at the worst point of each critical road, not at the MIA; it is published with its source and time, so that ch-04-02's rule "conditions carry a source and an age" can apply; and it is compared with the integrity events that actually happened, shift after shift.

The sparse-sky case. Thirty-two well-spread synthetic satellites make the geometry forgiving. To show what happens when fewer satellites are usable — fewer signals tracked, a satellite set unhealthy, a receiver configured for fewer systems [A] — the lab repeats everything with the first 20 of the same 32. At P2 at 14:20 seven are above the horizon and five are visible; PDOP is 9.63 and the state is FLOAT. Over the shift the fixed solution holds 80.6 % of the time at P2, and the level is very poor for 33.3 % of the shift, including 14:00–14:40.

4.5 The error budget

The lab's error model follows section 4.3 with assumptions [A] for every term:

σ=(UEREsHDOP2fmp)2+(0.002τ)2+(10−6b)2

per horizontal axis, with UERE 0.01 m for FIXED, 0.15 m for FLOAT and 1.5 m for STANDALONE, a multipath factor fmp of 1, 2 and 4 for low, medium and high risk, an age term of 0.002 m per second of correction age τ (FIXED only) and a baseline term of 1 ppm of the distance b to GB1. In addition, each fix is an outlier of 1–5 m in a random direction with probability 0, 0.01 or 0.05 by risk class. The baseline term is the model's only stand-in for atmospheric residuals and other distance-dependent errors; the model has no ionosphere or troposphere of its own.

Moving GB1 closer to the pit would buy almost nothing in this model; a better antenna site on the truck or a ramp design with less reflecting wall would act on the dominant term [A].

  2. The σ is not the story; the outliers are. A 0.037 m σ is harmless against the 2.2 m lane margin of a straight (ch-07-06). A 1–5 m outlier on one fix in twenty is not. LAB-05-02-01 drives the loaded climb of R05 at 11.44 km/h (ch-07-01) — 422 fixes at one per second — twenty times:

  3. With fewer satellites, geometry takes over. In the sparse-sky case the lower ramp drops to FLOAT with HDOP 4.40: σ = 0.15 × 4.40 / √2 × 4 = 1.868 m per axis at P2, and 0.934 m at the midpoint of R24.

4.6 Correction age and the solution state

The ahs_sim state rule [A] (FIG-05-02-03) is: FIXED needs at least six visible satellites, PDOP of at most 6.0 and corrections no older than 5 s; FLOAT needs at least five satellites and corrections no older than 60 s; STANDALONE needs four satellites; otherwise NONE. The thresholds are teaching values; real receivers set their own, often configurably [TBV].

At HDOP 1.30 on a high-risk segment the three states mean σ of 0.037 m (FIXED, 1 s old corrections), 0.552 m (FLOAT) and 5.515 m (STANDALONE) per axis. On lower R05, one standard deviation of a FLOAT position uses a quarter of the lane margin; a STANDALONE truck does not know which lane it is in. Note also what FIG-05-02-03 does not show: a time to fix. When corrections return, the model returns to FIXED at once. Real receivers must re-resolve the ambiguities, which takes time that depends on the receiver and the conditions [TBV]. A time to fix is available since ahs_sim 0.14.1 (RtkTracker(time_to_fix_s) [A]); this chapter's results use the memoryless rule.

R05 is marginal throughout, R24 marginal, R04 marginal and only R22 poor (mine.yaml). On a marginal link a truck loses its fixed solution for a few seconds about once every one and a half hours of driving in this model, never long enough to fall to STANDALONE. The burstiness matters more than the mean: at 7.27 % independent losses the chance of ten in a row would be negligible.

The R05 handover. A truck descending R05 changes from CT2 to CT3 1000 m below P1, and for 1.5 s no AMS message passes (ch-11-03, network.yaml). The corrections share the gap. With the last message up to one slot old, the age reaches 2.0 s at most: below 5 s, so the handover does not cost the fix.

4.8 R05 against R24

The two ramps differ in three mine.yaml attributes that matter here: multipath risk (high against medium), length (1339.6 m against 1425.0 m) and depth (both end at the crest at RL 420; R05 starts 30 m lower). The simplified wall model gives both the same wall — it applies to every medium- or high-risk ramp — and in the synthetic sky the wall faces the same part of the sky on both. A different wall height per segment is available since ahs_sim 0.14.1 (highwall_mask(…, wall_top_rl=…)); the results here use the shared default. The comparison therefore separates geometry from multipath cleanly:

  • Geometry is the same. At 14:20 both ramps see 10 satellites on their lower parts and 11 near the crest; the wall hides S01 355 m below P1 on R05 and 503 m below P3 on R24. In the sparse-sky case both drop to FLOAT on their lower parts.
  • Multipath is not. σ 0.037 m against 0.019 m in the fixed state; outliers 20.55 against 4.75 per climb; 95th percentile 0.127 m against 0.045 m.

So in this model the reason R24 is the better ramp under poor geometry — ch-04-02's ODD keeps it inside without a derate — is its multipath class, not its sky. A real site would measure both: the mask at the toe of each ramp, and the integrity events per pass [R]. If both ramps alarm together, the cause is probably geometry (or GB1); if only R05 alarms, it is probably multipath on R05. That reading is one of the most useful things an operations reader can take from this chapter, and it needs R24 traffic — or at least R24 history — to work.

4.9 Model limits

  • Synthetic constellation; no almanac, signals or frequencies; the half-sidereal-day period is a teaching device.
  • One wall model for every medium / high-risk ramp (per-segment heights possible since 0.14.1, not used here); the pit shell smooths benches.
  • Multipath as white noise plus random outliers; real multipath is correlated along the road and with the satellites' motion [TBV] (SRC-PUB-GNSS-MULTIPATH-OPEN-PIT).
  • No ionosphere or troposphere model; the 1 ppm baseline term stands in for all distance-dependent errors.
  • No time to fix in the results (optional since 0.14.1); no wrong fixes; no receiver-specific behaviour.
  • Corrections at one message per 0.5 s slot; the true rate and format are not described.
  • 2D only; heights are ch-05-01's subject.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

At shift start the conditions panel shows GB1 up, correction age normal on every reporting truck, and the geometry forecast for the shift (ch-04-02 operating conditions, ch-16-07 conditions panel). Trucks report FIXED nearly everywhere; brief losses of the fixed state on marginal links — 0.68 per hour of driving in the model — are not alarms by themselves. The incoming controller briefs the forecast's poor windows at handover: in the model 11:20–11:50, 12:25–12:40, 13:10–14:30 and 15:30–16:55 at P2.

Seen from the receivers, the scenario has two lessons that complete the other chapters' timelines. First, in the baseline model the fixed solution held throughout: the integrity drops at T+0 s and T+40 s come from poor geometry and the multipath of R05's high class — outliers that a filter must catch — not from a lost fix. The Survey answer in the timelines of ch-04-02 and ch-16-07, "GB1 and corrections working; geometry", is therefore correct but incomplete: it should say "geometry and multipath on lower R05", as in the "later" row above. Second, the T+150 s at which ch-04-06 has DT17 past the degraded section is an assumption of that chapter; the GNSS model does not produce a section boundary that sharp. Climbing at 10 km/h, DT17 covers about 417 m in 150 s; in this model the wall's effect on geometry begins 355 m below P1, but the high multipath class applies to the whole of R05.

6.3 Degraded operation — SCN-COMMS-R05: corrections lost with the link

CT3 fails at 14:05:00 (ch-11-01). The registry gives T+0 (CT3 down), T+3 s (trucks detect link loss and stop), T+10 s (AMS marks them communication-lost) and T+900 s (CT3 restored, reconciliation); ch-11-07 completes reconciliation at T+932 s. On the GNSS side (section 4.7) a truck on lower R05 is FIXED at T+3 s, FLOAT from T+5 s, STANDALONE from T+60 s, and FIXED again when corrections return at T+900 s in the model. The trucks stop on link loss regardless of their receivers; what the receivers change is the quality of the positions reported during and immediately after the outage. Trucks on upper R05, on the pit floor and on R24 keep their corrections through CT2.

6.4 Planned GB1 maintenance

A GB1 outage removes corrections from every truck at once (section 4.7). In the model, every truck is FLOAT within 5 s and STANDALONE within 60 s; at P1 STANDALONE means σ 4.07 m per axis, on the pit floor at Z 1.02 m. No truck can follow a lane on that. Planned GB1 work — antenna or receiver replacement, a firmware change, power work at the MIA, the antenna move of ch-12-01 — therefore needs a window in which no autonomous truck depends on GB1 [H]:

  • Who: Survey owns the plan (GB1's coordinate and the post-work check); the Field Technician does the hardware work; the Maintenance Planner books it; the Pit Supervisor approves the window; the AHS Controller executes the fleet side.
  • When: in a window when the fleet is parked anyway or the production loss is accepted [A], agreed at least one shift ahead [A], and with good forecast geometry for the post-work check, because the GB1 position check of ch-12-01 re-observes check points with a rover on GB1's corrections.
  • How: trucks parked before GB1 goes down (not stopped where the loss finds them); after the work, GB1 status and the position check recorded before any truck uses GB1 again (REQ-12-01-005); the control room informed at start, at completion and at release.

The registered scenario SCN-GB1-MAINTENANCE fixes the times [A]: trucks parked at T−600 s, GB1 down at T+0, work complete at T+3600 s, status and position check recorded at T+4200 s, release approved by the Pit Supervisor at T+4500 s. The fleet is therefore parked for 5100 s for one hour of work.

An unplanned GB1 failure is the site-wide pattern of ch-11-01 and is coded DLY-SE-02.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along SCN-GNSS-R05

  • 06:00. The forecast reaches the AMS as an operating condition with its source (Survey's forecast) and its time; stale forecasts become unknown, and unknown is outside (ch-04-02).
  • T+0 s. DT17's position reports carry FIXED, 10 satellites, PDOP 2.03, age 1 s, and the ADS's integrity indicator. The derate is the ADS's decision; the receiver state alone would not have called for it.
  • T+40 s. DT13 reports integrity lost. Because both trucks report their receiver state, the AMS can show that both were FIXED with ten satellites at the same chainage — a location pattern, not a truck fault.
  • T+60 s. The restriction is an AMS object (ch-04-02); no message goes to the receivers.
  • T+300 s. Survey reads the solution-state history and correction age per truck from the AMS log rather than asking the controller to remember them (REQ-05-02-007).
  • T+900 s in SCN-COMMS-R05. Corrections return with the link; the first reports may be pre-fix; the AMS knows from the state field.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

Per truck, beside the integrity indicator of ch-05-06: the RTK solution state (FIXED, FLOAT, STANDALONE, NONE), the number of satellites used, PDOP and the correction age [H]. Per site: GB1 status, the correction age seen by trucks per comms site, and the geometry forecast with its poor windows. What the controller must not read into them:

  • FIXED does not mean "trusted". In SCN-GNSS-R05 both trucks were FIXED when their integrity dropped. The ADS decides on integrity (ch-05-04, ch-05-06).
  • FLOAT is not a fault by itself. A truck on a marginal link loses FIXED for a few seconds now and then. A FLOAT that persists at one place on several trucks is a pattern; on one truck everywhere it is that truck.
  • Satellite count is relative. Ten satellites at P2 at 14:20 is normal for that place and time in the model; eleven at P1. A drop from the usual count for a place is what matters.
  • Correction age rising everywhere points to GB1 or the core; rising in one area points to the comms site.

8.3 Procedure excerpts

A. Responding to a GNSS pattern on R05 (SCN-GNSS-R05, from the receiver side).

  1. AHS Controller (T+40 s): compare the two trucks' solution states, satellites and correction age on the HMI. Both FIXED with normal age and the usual count for the place → location pattern; continue with ch-04-02 and ch-04-06.
  2. AHS Controller (T+60 s): restrict per ch-04-02 with reason "GNSS — geometry poor (forecast), R05 high multipath".
  3. AHS Controller (T+300 s): call Survey with the pattern and the time window; check whether any truck is on R24 or the pit floor and what it reports.
  4. Survey: check GB1 status and correction age; read the solution-state history of the affected trucks; compare with the forecast; report "GB1 / corrections / geometry / multipath" separately.
  5. Pit Supervisor: keep the restriction with its review condition; use the forecast's next window (15:30) in the review.

B. Planned GB1 work.

  1. Survey (at least one shift ahead [A]): propose the window, the work, the expected duration and the post-work position check; choose a window with good forecast geometry.
  2. Pit Supervisor: approve the window; Maintenance Planner books the Field Technician.
  3. AHS Controller (before the window): park the trucks at their park-up or hold points; confirm none is moving autonomously.
  4. Field Technician: do the work; Survey: run the GB1 position check of ch-12-01 and record it.
  5. Pit Supervisor: approve the release only after Survey has recorded the check; AHS Controller: return the trucks to autonomous operation, inform the control room and record the times (SCN-GB1-MAINTENANCE).

8.5 Training and competency implications

AHS Controllers learn the four solution states, why FIXED is not "trusted", and the four alarm patterns. Survey learns to produce and publish the geometry forecast and to analyse solution-state histories. Pit Supervisors learn what a GB1 window costs and why trucks are parked first. Field Technicians learn that GB1 work is site-wide work.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-05-02-001 [H] The ADS shall report with every position report its RTK solution state, the number of satellites used, PDOP and the correction age. Rationale: the same integrity drop has different causes with different states (sections 4.6, 7.3); reconciliation needs the quality of a position (section 4.7). Verification: inspection of the message definition; test (log replay of SCN-GNSS-R05)

REQ-05-02-002 [H] The ADS shall use the RTK solution state and correction age as inputs to localisation integrity and shall not treat a fixed solution as sufficient evidence of integrity. Rationale: in SCN-GNSS-R05 both trucks were FIXED when integrity dropped; multipath outliers occur in the fixed state (section 4.5). Verification: analysis (LAB-05-02-01 passes), test with injected outliers (ch-05-06)

REQ-05-02-003 [H] The ADS shall monitor correction age and shall stop using corrections older than a documented site limit; the limits shall be recorded with the ADS configuration. Rationale: old corrections degrade the solution (section 4.6); the model's 5 s and 60 s are assumptions. Verification: inspection of configuration; test (corrections withheld)

REQ-05-02-004 [H] The AMS shall show GB1 status and, per truck, the solution state, satellites used and correction age, and shall group RTK alarms by place and by correction path (comms site, GB1). Rationale: the four alarm patterns (section 8.2) are visible only when states and paths are shown together. Verification: inspection of the HMI; demonstration with the CT3 and GB1 outage patterns (section 4.7)

REQ-05-02-005 [H] Planned work on GB1 shall be scheduled by Survey with the Pit Supervisor's approval at least one shift ahead [A]; autonomous trucks shall be parked before GB1 stops, and shall not use GB1's corrections again until Survey has recorded a passed GB1 position check. Rationale: a GB1 outage removes corrections from every truck within seconds (section 6.4); REQ-12-01-005. Verification: inspection of the work order and the control-room log; drill

REQ-05-02-006 [H] Survey shall publish, at the start of each shift, a satellite-geometry forecast at the toe of R05 and R24 and at loading areas under walls, stating its source, its time and the windows of each condition level. Rationale: ch-04-02's ODD depends on the geometry condition; a forecast without source and time becomes unknown (section 4.4). Verification: inspection; comparison with recorded integrity events over a month [A]

REQ-05-02-007 [H] An RTK problem reported to Survey shall contain the time window on the AMS clock, the location, the trucks affected and not affected nearby, the solution-state history and correction age, GB1 and comms status, the forecast at the time, the truck behaviour and recent changes. Rationale: Survey cannot separate geometry, multipath, corrections and GB1 without them (section 8.3 C). Verification: inspection of reports; drill

REQ-05-02-008 [H] During and after a loss of corrections, position reports used by the AMS for reconciliation shall carry their solution state, and the AMS shall not treat a standalone or float position as equivalent to a fixed one when comparing it with a frozen reservation. Rationale: a truck on lower R05 is STANDALONE from T+60 s of a CT3 outage; its first reports after the link returns may precede a re-fix (section 4.7). Verification: test (reconciliation with injected float and standalone reports, ch-11-07)

10. Verification and validation

  • The lab verifies its arithmetic. Eight tests in LAB-05-02-01 check the sky at P2 and the wall onset, the shift forecast, geometry along both ramps, the error budget, the seeded passes, the network facts reused from Volume 11 (burst model, handover, outage table), the CT3 outage timeline and the GB1 outage.
  • The lab does not validate GNSS. The constellation is synthetic, the wall is one shape, multipath is white noise with outliers, and there is no time to fix. Its job is to show which term dominates and how the state follows the link.
  • Site validation [R]. Measure the sky mask at the toe of R05, R24 and under the EX02 face; log solution state, satellites, DOP and correction age per truck along R05 for several weeks; compare forecast windows with recorded integrity events; check FIXED positions of parked trucks at check points (ch-12-01).
  • Procedural validation. Drills: a planned GB1 window (trucks parked, check, release); a simulated GB1 failure in a test window (pattern "everywhere" recognised); an RTK report written from a replayed SCN-GNSS-R05 log.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Treating FIXED as integrity, and driving the mode from the receiver state.
  • Putting the geometry forecast at the base station instead of at the toe of the ramp.
  • Sending positions without their solution state, so that nobody can tell a float position from a fixed one later.
  • Assuming a second base station will cure multipath.
  • Assuming corrections and the AMS link fail independently: they share CT1–CT3.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Accepting "GB1 is transmitting" as the answer to "check GB1" (ch-12-01).
  • Calling Survey with "R05 alarms again" and no time window, trucks or states.
  • Working on GB1 with trucks running "because it will only take a minute".
  • Reading a FLOAT blip on one truck on a marginal link as a site problem.
  • Lifting a restriction because the forecast turned good, without integrity recovered over several passes (ch-05-06).

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — the budget at P2 by hand. HDOP 1.30, FIXED, high risk: geometry 0.01 × 1.30 / √2 = 0.0092 m; with multipath × 4 = 0.0368 m; age 0.002 × 1 = 0.0020 m; baseline 1 × 10⁻⁶ × 3640 = 0.0036 m. Total √(0.0368² + 0.0020² + 0.0036²) = 0.0371 m. Remove the baseline entirely and σ is 0.0369 m; halve the multipath factor and it is 0.0189 m.

Example 2 — the sparse sky. Five satellites, PDOP 9.63, HDOP 4.40 at P2: FIXED needs six, so FLOAT. σ = 0.15 × 4.40 / √2 × 4 = 1.867 m per axis (the lab gives 1.868 m with the baseline term). The 95th percentile of the horizontal error over a climb, 4.85 m in the lab, is more than twice the 2.2 m lane margin.

Example 3 — the handover. Descending R05 at 40 km/h, the CT2–CT3 gap of 1.5 s starts 1000 m below P1. Last correction up to 0.5 s old at the start: age at most 2.0 s < 5 s. FIXED kept. Without a handover, the age passes 5 s only when eleven consecutive messages are lost (5.5 s); on the marginal link the lab sees that 0.68 times per hour of driving.

Example 4 — a GB1 outage at the crest. P1, HDOP 0.96, high risk, STANDALONE: 1.5 × 0.96 / √2 × 4 = 4.07 m per axis. On the pit floor at Z, low risk: 1.02 m. Neither is usable for lane following; a GB1 window needs parked trucks.

15. Key takeaways

  • RTK gives centimetres only with current corrections from GB1 and resolved ambiguities; its result is relative to GB1's surveyed coordinate.
  • On lower R05 the multipath class, not the geometry or the baseline, dominates the error: σ 0.037 m and one fix in twenty an outlier of 1–5 m in the model.
  • The highwall hides part of the sky; in the model the afternoon geometry at P2 is poor 13:10–14:30 and 15:30–16:55, and 14:20 falls inside. A forecast at the toe of the ramp makes that predictable.
  • With fewer satellites the same wall turns lower R05 and lower R24 into FLOAT zones.
  • Corrections share the AMS link: a comms-site outage removes both in its area; a GB1 outage removes only corrections, everywhere. Marginal links cost the fix for seconds about once every one and a half hours of driving.
  • FIXED is not integrity; the ADS decides on integrity, the AMS shows the states, Survey diagnoses, and GB1 work is site-wide work.
