1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Monday, DAY shift, a little after the hour. EX02 has moved to its new face and the AMS has just activated the new loading area. DT17 reverses in for the first load and stops. The Shovel Operator radios, puzzled rather than alarmed: "Your truck is short — half a metre short and off to my left. The bucket reaches, but that's not where the spot should be." The AHS Controller looks at the HMI. DT17 reports a healthy RTK fix, no localisation alarm, and a final position exactly on the loading spot of the new map version.

Both people are right. The truck is exactly where the map says; the map is not exactly where the face is. Somewhere between the surveyor's rover, the mine grid, the map and the truck's own antenna, one link in a chain of coordinate frames was converted the wrong way — and nothing on the truck can see it.

The scene raises the questions of this chapter:

  • Which coordinate frames does an autonomous truck use between its antenna and the AHS map, and who owns each link?
  • What is the relation between a geodetic datum, a map projection and the local mine grid REFMINE-GRID — and why is the reference mine's grid deliberately not tied to the Earth?
  • How is a Helmert transformation between a projected grid and the mine grid fitted, and what do its residuals tell?
  • What happens when a conversion leaves out the scale, or when a height is in the wrong vertical datum?
  • How do reduced-level errors reach the grades of R05 and R24, and the speed and retarding calculations built on them?
  • What does the control room do when a survey pickup and the AHS map disagree at a loading spot?

This chapter covers:

  • the frame chain of an autonomous truck — sensor frames, body frame, local mine grid, AHS map — and the owner of each transformation (FIG-05-01-01);
  • datums, projections and the local grid, as concepts, and what the reference mine leaves out by design (FIG-05-01-02);
  • the Helmert link between a teaching projected grid and REFMINE-GRID, fitted with ahs_sim.localisation, its residuals, and two mistaken scale-free conversions (LAB-05-01-01, FIG-05-01-03);
  • heights and grades: how RL and vertical-datum errors propagate into the grades of R05 and R24 and into the dynamics of ch-07-01 and ch-07-02;
  • the SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE walkthrough: a loading spot issued in the wrong conversion, and how operations finds and fixes it.

Survey control, the site transformation of survey instruments, error propagation from control marks and the GB1 check are the subject of ch-12-01; this chapter builds on them and does not repeat them. GNSS and RTK themselves are ch-05-02. Every scale, rotation, offset, lever arm and error value in this chapter is an illustrative assumption [A]; geodetic facts are topics only [TBV].

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Explain the relationship between geodetic datums, map projections and the local mine grid REFMINE-GRID, including why the reference mine's grid is deliberately not tied to a geodetic coordinate reference system.
  2. Calculate a 2D similarity (Helmert) transformation between a projected grid and the local mine grid from [A] control points, and quantify the residuals.
  3. Specify the coordinate frames in the AHS chain (vehicle body frame, sensor frames, local mine grid, AHS map) and which organisation or role owns each transformation.
  4. Analyse how reduced-level and vertical-datum errors propagate into the grades computed for R05 and the west ramp R24, and into the speed and retarding calculations that use them.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Recognise symptoms of a datum or grid mismatch (map offset, trucks stopping short of EX02-L, survey pickups not matching the AHS map) and escalate to Survey.
  2. Explain why grid and reduced-level conventions must be identical across survey, mine planning, FMS and the AHS map, and who signs off a change.
  3. Verify with Survey that new nodes and roads, such as the west ramp crest P3 and R24, are issued in the mine grid and appear consistently in the FMS, the AHS map and planning tools before they are used.
  4. Follow the control-room steps when the AHS map and a survey pickup disagree at a loading spot such as EX02-L, including holding trucks from the affected spot until Survey confirms.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Every position in the AHS is a number in some frame: the lanes the AMS grants, the position the ADS estimates, the node coordinates from which the FMS derives lengths and grades. The system works only if all these numbers share origin, axes, scale and height reference. When they do not, nothing breaks loudly: the truck follows the map perfectly and is still in the wrong place.

The reference mine makes the frame simple on purpose. All positions are easting, northing and reduced level in REFMINE-GRID, a local engineering grid in metres with its origin at the crusher C01 (0, 0, RL 424) (mine.yaml). Its note says it is "not a geodetic CRS", and that road-network.geojson is therefore not RFC 7946 compliant: the fictional mine must not be mistakable for a real place (ch-01-03). RL is the height above a fictional local datum; the surface is at RL 420 and the pit floor at RL 300. GB1, the RTK base station, stands at the MIA at (−1450, 250, RL 425).

Three pieces of work in the coming weeks touch the frames:

  • SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE. EX02 moves to a new face; the AMS activates the new loading area at T+3600 s (registry). The loading spot is only as right as the coordinates Survey issues for it — in the frame the trucks use.
  • SCN-MAP-R05. R05 is reworked and re-surveyed; its crest P1 and toe P2 carry RLs from which the AHS map, the FMS and the ADS derive the 9 % grade that sets the loaded climb speed and the descent braking (ch-07-01, ch-07-02).
  • A contractor's survey. A drill-and-blast contractor and a regional mapping job deliver data in a projected grid of their own, not in REFMINE-GRID. Someone has to convert it, and the conversion has a scale.

What makes them hard is that the error they create is systematic: the same offset on every truck, every pass, with every receiver reporting a healthy fix.

3. Terminology and foundations

Geodetic datum (glossary geodetic-datum). A reference frame for positions on the Earth, usually defined by a reference ellipsoid and realised by marks whose coordinates are published for a stated epoch [TBV] (SRC-PUB-GEODETIC-DATUM-EPOCH). It gives latitude, longitude and ellipsoidal height.

Map projection. A mathematical mapping from latitude and longitude on the ellipsoid to plane easting and northing [TBV]. A projected grid is convenient — plane, in metres — but its scale is not exactly one and changes from place to place, and its grid north differs from true north by an angle that also changes [TBV].

Grid scale factor. The ratio of a distance measured in a projected grid to the same distance on the ground. On a real site it combines the projection's scale at that place with a reduction for height [TBV]; in LAB-05-01-01 the combined scale factor is 0.999760 [A]: a grid distance is 240 ppm shorter than the ground distance.

Local mine grid (glossary local-mine-grid). A plane grid chosen by the site, with an origin and orientation that suit the mine and a scale of one at the site. REFMINE-GRID is one. Real sites relate their local grid to a national datum and projection by a documented transformation [TBV]; the reference mine does not, by design.

Similarity (Helmert) transformation (glossary similarity-transformation). The 2D conversion with one scale, one rotation and two shifts. Between a projected grid and a local grid it is the link this chapter fits. A coordinate transformation in general is any rule that converts coordinates from one frame to another.

Reduced level and vertical datum. RL (glossary reduced-level) is the height above the site's vertical datum, a reference surface for heights. GNSS gives heights above the ellipsoid; turning them into RL needs a height model or a fitted offset [TBV] (ch-12-01 fits an offset on control). Two heights are comparable only if they refer to the same vertical datum.

Body frame and vehicle reference point. The body frame is fixed to the truck: x forward, y left, z up, with its origin at a vehicle reference point — in this handbook the centre of the rear axle on the ground [H], the point whose position the ADS reports and that the path-tracking controller steers (ch-07-06).

Sensor frame and lever arm. Each sensor — GNSS antenna, IMU, LiDAR, radar — measures in a frame of its own. The lever arm is the position of a sensor's reference point (for the GNSS antenna, its phase centre) in the body frame. The rotation between a sensor frame and the body frame is the sensor's mounting alignment; together they are its extrinsic calibration (glossary calibration).

Pose and attitude. The pose is the position and heading of the body frame in the grid; attitude adds pitch (nose up or down) and roll (side to side). On R05 a truck is pitched by the grade; on a cambered road it is rolled by the crossfall.

Residual and check point (glossary check-point). As in ch-12-01: a residual is the difference between a published coordinate and its fitted position; a check point is a point of known coordinates not used in the fit and observed to test it.

The frame chain [H]. The ordered set of transformations that carries a measurement from a sensor to a position on the AHS map (section 4.1). Each link has an owner, a version and a way of being checked.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The frame chain of an autonomous truck

FIG-05-01-01 shows the frames an autonomous truck uses and who owns each link.

Read it from the top. The GNSS antenna measures where its phase centre is. The ADS knows where the antenna sits on the truck — the lever arm, (3.0, 1.2, 7.0) m in LAB-05-01-01 [A]: 3.0 m ahead of the rear axle, 1.2 m left of the centre line, 7.0 m above the ground. With the truck's heading (and, if it models them, pitch and roll) the ADS turns the antenna position into the position of the reference point. That is the pose the AMS sees and the controller steers.

The antenna's position is in the grid because of RTK: the rover measures its position relative to GB1, and GB1's coordinate is a grid coordinate held in Survey's control register (ch-12-01, ch-05-02). The AHS map is built in the same grid. The FMS and the mine planning tools design and compute in it too. Between grid, map, FMS and planning there is deliberately no transformation at all [H]: the cheapest transformation to get right is the one that does not exist.

The only conversion into the grid that remains is at the boundary: data arriving in some other frame — a contractor's projected grid, a regional survey — must be converted once, by Survey, with a versioned link, before anyone else uses it. Section 4.3 fits such a link.

Each link fails in its own way [H]:

Link Owner How it fails What it looks like downstream
Sensor → body (lever arm, alignment) ADS supplier defines; the site keeps the values per truck and the Field Technician checks them after maintenance [A] Antenna or bracket replaced and the old lever arm kept; bracket bent One truck, a constant offset everywhere it goes (section 4.6)
Body → grid (pose) ADS localisation, online Attitude ignored on a grade; heading error Offsets that depend on the road, the same on every truck of the class (section 4.6)
GB1 coordinate Survey GB1 moved and given a wrong coordinate Every truck and every rover shifted by the same vector (ch-12-01)
Projected grid → mine grid (link) Survey Scale left out; wrong version; one-point conversion Survey data off by an amount that grows with distance (section 4.4)
GNSS height → RL Survey Wrong height model or offset; heights mixed from two datums Grades and vertical positions wrong (section 4.5)
Grid → AHS map, FMS, planning Map team [A], FMS and planning owners; Survey signs off coordinates A second frame allowed into one of them The three systems disagree about the same node (section 8.3 B)

The last row is not a mathematical link but an organisational one, and it is where most frame errors enter.

4.2 Datum, projection and local grid

FIG-05-01-02 places the three on one page.

On a real site positions are traceable to the Earth in three steps [TBV] (SRC-PUB-GEODETIC-DATUM-EPOCH). A geodetic datum fixes latitude, longitude and ellipsoidal height. A map projection turns latitude and longitude into plane easting and northing; it cannot do so without distortion, so its scale factor and grid convergence vary across the zone it covers. A local mine grid is then related to the projection by a transformation the site documents: the mine grid is rotated to suit the pit, shifted to a convenient origin, and scaled so that a grid metre is a ground metre at the mine. Heights follow a parallel path: GNSS measures heights above the ellipsoid, a height model relates them to a vertical datum, and the mine's RL is defined against that datum or against a local one.

Why the trouble? A plane grid at ground scale makes everyday work simple: distances, volumes and grades are read straight from coordinates. A projected grid a few hundred ppm off ground scale does not — over the 4500.5 m loaded leg of M-20481, 240 ppm is 1.080 m (LAB-05-01-01) — and a geodetic frame is not plane at all.

The reference mine keeps only the last box. REFMINE-GRID and RL are the only frame: there is no datum, no projection and no published link (dashed in FIG-05-01-02). This is deliberate [H]: the mine is fictional and must not be placeable on the Earth, and the AHS needs nothing else, because every system on site uses the grid. What the reference mine leaves out is the work a real site does once, at the boundary, and controls thereafter. LAB-05-01-01 therefore invents a teaching projected grid, PRJ-TEACH [A], with a combined scale factor of 0.999760, a rotation of 1.10° and C01 at PRJ-TEACH (52 000, 18 000). These numbers describe no real projection and place the mine nowhere; they are chosen to be of a plausible size so that the errors in section 4.4 are too.

4.3 The Helmert link

The 2D similarity transformation converts PRJ-TEACH coordinates (x,y) to grid coordinates (e,n):

e=ax−by+te,n=bx+ay+tn,a=scosθ,b=ssinθ

In this form the four unknowns a,b,te,tn appear linearly. Each control mark observed in both frames adds two equations; with two marks the link is determined, with more it is fitted by least squares and the residuals measure how well the marks agree with it. ahs_sim.localisation.fit_similarity (reused from ahs_sim.mapping, the function ch-12-01 used) does exactly this and returns the scale s=a2+b2, the rotation θ=atan2(b,a) and the residuals.

LAB-05-01-01 uses the six control marks CM1–CM6 of ch-12-01's network A — around the pit and the MIA, near D01 — with their published grid coordinates, and observes them in PRJ-TEACH with a per-axis error of 0.02 m [A] (seed 501). The fit:

Quantity True [A] Fitted
Scale (PRJ-TEACH → grid) +240.1 ppm +231.9 ppm
Rotation 1.10° 1.1002°
RMS residual at the six marks — 0.0167 m
Largest residual — 0.0246 m at CM5

The residuals per mark are 0.0098 m (CM1), 0.0135 m (CM2), 0.0206 m (CM3), 0.0065 m (CM4), 0.0246 m (CM5) and 0.0179 m (CM6) — all of the size of the observation noise, none standing out (FIG-05-01-03 a). Applied to every node of mine.yaml and to GB1, the fitted link puts each point within 25.9 mm of its published coordinate, the largest at D01. The 8.2 ppm by which the fitted scale misses the true one is the noise of six marks, not a mistake; over the mine it amounts to the centimetres just quoted. The behaviour of such a fit with distance from the control, and with clustered control, is ch-12-01's subject (its section 4.4) and is not repeated here.

The shifts te and tn are tens of kilometres because PRJ-TEACH has a false origin far from the mine, so a scale or rotation acts about a distant point and small parameter differences become large position differences. And a link has a version: re-established control gives a new fit, and every delivery must name the version it used (ch-12-01's hand-off package).

4.4 Leaving out the scale

The mistake that matters is not a slightly wrong parameter but a missing one. Two scale-free conversions are examples of this mistake, and the lab computes both against the same PRJ-TEACH observations:

  • Rigid fit. Rotation and shifts fitted by least squares on the six marks, scale held at 1 — for instance because a software default assumes the projected grid is already at ground scale.
  • One-point conversion. Rotation taken from the link, scale 1, and the shift set so that one mark — CM1, near the MIA — matches exactly. This is what remains when a job set up for a small area near one mark is reused elsewhere.

Both leave out the 240 ppm. The results, as horizontal error of the converted position against the published grid coordinate (FIG-05-01-03 b and c):

GB1 21.7 mm 0.671 m 0.054 m
C01 15.5 mm 0.461 m 0.303 m
X 5.8 mm 0.193 m 0.470 m

P3 8.5 mm 0.333 m 0.471 m

The pattern follows from the equation. Leaving out a scale s moves a point by approximately (s−1) times its distance from the point about which the conversion is pinned: the centroid of the marks for the rigid fit, CM1 for the one-point conversion. EX02-L lies 4067.2 m from CM1, and 240×10−6×4067.2≈0.98 m; the lab gives 0.964 m, the difference being the rotation and noise taken from the fit.

The rigid fit announces itself; the one-point conversion does not. Fitted on six marks, the rigid fit's residuals are 0.4396 m RMS, largest 0.6469 m at CM1, the mark farthest from the centroid: twenty-six times the residuals of the proper fit. A surveyor who reads the residuals per mark (ch-12-01's rule) sees at once that a parameter is missing. The one-point conversion has no residuals to read — it fits one mark exactly by construction — and its error grows quietly with distance from the mark. Only a check point far from the anchor reveals it: re-observed with the same job, CM5 on the north rim beyond EX02 comes out 1.051 m from its published coordinate, against 19.1 mm with the fitted link.

Lengths are almost unaffected, positions are not. A scale error of 240 ppm changes the length of R05 by 0.321 m and the loaded leg of M-20481 by 1.080 m, which no cycle-time calculation will notice. The same scale error moves a loading spot four kilometres from the anchor by almost a metre, which a Shovel Operator notices on the first truck (section 6.2). That asymmetry is why frame errors survive so long: they do not show in any production number.

4.5 Heights, grades and the dynamics that use them

The AHS uses heights in two ways: directly, for vertical positions such as dump crest heights and windrow heights, and indirectly, through the grade of every segment. mine.yaml defines grades from node coordinates: for R05, from the RL of P1 (420) and P2 (300) over a horizontal run of 1334.2 m, 8.994 %; for R24, from W (330) and P3 (420) over 1422.1 m, 6.328 %. If the RL of the lower end carries an error εa and that of the upper end εb, the map's grade is

Gmap=ΔRL+εb−εaLh,δG=εb−εaLh

which is ahs_sim.localisation.grade_with_rl_error. Two consequences follow at once. A common offset of both ends — the whole mine on a vertical datum that is a constant off — leaves every grade unchanged (the lab checks it). A difference between the ends changes the grade by 0.0750 % per metre on R05 and 0.0703 % per metre on R24.

Where do grades enter the system? ch-07-01 and ch-07-02 give the answers: the loaded climb speed is set by power against grade plus rolling resistance, v=ηP/(mg(G+RR)); the retarding power on an empty descent is mg(G−RR)v; and the stopping distance on a descent depends on the grade through the downhill force. The ADS plans speeds and brakes with the map's grade; the FMS estimates cycle times with it; the truck itself climbs and stops on the true grade. LAB-05-01-01 recomputes these quantities with ahs_sim.dynamics on the map grade for a range of RL errors at the lower end of each ramp (a positive ε means the map holds P2 or W too high):

The first row reproduces the numbers of ch-07-01 and ch-07-02: 11.44 km/h and 421.4 s (ch-07-01), 1078.1 kW, 25.2 m and 56.2 m (ch-07-02, dry and wet adhesion 0.45 and 0.20 [A]). R24 behaves alike at a smaller scale: its true row is 14.71 km/h, 348.6 s and 598.6 kW; with +12.0 m at W the map grade becomes 5.485 % and the predicted climb 16.18 km/h; with −12.0 m, 7.172 % and 13.49 km/h.

Three readings carry the message.

  1. Survey-sized RL errors do not matter to the dynamics. Five centimetres at P2 changes the predicted climb speed in the second decimal and the stopping distance by a centimetre. Even half a metre moves the climb time by about a second. The sensitivity is ∂v/∂G=−v/(G+RR): −0.095 km/h per 0.1 % of grade on R05.
  2. A vertical-datum mix does matter. If P2's RL is taken from one height system and P1's from another — here offset by 12.0 m [A], the size of ch-12-01's teaching height offset — the map grade is off by 0.9 percentage points. The FMS then predicts a climb 31.6 s faster than the truck can make (389.8 s against 421.4 s) and an M-20481 cycle of 1532.5 s against 1564.1 s; more importantly, a stopping distance computed on the map grade is 52.82 m on a wet descent while the truck needs 56.23 m. An error that makes the map flatter than the road is the non-conservative direction for braking.
  3. The error is in what was planned, not in what the truck does. The truck's power and brakes act on the real slope. What goes wrong is everything computed in advance from the map: the speed profile and its feed-forward (ch-07-06), the cycle time the FMS dispatches on, and the stopping distance against which speeds and horizons are set.

The cure is not more precise heights but one vertical datum and a validation that compares segment grades between map versions (REQ-05-01-007). A grade that jumps by 0.9 points after an as-built survey of R05 is a frame error until proved otherwise. Short segments are more sensitive than long ones — the same metre on the 461 m bench ramp R09 is about three times the grade change on R05 — and vertical positions of dump crests, windrows and loading floors use RL directly, without the averaging of a long run.

4.6 From antenna to reference point

The last link of the chain is on the truck. The ADS knows the antenna's grid position; it wants the reference point's. In the level, two-dimensional case this is body_to_grid run backwards: subtract the lever arm, rotated by the heading. Three things make it less simple.

Attitude. The antenna is 7.0 m above the ground [A]. When the truck is pitched, the antenna moves horizontally by about hsinθ; when it is rolled, sideways by hsinϕ. On R05 the truck is pitched by the grade, 5.14°; on a road with 2 % crossfall [A] it is rolled by 1.15°. If the ADS ignores attitude, LAB-05-01-01 gives:

Case on R05 Error of the reference point Direction
Pitch ignored, climbing 0.639 m along track, placed further down the ramp
Pitch ignored, descending 0.615 m along track, placed further down the ramp
2 % crossfall ignored 0.140 m lateral
Lever arm recorded 0.25 m wrong (left) [A] 0.250 m lateral, constant everywhere
Heading error 1° [A] 0.056 m mostly lateral

The signature tells the cause. Pitch-related errors appear on ramps only and are the same for every truck of the class; they matter at loading and dumping spots on grades and in along-track stopping positions. A wrong lever arm follows one truck everywhere and is a constant lateral bias — exactly the kind of error ch-07-06 showed the tracking monitor cannot see, because the controller follows it (its section 4.9: a 0.5 m bias left the truck 0.500 m off the path with a measured error of 0.000 m). A heading error scales with the length of the horizontal lever arm, which is one reason to mount antennas near the reference point where the truck allows it [A].

Ownership. The ADS supplier defines the body frame, reference point and lever arms; their values per truck are site configuration records that change with hardware [A]. After work on an antenna, a bracket or the cab, the Field Technician verifies the lever arm before the truck returns to autonomous operation (REQ-05-01-003), confirmed by a static check on a surveyed point [R].

Who signs off a change of grid or RL convention? Survey, because Survey owns the frame — but the map team, the FMS and planning owners, the ADS support function and the AHS Controller must hear of it first. A change to the grid itself would make every coordinate in every system wrong at once; the handbook treats it as a change to the whole AHS [H].

4.8 Trade-offs and model limits

  • PRJ-TEACH, the lever arm, antenna height, crossfall and every error input are assumptions [A]; real projections, height models and truck geometries are site matters [TBV]. The link is 2D plus a separate height.
  • Nodes are converted without observation noise, so only the conversion error shows.
  • Attitude is a simple rotation of the lever arm; grade effects use the ahs_sim dynamics of ch-07-01 and ch-07-02.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

All systems hold the grid and RL; Survey issues every new coordinate with its control, link and GB1 versions and a check point result (ch-12-01's hand-off package). Lever arms are verified after maintenance and at intervals the site sets [A]. Map validation compares grades between versions. Nothing about frames appears on the HMI in normal operation except the map frame record next to the map version.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE: a loading spot in the wrong conversion (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE was chosen over SCN-MAP-R05 for the walkthrough for three reasons. A loading spot is where a frame error shows directly — a truck spotting against a shovel has little to spare, a truck on a 30 m road has metres. The new loading area is a single point issued by Survey in one delivery, so the chain from rover to truck is short and complete. And ch-12-01 has already walked SCN-MAP-R05 in detail from the survey side; section 6.3 adds what this chapter contributes to it.

The face advance keeps EX02-L as the node of the loading area; the new spot is issued at its coordinates.

Seen from the frames, the scenario has one cause and three missed barriers. The cause is a conversion without scale. The barriers are the residual check (none possible for a one-point conversion), the check point (not observed), and the package rule (not applied). The truck could not be a barrier: its fix was healthy, its position matched the map, and its tracking error was zero.

6.3 Abnormal operation — SCN-MAP-R05: heights in the as-built

ch-12-01 walks SCN-MAP-R05 from the survey side: detour surveyed before the closure, R05 surveyed during it, the as-built submitted at T+0 s, validated at T+1800 s, deployed at T+2400 s (registry). This chapter adds one check to the validation step [H]: the map change compares the grade of every changed segment with the previous version. A windrow and edge rework does not change the RL of P1 or P2 by metres; if the new R05 grade differs from 8.994 % by more than the site's threshold [A], the heights of the as-built are suspected of a vertical-datum or height-model error before the map is deployed. With a 12.0 m mix the change would be 0.9 percentage points — unmissable if anyone looks, invisible if no one does.

6.4 Degraded operation — a truck with a wrong lever arm

After a cab repair a truck returns with its antenna bracket refitted 0.25 m further left and the lever arm unchanged [A]. With a healthy fix and a small tracking error it drives 0.25 m to one side of every lane (section 4.6). The pattern — one truck, every road, the same side — routes it to the Field Technician and the Maintenance Planner, not to Survey. The barrier is the lever-arm check (REQ-05-01-003); if it fails, a static check on a surveyed point or a Shovel Operator who sees one truck always spot off-line finds it.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE

  • T+900 s. The rover's job is configuration, not data: it carries the link. A rover job that is not the site's current link is a frame error waiting for a delivery. The rover should report the link version it used into the delivery automatically [R].
  • T+1500 s. The hand-off package is the interface that should have stopped the error: frame, link version and a check-point result are mandatory fields (ch-12-01 section 4.8). A missing check-point result is a reason to reject.
  • T+2400 s and T+3600 s. The map change and the activation are correct operations on wrong data; the AMS cannot know. The map frame record (REQ-12-01-008) can show the link version of the deliveries a version rests on, which lets a validation tool flag a delivery made with a version other than the current one [R].
  • T+3700 s. The truck's report is consistent: healthy fix, final position on the map's spot, tracking error near zero. The first independent observation is a person's: the Shovel Operator.
  • T+3900 to T+4500 s. The check point discriminates in one observation: 1.051 m against a 19.1 mm expectation is not noise. The reprocessing does not need a new pickup; the raw observations are re-converted with the right link.
  • T+5100 to T+5400 s. The correction is a new map version through the normal change process (ch-12-09).

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

Almost nothing, and that is the point of the chapter. A frame error produces no alarm: the trucks report healthy RTK fixes, positions on their map spots and small tracking errors. What the control room does see:

  • Reports from people. A Shovel Operator whose trucks spot short, long or to one side; a Survey pickup that does not match the map.
  • The pattern of those reports. All trucks at one new spot → the spot (a conversion or a survey error). One truck at all spots → the truck (lever arm, antenna). All trucks on ramps only, along track → attitude handling (ADS).
  • The map frame record next to the map version (ch-12-01 REQ-12-01-008): control register, link and GB1 versions on which the map rests, and — with this chapter — the link version of the latest deliveries.
  • Grade changes reported by map validation between versions.

8.3 Procedure excerpts

A. The AHS map and the face disagree at a loading spot (AHS Controller, Shovel Operator, Survey) — SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE.

  1. Shovel Operator (T+3730 s): report by radio where the truck spotted relative to where it should be: short or long, left or right, roughly how far. Load the truck only if it is safely within reach; otherwise ask for it to be moved off.
  2. AHS Controller (T+3760 s): check the pattern: is this the first truck at a new spot, and have other trucks spotted correctly elsewhere today? Check the truck's solution state and localisation status.
  3. AHS Controller: if the spot is new or recently changed, suspend the loading area in the AMS (an AMS operation); hold trucks already at the queue point; have the FMS reassign EX02's trucks. Inform the Pit Supervisor of the hold and the expected cost.
  4. AHS Controller → Survey: "Trucks spotting about half a metre short and to the left at the new EX02 spot, first truck after activation at T+3600 s. Map version …; delivery ID …. Please check the spot's coordinates with a check point."
  5. Survey (T+3900 s): re-observe a check point near the face with the same equipment and job; compare with its published coordinate. A difference far above the expected noise points to the conversion; check the job's link version.
  6. Survey (T+4500 s): reprocess the pickup with the current link; observe the check point again; re-issue the package with the check result.
  7. Map team [A] (T+5100 s): validate the corrected loading area; deploy through the map change process.
  8. AHS Controller (T+5400 s): activate the corrected loading area; release the hold; ask the Shovel Operator to confirm the first truck's spot.
  9. AHS Controller: record the event, the cause (conversion), the delay codes and the barrier that failed (the package accepted without a check-point result).

Never: edit the spot in the map "to where the trucks stop"; let trucks continue to the spot "because the bucket reaches"; or close the event without knowing why the coordinates were wrong.

B. New nodes and roads before use — e.g. P3 and R24 (Survey, map team, FMS and planning owners).

  1. Survey: issue the coordinates of every new or changed node (here P3, W and the R24 edge strings) in REFMINE-GRID and RL, with link, control and GB1 versions and a check-point result.
  2. Map team [A], FMS owner [A], planning owner [A]: each loads the coordinates into their system from the same delivery — not from each other's systems.
  3. Survey: compare the node coordinates as held in the three systems; any difference above the site's limit [A] stops the release. For scale: if one system held P3 from a rigid, scale-1 conversion, it would be 0.333 m off; from the one-point conversion, 0.471 m (LAB-05-01-01).
  4. Survey: compare the grades derived in the FMS and the map for every changed segment (R24 6.328 %).
  5. AHS Controller: use the new road only when Survey has confirmed steps 3 and 4 (REQ-05-01-008).

C. A truck returns from work on its antenna or cab (Field Technician, Maintenance Planner).

  1. Field Technician: verify the antenna and sensor lever arms against the truck's configuration record with the supplier's procedure [A].
  2. Field Technician: run a static check on a surveyed point [R]: the reported reference point against the point's published coordinate.
  3. Maintenance Planner: release the truck to autonomous operation only with both results recorded.

8.5 Training and competency implications

AHS Controllers learn that a healthy fix and a zero tracking error do not prove the truck is in the right place, and to read the pattern of spotting reports. Shovel Operators report spotting offsets early, with direction and size. Surveyors learn why a one-point job must never leave the area it was set up for, and to observe a check point far from any anchor. Map-team members reject packages without a check-point result; Field Technicians learn the lever-arm check.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-05-01-001 [H] Every position exchanged between Survey, mine planning, the FMS, the AHS map and the ADS shall be in REFMINE-GRID and RL; data in any other frame shall be converted once, by Survey, with the current versioned link, before any other system uses it. Rationale: there is no transformation between grid, map, FMS and planning; conversion errors enter only at the boundary (section 4.1). Verification: inspection of data interfaces and hand-off packages

REQ-05-01-002 [H] The site shall keep a frame-chain record naming, for each transformation from sensor to AHS map, its owner, its current version or values, what changes it and how it is checked. Rationale: every link in FIG-05-01-01 fails in its own way and has a different owner (section 4.7). Verification: inspection

REQ-05-01-003 [H] After any work that may move a GNSS antenna or another positioning sensor relative to the body, the truck's lever arms shall be verified and recorded before it returns to autonomous operation. Rationale: a 0.25 m lever-arm error is a constant lateral bias on every lane that neither the receiver nor the tracking monitor reports (sections 4.6, 6.4; ch-07-06). Verification: inspection of maintenance records; demonstration of the static check

REQ-05-01-004 [H] The ADS shall account for pitch and roll when converting the antenna position to the reference point, or the site's localisation error budget shall include the resulting error for the steepest grade and crossfall in the ODD. Rationale: ignoring pitch on R05 places the reference point 0.64 m along track; ignoring 2 % crossfall, 0.14 m laterally (LAB-05-01-01). Verification: analysis (LAB-05-01-01); test on a ramp with a surveyed point

REQ-05-01-005 [H] A conversion between another frame and REFMINE-GRID used for AHS data shall be a link with at least scale, rotation and two shifts, fitted on control marks around the area of use, with residuals reported per mark; a scale-free or one-point conversion shall not be used. Rationale: leaving out a 240 ppm scale gives 0.44 m RMS residuals in a rigid fit and, in a one-point conversion, 0.964 m at EX02-L with no residual to warn (section 4.4). Verification: analysis (LAB-05-01-01); inspection of link records

REQ-05-01-006 [H] A new or changed loading spot, dump spot or road shall be accepted into the AHS map only with a check point observed near it with the same equipment and job, within the site's limit [A]. Rationale: the check point is the only barrier against a conversion error that leaves no residual (CM5: 1.051 m against 19.1 mm). Verification: inspection of hand-off packages; test (LAB-05-01-01)

REQ-05-01-007 [H] Map validation shall compare the grade of every changed segment with the previous map version and flag a change above a site threshold [A] for Survey to confirm before deployment. Rationale: a 12.0 m vertical-datum mix changes the R05 grade by 0.9 percentage points and makes a wet stopping distance computed on the map 52.82 m instead of 56.23 m (section 4.5). Verification: test of the map validation tool; analysis (LAB-05-01-01)

REQ-05-01-008 [H] A new or changed node shall be used for autonomous operation only after Survey has confirmed that its coordinates in the FMS, the AHS map and the planning tools agree within the site's limit [A] and come from the same delivery. Rationale: systems loaded from different conversions disagree by decimetres (P3: 0.333 m from a rigid fit) without any alarm (section 8.3 B). Verification: inspection; demonstration before a new road is opened

REQ-05-01-009 [H] When spotting reports show a systematic offset at a loading spot, the AHS Controller shall suspend the loading area and hold trucks from it until Survey has confirmed or corrected its coordinates; the map shall not be edited to match where trucks stop. Rationale: the trucks follow the map exactly; only a survey check separates a wrong spot from a wrong truck (section 6.2). Verification: drill; inspection of event records

10. Verification and validation

  • The lab verifies its arithmetic. Seven tests in LAB-05-01-01 check the link round trip and an exact fit, the fit on six marks, the conversion errors at every node (fitted, rigid and one-point), the check point and hold cost, route lengths, the grade sweep against the Volume 07 numbers, the M-20481 cycle with grade errors, and the frame-chain cases.
  • The lab does not validate a site. PRJ-TEACH and all error inputs are assumptions [A]. A site validates its link with check points not used in the fit, spread over the area of use, in plan and height.
  • Validation of the whole chain on site. Park trucks on surveyed points — at a loading spot, on a ramp, on a flat road — and compare the reported reference point with the published coordinate [R]; on a ramp this also checks attitude handling.
  • Procedural validation. Drills: a package with a deliberately wrong link version (the map team must reject it); a spotting report at a new spot (the controller must suspend and call Survey, not edit the map); a truck returned from cab work (the lever-arm record must exist before release).

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Allowing each system to hold its own frame and convert "on import".
  • Using a one-point or scale-free conversion because "the site is small".
  • Treating lever arms as installation constants rather than versioned configuration per truck.
  • Ignoring attitude in the antenna-to-reference conversion on a ramp mine.
  • Validating map changes on geometry only, without comparing grades.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Reading a healthy fix and a zero tracking error as proof that the truck is in the right place.
  • Moving a loading spot in the map "by half a metre" to where the trucks stop.
  • Loading a new road into the FMS from the planning tool instead of from Survey's delivery.
  • Releasing a truck after cab work without the lever-arm check because "the antenna wasn't touched".
  • Accepting a survey package without a check-point result because the shovel is waiting.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — The scale-free one-point error at EX02-L. Scale error s−1=240×10−6 [A]. EX02-L is 4067.2 m from CM1. Error ≈240×10−6×4067.2=0.976 m; the lab gives 0.964 m because its rotation and anchor come from noisy observations. Decomposed along DT17's approach from EX02-Q: 0.521 m short and 0.811 m to the left. At GB1, 141 m from CM1, the same conversion is only 0.054 m off — which is why it looked fine where it was set up.

Example 2 — Why the rigid fit shows its error. Without scale the residual at a mark is about (s−1)ri, with ri its distance from the centroid. For network A, ∑ri2=21548333 m² (ch-12-01), so the RMS distance is 21548333/6=1895.1 m and the expected RMS residual 240.1×10−6×1895.1=0.455 m. The lab's 0.4396 m differs because the fit absorbs part of the error into rotation and shift and the observations carry noise. Against 0.0167 m with the scale, it is unmistakable.

Example 3 — Grade from a height-system mix. R05: ΔRL=120 m, Lh=1334.2 m, 8.994 %. With P2 12.0 m too high in the map: (120−12)/1334.2=8.095 %. Loaded climb speed on the map grade: ηP/(mg(G+RR))=1440000/(385000×9.81×0.11095)=3.437 m/s = 12.37 km/h, against 11.44 km/h on the true grade. Climb time on 1339.6 m: 389.8 s against 421.4 s.

Example 4 — Attitude on R05. Antenna height 7.0 m [A], pitch arctan(0.08994)=5.14°: 7.0sin5.14°=0.627 m, plus 3.0(1−cos5.14°)=0.012 m from the forward lever arm: 0.639 m along track, as the lab gives for the climb.

15. Key takeaways

  • The truck follows the map; a frame error moves the map, so the truck is precisely wrong with every indicator healthy.
  • One grid and one RL datum for every system; conversions only at the boundary, once, by Survey, with a versioned link.
  • A link without scale is the classic error: a rigid fit shows it in its residuals (0.44 m RMS); a one-point conversion hides it until a distant check point (EX02-L 0.964 m; CM5 1.051 m).
  • Lengths barely notice a scale error (1.080 m over M-20481); positions far from the anchor do.
  • RL errors of centimetres do not matter to grades and dynamics; a vertical-datum mix of metres does — and flatter in the map is the non-conservative direction for braking.
  • On the truck, a wrong lever arm is a constant bias that no monitor sees; attitude moves the reference point by hsinθ on every ramp.
  • When the face and the map disagree: suspend, hold, call Survey, find the conversion — never move the map to the trucks.
