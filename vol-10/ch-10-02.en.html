1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

At 10:00 crusher C01 trips on a blocked chute. On the queue display for C01-Q the AHS Controller sees one truck, which was already waiting for its turn; at 10:05 there are five, at 10:20 fifteen. At 10:05 the FMS proposes to send the four empty ore trucks to waste and the controller approves; had the trucks been held instead, the display would have shown nineteen at 10:25:45 — every ore truck of the shift. When C01 restarts at 10:45 the line starts to move one truck every 90 s.

The next morning three people look at queues and see different things. The plant engineer points out that, on a day without any stoppage, ore trucks queue for about 16.5 truck-hours at C01-Q, and asks why the mine sends more trucks than the crusher can take. The Shovel Operator at EX02 says that the shovel waits for trucks every hour while those trucks stand in line at the crusher. And the shift report of Volume 01 shows only 12.4 truck-hours of queueing for the whole fleet. Three questions follow:

  • Is the queue at C01-Q on a normal day a fault to fix, or the price of keeping the crusher fed?
  • Why do two models of the same mine give different queue levels, and which should a report use?
  • How do the queues change when the trucks are rerouted over the west ramp, and what should the controller watch?

This chapter extends the truck–shovel queueing of ch-01-05 to the closed network of the whole mine. It covers:

  • the reference mine as a closed queueing network of trucks, loading units, C01 and D01;
  • three models of that network — a deterministic bound, an analytic model with exponential service and a seeded simulation — and what each says about waiting at EX02-Q and C01-Q;
  • how one tipping bay at C01 and three tip points at D01 place the bottleneck, and how it moves with fleet size and with R05 open or closed;
  • the queue-state signals the FMS needs from the AMS, and how the waiting is coded.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Model EX02 and C01 as service points in a closed queueing network, using spot, load and dump times from equipment.yaml and mine.yaml, and calculate expected queue length and waiting time [A].
  2. Analyse how one tipping position at C01 versus three tip points at D01 moves the bottleneck as the number of assigned trucks changes.
  3. Evaluate the limits of match factor as a predictor of waiting when cycle times are stochastic, comparing analytic and seeded simulation results.
  4. Calculate how the longer west-ramp route during an R05 closure changes truck arrival rates at EX02-Q and C01-Q and shifts the bottleneck to haulage.
  5. Specify the queue-state signals (queue length, expected wait, service state) that the FMS needs from the AMS at each queue point.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Identify from the queue displays at EX02-Q and C01-Q whether the binding constraint is the shovel, the crusher or the haul road.
  2. Select a response to a growing queue at C01-Q (rebalance assignments, hold trucks, divert to waste) and estimate its tonnage effect with a worked example.
  3. Anticipate the queue changes at EX01–EX04 when trucks are rerouted over R24, and adjust assignments before loading units are starved.
  4. Distinguish a loading unit starved of trucks from a congested destination queue, and explain each situation to the Shovel Operator and to the plant.
  5. Attribute waiting time at EX02-Q and C01-Q over a shift to the correct delay codes and propose one improvement at the shift meeting.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

A queue is the most visible thing in a pit. It is also the easiest to misread, because the same line of trucks can mean three different things: a crusher that is doing exactly what it should, a crusher that is down, or a shovel that cannot keep up. Each has a different owner, a different delay code and a different fix.

Volume 01 left three loose ends at the reference mine:

  • The crusher queue on a normal day. C01 is the bottleneck of the ore system (ch-01-05, section 4.8): its single tipping bay accepts 40 trucks per hour and the 19 ore trucks could bring 43.6. ch-01-07's fleet model found 16.4 truck-hours of queue at C01-Q in an undisturbed shift. Nobody has yet asked whether that queue is waste or protection.
  • Two models, two queue levels. ch-01-05's shift KPI example used synthetic truck logs with 12.4 truck-hours of queue for all 37 trucks; ch-01-07's fleet model gives 16.4 truck-hours at C01-Q alone. ahs_sim.fms now provides one shared fleet model. This chapter does not change the numbers of ch-01-05 and ch-01-07; it explains the difference.
  • The single-loop view. ch-01-05 modelled EX02 with C01 as its private destination and found the queue at the shovel. Once EX01 shares C01, the queue is somewhere else.

Behind all three lies one question: which model of queueing does a controller, a planner or a plant engineer need, and what does each model leave out?

3. Terminology and foundations

Closed queueing network [H]. A set of servers visited in turn by a fixed population of customers that never leave. At the reference mine the customers are the 37 producing trucks, the servers are the four loading units, C01 and D01, and the roads between them are delays that every truck passes without waiting for another. In a closed network the arrival rate at a server is not an input; it is a result of how fast the trucks come back from everywhere else.

Service time. The time a server needs per truck, including spotting: 35 + 132 = 167 s at a shovel, 40 + 50 = 90 s at C01, 45 + 40 = 85 s at D01 (equipment.yaml, mine.yaml) [A]. A tipping bay is one position at which a truck can tip; C01 has one, D01 has three tip points.

Waiting per cycle and queue length. The time a truck spends waiting at a queue point in one cycle (seconds), and the number of trucks waiting there at a moment or on average. They are linked by Little's law — mean number waiting = arrival rate × mean wait — a general queueing result, proved for finite means and stationary processes [F] (SRC-PUB-LITTLE-1961), that the lab checks numerically for its own model.

Busy fraction (server utilisation, ch-01-05). The fraction of time a server is serving trucks. A server with busy fraction 1.0 over a period is the bottleneck of that period.

Starved and congested. A server is starved when it is working and waiting for trucks: a shovel's idle time (DLY-NP-03), or a crusher with gaps between tips. A queue point is congested when trucks wait there for a working server (DLY-NP-01, DLY-NP-02). Waiting for a server that is out of service is neither: it is an operating delay (DLY-OD-10, DLY-OD-11).

Mean value analysis (MVA). An exact recursive method for the mean throughput, waiting and queue length of a closed network with a product-form solution [F] (Reiser and Lavenberg, SRC-PUB-REISER-LAVENBERG-1980); that exponentially distributed service times at first-come-first-served servers give such a solution is [TBV]. The handbook uses it as a pessimistic analytic bound, not as a description of the mine.

Warm-up period. The first part of a simulated shift, excluded from steady-state statistics because the trucks start in an arbitrary arrangement. The labs use one hour, as ahs_sim.queueing.simulate_loop does [A].

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The reference mine as a closed queueing network

FIG-10-02-01 shows the network. Ore trucks cycle EX01 or EX02 → C01 → back; waste trucks cycle EX03 or EX04 → D01 → back. Under fixed assignment the two ore circuits share one server, C01; the two waste circuits share D01.

Three properties make the network behave differently from ch-01-05's single loop:

  1. Arrivals are generated by the network. Nothing arrives from outside. If C01 slows down, fewer trucks come back to the shovels, which then wait for trucks; if a shovel slows down, C01 waits.
  2. Shared servers decide where the queue forms. In a single loop the slower server collects the queue. In a network the queue collects at the server whose capacity is smallest relative to the trucks that visit it — here C01, which serves two shovels.
  3. The population is fixed. Every truck that waits at C01-Q is a truck that is not at a shovel. The total waiting in the system is set by the number of trucks and the bottleneck, not by the places (ch-10-01, section 4.4).

4.2 Three models of the same network

Deterministic bound [H]. Constant times. The network delivers the smallest of what the trucks could bring, what the loading units could load and what the destination could accept (ch-10-01, section 4.3). When the destination binds, each of the N trucks sharing it completes one cycle every NtD seconds, and its waiting per cycle is

tw=NtD−tc

The bound is exact for constant times after start-up, optimistic otherwise. ahs_sim.queueing.deterministic_loop is its single-loop form; ahs_sim.fms.simulate_fleet without a seed computes the same network event by event.

Mean value analysis [H], method [F] (SRC-PUB-REISER-LAVENBERG-1980). For trucks of class c (EX01's or EX02's) with population vector 𝐧, mean service time Dc,k at queueing station k and travel time Zc, the recursion over populations is

Rc,k(𝐧)=Dc,k(1+Qk(𝐧−𝐞c)),Xc(𝐧)=ncZc+∑kRc,k(𝐧),Qk(𝐧)=∑cXc(𝐧)Rc,k(𝐧)

where R is the time spent at a station (waiting plus service), X the throughput and Q the mean number at a station. It assumes exponential service, a coefficient of variation of 1 — far more variable than a shovel or a crusher — so it over-estimates waiting. It is implemented lab-locally (mva in LAB-10-02-01).

Seeded simulation. ahs_sim.fms.simulate_fleet with a seed draws service and travel times from gamma distributions with coefficients of variation 0.15 and 0.10 [A], the values of ch-01-05. The lab averages ten seeds and measures after a one-hour warm-up. For the single loop of ch-01-05 the lab calls ahs_sim.queueing.simulate_loop with the same settings.

Little's law ties the three together. With arrival rate λ at a queue point and mean wait W per arrival, the mean number waiting is

L=λW

and the lab verifies it on the fleet model's own event log: computed from the time-average of waiting trucks and from arrivals times waits, the two agree to machine precision.

Four points stand out.

  1. The deterministic models agree. The bound and the fleet model give the same 40 loads per hour and the same 146 s wait at C01-Q — the "about 146 s" of ch-01-07. All the waiting is at the crusher; EX02-Q is empty.
  2. Variability moves waiting to the shovel and costs the bottleneck. With realistic variability EX02's trucks wait 64.7 s at EX02-Q and 110.3 s at C01-Q: 175 s per cycle in total, 29 s more than in the deterministic model. C01 is busy 96.7 % of the time instead of 100 %: in 3.3 % of the shift no truck is ready to tip, and the system loses 288 t per hour.
  3. The exponential model is far too pessimistic. MVA predicts 7202 t/h and C01 only 82 % busy. Exponential service treats a 167 s loading as if it were as likely to take 10 s as 400 s. It is useful as a bound — "it cannot be worse than this unless something else is wrong" — not as a forecast.
  4. The models bracket the truth. Deterministic ≥ seeded gamma ≥ exponential for throughput, in that order, as the lab's test asserts. Which one is closer to a real mine depends on the variability in its cycle events (Volume 22).

The single loop and the network disagree about where the queue is. In the single loop EX02 is the slower server (167 s against 90 s), so every truck beyond MF = 1 queues at EX02-Q and C01-Q never has a queue. In the network C01 is shared and saturated, so the queue forms at C01-Q first — 145.6 s per cycle already at the baseline nine trucks — and reaches EX02-Q only from the eleventh truck, when EX02 itself is overloaded. From 12 trucks the deterministic C01 wait stops growing at 355.6 s: EX02 has become a second bottleneck and the extra trucks queue at EX02-Q.

This answers the question ch-01-05 left open (section 4.6): the match factor of one shovel cannot predict waiting when loops share a destination. MF = 0.96 at nine trucks says "slightly under-trucked"; the network says "146 s of queue per cycle at the crusher". With variability the match factor is weaker still: at seven trucks (MF 0.75) the seeded network already has 37 s at EX02-Q and 67 s at C01-Q. The planning question is no longer "how many trucks per shovel?" but "how many trucks per bottleneck?".

The two destinations are built differently. C01 has one tipping bay and 90 s of service; D01 has three tip points and 85 s. The fleet model's waits here are averaged over both circuits of a system (the ore row: 127.5 s; EX02's trucks alone wait 145.6 s). Three conclusions:

  1. D01 never binds. With three tip points it is at most 34 % busy, even with 24 waste trucks; the waste system goes from truck-limited to shovel-limited (two shovels, 43.1 loads per hour) without D01 ever queueing.
  2. A single position would make D01 the bottleneck, but only just. One tip point accepts 3600 / 85 = 42.35 loads per hour, less than the two waste shovels' 43.11. Below 22 trucks it hardly matters (14.6 s of wait at 18 trucks); above 24 it decides the output.
  3. C01's single bay is what makes the ore system crusher-limited. With a second bay the 19 ore trucks would bring 42.27 loads per hour, limited by EX01's shovel (its ten trucks could bring more than it loads) and by EX02's nine trucks, and from 21 trucks by the two ore shovels. The ore system would have had its bottleneck at the loading units, as the single-loop view assumed.

4.7 Why queue levels differ between models

How much do trucks queue on a normal DAY shift? The handbook now has four answers. LAB-10-02-01 computes three of them from code — loading the fleet model of ch-01-07 (LAB-01-07-01), unchanged, by file path — and quotes the fourth from ch-01-05:

ch-01-05 shift KPI example (synthetic logs) all queues of 37 trucks: 12.4 — —
ch-01-07 fleet model (LAB-01-07-01) 16.38 / 15.58 1.96 / 1.74 103 180

"After 1 h" counts only waiting between 07:00 and 18:00. Three effects explain every difference:

  1. Start-up. ahs_sim.fms starts each shovel's trucks spread over the empty leg, ch-01-07's model over the whole cycle. In ahs_sim.fms the trucks therefore reach the shovels in a bunch and queue there in the first half hour (6.22 against 1.96 truck-hours of DLY-NP-01), and C01 tips only 29 loads in the first hour. After the first hour the two deterministic models agree within 0.03 truck-hours on both codes. Neither start is "right": a real shift starts from wherever the handover left the trucks.
  2. Variability. Seeded runs queue less at C01-Q and much more at the shovels: 10.40 of the 17.30 truck-hours of DLY-NP-01 after the first hour belong to ore trucks, the rest to waste trucks, whose shovels have queues of their own once arrivals bunch. Total queueing after the first hour rises from 17.3 to 30.4 truck-hours, and ore falls by 3.3 %.
  3. Synthetic logs. ch-01-05's KPI example drew queue events for each truck independently [A]. They are not tied to the bottleneck, and they are lower than any network model gives.

This chapter does not change the numbers of Volume 01: ch-01-05's KPI arithmetic is correct for its synthetic shift, and ch-01-07's fleet model is correct for its start-up. For queue levels, the handbook's reference from Volume 10 on is ahs_sim.fms, stated with its start-up treatment and seed (REQ-10-02-004). The ore tonnage — the number the plant and the plan care about — is the same in both deterministic models, because the bottleneck sets it.

4.8 The queue-state signals the FMS needs

The FMS cannot see a queue; the AMS holds the trucks and the reservations. For each queue point the FMS needs [H]:

Signal Source Used for
Trucks queued for a working server, and trucks held for a server out of service — as two counts AMS (holds at queue points, reservation withheld with reason) Pre-coding DLY-NP-01/02 versus DLY-OD-10/11 (ch-01-05, REQ-01-05-004)
Service state of the server, with cause, since-time and expected return if known Plant (ICD-C01-STATUS: READY, STOPPING, NOT_READY), Shovel Operator Dispatch inputs (ch-10-01); expected wait
Expected wait for the next arrival FMS, from queue length, service time and service state HMI; dispatch's expected start Su(t)
Arrivals and departures per interval AMS cycle events Arrival rate; Little's-law check on the counts
Timestamp of each value AMS, plant Staleness (ch-10-01, REQ-10-01-004)

Two rules matter more than the fields. The counts of trucks queued and trucks held must stay separate, or the FMS cannot tell a congested crusher from a stopped one. And an expected wait must never be computed from a normal service time while the server is not ready: a display that shows "C01-Q: 19 trucks, expected wait 28 min" at 10:26, as if C01 were tipping, is wrong by the length of the stoppage (REQ-10-02-006).

4.9 Trade-offs and model limits

  • Which model, when? The deterministic bound is enough to find the bottleneck and the knee; a seeded model is needed for waiting below the knee and for the value of protecting the bottleneck; MVA bounds the pessimistic side. None includes road interaction.
  • Assumed variability. CVs 0.15 and 0.10 [A]; with real cycle events they would be fitted per server (Volume 22).
  • Fixed assignment in the sweeps. Dynamic dispatch moves waiting between queue points without changing the total when the bottleneck is fixed (ch-10-01).
  • Start-up and warm-up. Every steady-state number depends on excluding the first hour; whole-shift numbers include a start-up that depends on the model's initial positions.
  • Product form. MVA's exactness needs exponential service with the same mean for every class at a server; at C01 both ore classes share 90 s, so the assumption holds formally, but the exponential distribution itself does not describe the mine.

5. Responsibilities and interface boundaries

Element Responsibility for queues
FMS Decides the population of each circuit (allocation, dispatch); estimates expected waits; codes waiting from AMS events; reports queue time by place and code
AMS Holds trucks at queue points; grants the tipping bay or loading spot in turn; withholds reservations when a server is out of service, with reason; publishes queued and held counts and arrival events
ADS Stops the truck at the queue point within its granted path; reports arrival at and departure from queue points
VCS Spotting and tipping execution; reports tipping complete
Independent safety functions May stop trucks in a queue; such stops are not queueing and are coded DLY-OD-13 (Volume 04)
Onboard safety-related systems A payload alarm at the loading spot can extend a service time (ch-10-07, ch-13-04)
People Plant reports C01's state and expected return; Shovel Operator reports loading-unit state; AHS Controller watches queue displays, approves responses, confirms codes; Pit Supervisor decides on rebalancing and diversion

The boundary that matters is between queued and held. Only the AMS knows that a truck at C01-Q is stopped because the tipping-bay reservation was withheld, and only with that event can the FMS separate a queue (non-productive time) from a hold (operating delay). The FMS, in turn, must not infer a server's state from its queue: a long line at C01-Q is what a healthy, saturated crusher looks like.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — the crusher-limited baseline

With R05 open and all machines available C01 tips 40 loads per hour and is never starved in the deterministic model. On average 1.42 trucks wait at C01-Q and every EX02 truck waits 145.6 s per cycle. This queue is the buffer that keeps the bottleneck busy: with realistic variability it shrinks to 1.19 trucks and C01 is starved 3.3 % of the time. The queue at C01-Q on a normal day is therefore not a fault — it is the price of a fed crusher, and the place to look for an improvement is the crusher's capacity or the allocation of trucks between ore and waste, not the queue.

Reading the queue. Before T+0 the queue is the normal buffer of one or two trucks. From T+0 it grows at the rate the ore trucks arrive — 18 trucks in 1545 s, about one every 86 s — until the population runs out: with all 19 ore trucks held, the queue cannot grow further, and both ore shovels have nothing to load. After the restart it drains at C01's rate, 40 trucks per hour, minus the trucks that come back from the shovels in the meantime. The hold costs 10.25 truck-hours of DLY-OD-10 and adds 2.57 truck-hours of DLY-NP-02 after the restart (19.12 against 16.55 truck-hours in the undisturbed shift); ore lost is 6380 t, as ch-01-07. The per-truck timeline of ahs_sim.fms differs from ch-01-07's because the models start the trucks differently (section 4.7): the first truck held at T+0 is a different truck. The totals — 10.25 truck-hours of DLY-OD-10 and 6380 t under hold — are the same; ch-01-05 and ch-01-07 remain the reference for truck-level events.

6.3 Loading unit relocating — SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE

When EX02 relocates for an hour (ch-10-01, section 6.2), the bottleneck moves from C01 to EX01 and the queues redistribute. Under fixed assignment EX02's nine trucks park at EX02-Q (operating delay, not a queue) and C01-Q drains: queueing at C01 falls by 1.73 truck-hours over the shift. Under dynamic dispatch seven trucks queue at EX01-Q at 10:59. EX03 and EX04 are untouched as long as the trucks stay on ore. The queue display shows a congested EX01-Q and an empty C01-Q with gaps between tips: a starved crusher behind a saturated shovel.

6.4 Degraded operation — SCN-MAP-R05

While R05 is closed (registered timeline: survey T+0 s, validation T+1800 s, reopening T+2400 s, routes back at T+2700 s; ch-10-01, section 6.3), the queue at C01-Q almost disappears (0.04 trucks on average) and the ore shovels wait longer for trucks. When routes return to R05 at T+2700 s the ore cycles shorten by 236 s, the knee moves back to 17.4 trucks, and the C01-Q buffer rebuilds. The controller should expect the crusher queue to return and should not read it as a new problem.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Single loop, network bound or simulation? A single loop is easy and wrong whenever loops share a server. The network bound is still easy — three numbers per system — and finds the bottleneck and the knee exactly. A seeded simulation is needed for the region around the knee and for the value of buffers. A planning tool should start with the bound and show the seeded estimate with its assumptions (REQ-10-02-003).

Report queues as time or as counts? Truck-hours by code are what the time usage model needs; counts are what a controller reads. Little's law converts one into the other, provided both refer to the same window and population. A report that mixes a whole-shift time with an after-warm-up count will not reconcile.

Where to put the buffer? At a crusher-limited mine the queue at C01-Q is a buffer that protects the bottleneck. A policy that empties it — fewer ore trucks, or holding trucks at the shovels — saves truck-hours of non-productive time and loses ore whenever variability creates a gap. The handbook's position [R]: keep a small, visible queue at the bottleneck and size it from the seeded model, not from the deterministic one, which needs none.

Expected wait during an outage. Computing an expected wait from queue length × service time is correct only while the server works. During a stoppage the honest display is "held — server not ready since 10:00", with the plant's estimate if it has given one (REQ-10-02-006).

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

Walk the C01 timeline of section 6.2 and ask which signal must exist:

  • T+0 s — the plant's C01_STATUS NOT_READY with reason CHUTE_BLOCKED and since_s (ICD-C01-STATUS, ch-02-06). Without since_s no display can show how long the server has been down.
  • T+5 s — AMS reservation withheld for the tipping bay, with reason. From now on the AMS reports the truck already at C01-Q as held, not queued; the FMS closes its DLY-NP-02 and opens DLY-OD-10.
  • T+105 s onwards — ADS arrived at queue point C01-Q for each truck; the AMS increments the held count. The arrival times are what makes the queue grow at about one truck every 86 s, and they are the evidence for the decision at T+300 s.
  • T+300 s — FMS assignment changes; the AMS creates new missions for the reassigned trucks (ch-10-01, REQ-10-01-005).
  • T+2700 s — C01_STATUS READY; AMS grants the tipping bay in queue order and converts held trucks to queued. The FMS closes DLY-OD-10 and opens DLY-NP-02 at this second for every truck still waiting (ch-01-05, section 7.3).
  • T+2790 s to T+4410 s — tipping-complete events at 90 s intervals; the queue count falls; the expected wait becomes meaningful again.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

On the reference mine's queue display [H], per queue point (EX01-Q to EX04-Q, C01-Q, D01-Q):

  • trucks queued (server working) and trucks held (server not available), as two numbers;
  • the server's state, its age and, if given, the expected return;
  • the expected wait for the next arrival while the server works;
  • for loading units, idle minutes in the last hour and trucks en route; for C01, gaps between tips in the last hour.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — a growing queue at C01-Q

The steps follow the shared timeline of section 6.2.

  1. AHS Controller (T+5 s): check whether the trucks at C01-Q are held (C01 not ready) or queued (C01 working). If held, follow the stoppage procedure (ch-01-07, ch-10-07); if queued and C01 tips without gaps, the queue is the normal buffer.
  2. AHS Controller (T+60 s): ask the plant for an expected return; note it on the event.
  3. AHS Controller (T+300 s): review the FMS proposal with the Pit Supervisor. Options and their effect for a 45 min stoppage (section 12, example 5): hold — 6380 t of ore lost; reassign the empty ore trucks to waste — 6820 t of ore lost and 1100 t of waste gained; rebalancing ore trucks between EX01 and EX02 — no effect while C01 is down.
  4. AHS Controller (T+1545 s, hold branch): when the held count equals the number of ore trucks, tell both Shovel Operators that their shovels are starved by the crusher, not by dispatch.
  5. AHS Controller (T+2700 s): confirm that the display has turned held trucks into queued ones and that tipping has resumed.
  6. AHS Controller (T+4140 s to T+4410 s): close the event when the queue is back to its normal level and the last held truck has tipped; confirm DLY-OD-10 for the held intervals and DLY-NP-02 for the drain.
  7. Pit Supervisor (shift meeting): present the queue time by place and code, and one improvement (for example, a plant estimate of restart time at T+60 s on every stoppage).

Waiting Where Code Category Owner of the cause
Queue for a working shovel EX01-Q to EX04-Q DLY-NP-01 non-productive allocation, dispatch
Queue for a working C01 or D01 C01-Q, D01-Q DLY-NP-02 non-productive crusher capacity, allocation

Over a shift, the attribution question is always the same: was the server working (non-productive time) or not (operating delay)? The answer comes from the AMS's hold events, not from the place.

8.5 Training and competency implications

AHS Controllers must read a queue display as a statement about the bottleneck: a queue at a working crusher on a normal day is a buffer, a held line is a stoppage, an empty display with idle shovels is a haulage limit. They must know that the queued and held counts are different numbers with different codes. Pit Supervisors must expect the crusher queue to disappear during an R05 closure and to return when R05 reopens. Plant engineers should be shown section 6.1: the queue protects their crusher. Shovel Operators should be told when their shovel is starved because of the crusher, so that they do not ask for trucks that would only queue.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-10-02-001 [H] For each queue point the AMS shall publish, at every change and at a configurable interval, the number of trucks queued for a working server and the number held for a server that is not available, as separate values with a timestamp, together with the server's state, cause and since-time. Rationale: queued and held trucks have different causes, codes and responses (sections 4.8, 7.3). Verification: inspection of the queue-state message; test (LAB-10-02-01 reads both counts from the coded events of the C01 timeline).

REQ-10-02-002 [H] FMS queue displays and shift reports shall distinguish waiting at a working server (DLY-NP-01, DLY-NP-02) from waiting for a server out of service (DLY-OD-10, DLY-OD-11), using the AMS's hold events and not the truck's location. Rationale: the same line at C01-Q is a buffer on a normal day and an operating delay during a stoppage (sections 6.1, 6.2). Verification: test (SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN coded by LAB-10-02-01: 10.25 truck-hours DLY-OD-10); inspection.

REQ-10-02-003 [H] A planning or dispatch tool that estimates queue time or output for loading units sharing a destination shall model the shared destination, and shall state its model (deterministic, analytic with stated service distribution, or simulation with stated coefficients of variation and seeds) with every estimate. Rationale: a single loop places the queue at the wrong server, and the deterministic model shows no waiting below the knee (sections 4.3, 4.4). Verification: analysis (LAB-10-02-01 compares the models); inspection.

REQ-10-02-004 [H] Every reported queue statistic shall state the model or data source, the population, the period and whether the start of the shift (warm-up) is included; queue statistics on different bases shall not be compared. Rationale: start-up treatment alone changes loading-unit queueing by several truck-hours per shift (section 4.7). Verification: inspection; test (LAB-10-02-01 reproduces both deterministic models within 0.03 truck-hours after the first hour).

REQ-10-02-005 [H] The controller HMI shall show, side by side, each loading unit's idle time and trucks en route and each destination's queue against its normal level, so that a starved loading unit and a congested destination can be told apart. Rationale: a starved shovel behind a saturated crusher calls for no extra trucks (sections 6.3, 8.1). Verification: demonstration with the R05-open and R05-closed cases of LAB-10-02-02.

REQ-10-02-006 [H] While a server is not available, the FMS shall not display or use an expected wait derived from that server's normal service time; it shall show the hold and the source's expected return, if one has been given. Rationale: an expected wait computed as if C01 were tipping understates the wait by the length of the stoppage (section 4.8). Verification: test (LAB-10-02-01 expected_wait_s with C01 NOT_READY); inspection.

10. Verification and validation

Verification — all run by make test CH=ch-10-02:

  • the exact MVA reduces to hand results for one and two trucks and its two-class form reduces to the single-class form;
  • the network bound reproduces ahs_sim.queueing.deterministic_loop for a single loop;
  • throughput is ordered deterministic ≥ seeded gamma ≥ exponential for the baseline ore system;

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • One loop per shovel. Shared destinations are where the queue goes.
  • Exponential service as a forecast. MVA with CV = 1 is a pessimistic bound, not a model of a shovel.
  • Deterministic buffer sizing. It says no buffer is needed; variability says otherwise.
  • One count for queued and held trucks. It hides every crusher stoppage.
  • An expected wait during an outage. It is wrong by the length of the outage.
  • Comparing whole-shift and steady-state numbers. Start-up alone changes queue hours by several truck-hours.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Treating the normal C01 queue as a fault. It keeps the crusher fed; removing it costs ore when cycles vary.
  • Adding trucks to a shovel that waits because the crusher holds them. They will join the line at C01-Q.
  • Coding by place. A truck at C01-Q is DLY-NP-02 or DLY-OD-10 depending on C01's state, not on where it stands.
  • Expecting the crusher queue during an R05 closure. It disappears; idle shovels are the symptom of a haulage limit.
  • Forgetting the drain. The disturbance lasts until the queue is back to normal (11:09 here), not until the crusher restarts.

12. Worked examples

All values [A], computed by LAB-10-02-01 and LAB-10-02-02 with ahs_sim 0.7.0 on mine version 0.3.0.

Example 1 — waiting at a saturated crusher. 19 ore trucks share one bay of 90 s: a cycle every 19 × 90 = 1710 s per truck. EX02's free cycle is 1564.1 s, so each EX02 truck waits 145.9 s per cycle; the fleet model gives 145.6 s.

Example 2 — Little's law at C01-Q. EX01's trucks arrive 21.0 times per hour and wait 111.3 s, EX02's 19.0 times and wait 145.6 s: L = 21.0 / 3600 × 111.3 + 19.0 / 3600 × 145.6 = 0.65 + 0.77 = 1.42 trucks, the time-average count of the fleet model.

Example 3 — the knee with R05 open and closed. 1564.1 / 90 = 17.4 trucks; 1800.1 / 90 = 20.0 trucks. With 19 ore trucks the system is crusher-limited with R05 open and truck-limited with R05 closed.

Example 4 — the cost of variability. Seeded C01 busy fraction 0.967: 3.3 % of 8800 t/h is 288 t/h; over the eleven steady-state hours that is about 3200 t.

Example 5 — a growing queue at C01-Q, 45 minutes. C01 accepts 220 t every 90 s, 146.7 t per minute; 45 minutes is about 6600 t before any decision. Hold: 6380 t of ore lost (fleet model). Reassign the four empty ore trucks to waste: 6820 t of ore lost, 1100 t of waste gained, 5720 t net (ch-01-07). Rebalancing ore trucks between EX01 and EX02: no ore while C01 is down.

Example 6 — the drain. At the restart 19 trucks wait; C01 serves one every 90 s: 19 × 90 = 1710 s, so the last held truck tips at T+2700 + 1710 = T+4410 s (11:13:30). With the reassignment, 15 trucks: 1350 s, T+4050 s (ch-01-07).

Example 7 — one tip point at D01 (what-if). 3600 / 85 = 42.35 loads per hour against two waste shovels at 2 × 3600 / 167 = 43.11: one tip point would be the bottleneck, but only beyond about 22 trucks.

15. Key takeaways

  • The reference mine is a closed queueing network: arrivals at each server are produced by the others, and the queue collects at the server that is smallest relative to the trucks that share it.
  • With R05 open that server is C01: each EX02 truck waits about 146 s per cycle at C01-Q, and none at EX02-Q, in the deterministic model. The single-loop view of ch-01-05 puts the queue at the wrong server.
  • Deterministic ≥ seeded ≥ exponential: the deterministic model needs no buffer, the seeded model loses 3.3 % of the crusher's time to gaps, MVA is a pessimistic bound.
  • The match factor of one shovel cannot predict waiting when loops share a destination; plan trucks per bottleneck.
  • One tipping bay makes C01 the bottleneck; three tip points keep D01 out of the way.
  • With R05 closed the knee moves from 17.4 to 20.0 trucks, the crusher queue disappears and the bottleneck shifts to haulage.
  • Queue levels differ between models because of start-up and variability; after the first hour the two deterministic models agree. Always state the basis.
  • Queued and held are different counts with different codes; the AMS must publish both.
