1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

At 10:00 on a DAY shift shovel EX02 stops loading and walks to a new face. The relocation plan says the new loading area will be active at 11:00. The FMS runs dynamic dispatch: every ore truck that tips at C01 is sent to the loading unit where it is expected to start loading first. Seventy-one seconds later the first of EX02's trucks is sent to EX01 instead, and over the hour eight of them are redirected fourteen times. At 10:59 seven trucks queue at EX01-Q and three are still parked at EX02-Q.

At the shift meeting the Pit Supervisor compares the hour with the same move a month earlier, when the FMS still used fixed assignment and EX02's nine trucks simply parked at EX02-Q. Ore lost then: 3740 t. Ore lost now: 3960 t. The operating delay of the trucks fell from 6.99 to 2.77 truck-hours, so the KPI summary looks better. The tonnes do not. Three questions follow:

  • Why did sending EX02's trucks to EX01 save no ore?
  • Could any dispatch policy have saved ore during the move, and what would it have had to do?
  • Next week Survey closes the main ramp R05 for rework. Will the same policy be right then?

The answers depend less on the cleverness of the dispatch rule than on where the bottleneck of the system is at that moment, and on whether the FMS knows the current state of the loading units, the destinations and the roads. This chapter covers:

  • dispatch as a decision problem: decision epoch, objective, constraints and information;
  • fixed assignment and dynamic dispatch in ahs_sim.fms, and what each changes — and does not change — at the reference mine;
  • the FMS → AMS assignment interface for M-20481: what the FMS decides and what it must leave to the AMS;
  • dispatch with R05 closed and the pit served only over the west ramp R24;
  • the inputs dispatch depends on, and what the FMS should do when one of them is stale.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Formulate truck dispatch at the reference mine as a decision problem (decision epoch, objective, constraints) over 40 trucks, loading units EX01–EX04 and destinations C01 and D01.
  2. Compare fixed-assignment and dynamic dispatch policies in ahs_sim.fms, quantifying shovel idle time, truck queue time and tonnage per shift for each.
  3. Specify the FMS → AMS assignment interface for M-20481, stating which fields the FMS owns and which traffic decisions it must leave to the AMS.
  4. Calculate the change in cycle time and trucks needed per loading unit when R05 is closed and ore from EX02 is rerouted over R09, R24, R25 and R12 (loaded leg of M-20481 about 4.50 km → about 5.87 km), and show how the dispatch policy should respond.
  5. Analyse which dispatch inputs (truck availability, loading-unit state, destination readiness, road closures) must be kept current, and specify what the FMS does when an input is stale.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Explain on the controller HMI why DT17 was dispatched to EX02 rather than EX01, using the queue and cycle-time indicators the policy relied on.
  2. Decide when to override a dispatch recommendation (for example EX02 unavailable or C01 not ready) and record the override with the correct delay code.
  3. Brief a Pit Supervisor on how a change of dispatch policy shifts shift tonnage between ore to C01 and waste to D01.
  4. Rebalance assignments when R05 is closed and the pit is served only over the west ramp, and estimate the shift tonnage shortfall for the Pit Supervisor.
  5. Verify at shift start that the FMS reflects the actual state of loading units, destinations and road closures before its assignments are accepted.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Dispatch is the FMS function that controllers and supervisors watch most and understand least. It is visible every minute — a truck is sent here rather than there — and it is judged by what people see: a shovel standing without a truck, a line of trucks at a queue point, a controller approving one proposal after another. A dispatch policy that removes the visible symptom feels better even when the shift produces exactly the same tonnes.

The reference mine makes this concrete. Volume 01 established three facts that every dispatch decision runs into:

  • The ore system is limited by crusher C01, not by the shovels (ch-01-05, section 4.8). The 19 ore trucks of the DAY-shift baseline could bring 43.6 loads per hour to C01 if nothing ever waited; C01's single tipping bay accepts 40.0. Ore trucks therefore queue at C01-Q even when every machine is healthy — 16.4 truck-hours per shift in the fleet model of ch-01-07.
  • The waste system has spare shovel capacity. EX03 and EX04 are under-trucked; three more trucks on each would keep both busy (ch-01-07, section 4.4).
  • Closing R05 changes everything at once. The loaded leg of M-20481 grows from 4.50 km to 5.87 km and its cycle from 1564.1 s to 1800.1 s, while the waste cycles get shorter (ch-01-07, section 4.7).

Each fact moves the place where an extra truck, or a smarter choice of shovel, can create tonnes. A policy tuned for one of them can be useless or harmful in another. The questions this chapter answers are therefore not "which policy is best?" but "what does a policy change at this mine, in this state, and how do we know before we switch it on?"

3. Terminology and foundations

Dispatch is the FMS decision that sends a truck to its next task: a loading unit, and with it a destination. The result is an assignment (ICD-FMS-AMS, ch-02-06: truck, loading unit, destination, material), which the AMS turns into a mission. A new assignment given to a truck that already has one is a reassignment (ch-01-07).

Dispatch policy [H]. The rule the FMS applies at each decision. This chapter uses three:

  • Fixed assignment. Each truck belongs to one loading unit for the shift (the production plan's allocation) and always returns there. The allocation in equipment.yaml is EX01 DT01–DT10, EX02 DT11–DT19, EX03 DT20–DT28 and EX04 DT30–DT38 [A].
  • Dynamic dispatch. At each decision the truck goes to the available, reachable loading unit of its material where it is expected to start loading earliest (ahs_sim.fms.dynamic_policy, a greedy rule [H]).
  • Look-ahead dispatch [H]. Dynamic dispatch that asks whether a unit will be available when the truck arrives, not now; it needs the planned end of an outage as an input (lab-local in LAB-10-01-02).

Decision epoch [H]. The moment at which the policy is applied. In ahs_sim.fms it is the end of tipping: a truck that is already travelling keeps its destination. Real FMSs also decide at shift start, after an exception, or continuously with mid-leg reassignment; mid-leg changes need the AMS's cancellation protocol (ch-10-08).

Production plan [H]. The shift's targets by material (ore to C01, waste to D01) and the allocation of trucks to loading units that the plan assumes. The plan is the Pit Supervisor's; dispatch executes it.

Bottleneck. The resource that limits the throughput of a circuit (ch-01-05, ch-01-07). Here it is always one of three things: the trucks themselves (haulage), a loading unit, or a destination.

Stale input. A dispatch input whose age exceeds what the FMS was designed for, so that its value may no longer describe the machine or road it refers to. Staleness is a property of the data, not of the machine: a stale "EX02 available" may be true or false.

Waiting codes (delay-codes.yml, GMG Time Usage Model): a truck waiting at a working loading unit is DLY-NP-01, at a working destination DLY-NP-02 (non-productive time); waiting for a destination that is down is DLY-OD-10, for a loading unit that is not loading DLY-OD-11 (operating delay).

4. Technical explanation

4.1 Dispatch as a decision problem

FIG-10-01-01 shows the loop the FMS closes every few minutes: the plan sets targets and an allocation, the dispatch policy turns them into assignments, the AMS turns assignments into missions, the trucks produce cycle events, and the events update the inputs of the next decision.

Written as a decision problem [H]:

  • Decision epoch: a truck has finished tipping at C01 or D01 (ahs_sim.fms).
  • Decision: the next loading unit u for the truck. The destination follows from the material: C01 accepts ore, D01 waste (mine.yaml). In ahs_sim.fms a truck stays on its material; changing material is a plan change, not a dispatch decision (section 4.6).
  • Constraints: u is available (not relocating, not down), reachable on the open network, and digs the material of the truck's plan.
  • Information: for every unit, the empty travel time from the truck's destination, the time at which the shovel will be free, and the number of trucks already travelling to or waiting at it (FleetState).
  • Objective: the plan's tonnes by material over the shift. Waiting time, idle time and the number of moves are secondary measures; they matter only as far as they change tonnes or workload.

The dynamic policy replaces the shift objective with a local one: start loading as early as possible. With empty travel time te(d,u) from destination d, shovel free time Fu, pu trucks pending and shovel service time ts (spot plus load, 167 s), the expected loading start at unit u for a truck deciding at time t is

Su(t)=max(t+te(d,u), Fu+puts)

and the truck goes to the unit with the smallest Su, preferring its home unit on a tie. The rule is myopic: it does not ask what the choice does to the next truck, to the bottleneck or to the other material. That is its strength — it is simple, fast and explainable — and, as the rest of this section shows, its limit.

Optimisation-based dispatch (linear programming over flow rates, fleet-wide assignment) is the subject of ch-21-02. This chapter stays at policy level: what a rule does to a system whose bottleneck is known.

4.2 The fleet model behind the labs

ahs_sim.fms.simulate_fleet is a deterministic discrete-event model of the whole fleet [H]: the 37 producing trucks, four single-server loading units, C01 with one tipping bay and D01 with three tip points, and all travel and service times from ahs_sim.haul_cycle on the open — or restricted — road network. Loading units and destinations can be unavailable for windows. Every wait is recorded per truck with its delay code: DLY-NP-01, DLY-NP-02, DLY-OD-10 or DLY-OD-11. With a seed, service and travel times are gamma-distributed with coefficients of variation 0.15 and 0.10 [A], as in ch-01-05.

The model reproduces Volume 01. With fixed assignment it delivers 103 180 t of ore in the undisturbed DAY shift, as the fleet model of ch-01-07 (LAB-01-07-01), and with C01 down for 45 minutes from 10:00 it codes 10.25 truck-hours of DLY-OD-10 and loses 6380 t of ore, as ch-01-07's hold strategy. Waste differs by one load (88 000 t against 87 780 t) because the two models spread the trucks differently at shift start; ch-10-02 (section 4.7) explains why such start-up differences are expected.

Three simplifications matter for dispatch [H]: a truck already travelling keeps its destination; loading units do not move; and a truck stays on its material (ore trucks are never dispatched to a waste shovel). All three are discussed where they change a result.

4.3 Where is the bottleneck?

Before comparing policies, find the bottleneck. FIG-10-01-02 shows the material flow of the DAY-shift baseline with the capacity of every server.

A deterministic bound needs only the free cycles and service times [A]. With ni trucks on loading unit i, free cycle tc,i, shovel service ts, and a destination with P positions and service tD:

Qmax=min(∑i3600nitc,i, ∑i3600ts, 3600PtD)

in loads per hour, where the three terms are what the trucks could bring, what the loading units could load and what the destination could accept.

System Trucks could bring (loads/h) Loading units (loads/h) Destination (loads/h) Bottleneck
Ore, R05 open: EX01 10, EX02 9 → C01 43.6 43.1 40.0 C01
Ore, R05 closed 37.9 43.1 40.0 trucks
Waste, R05 open: EX03 9, EX04 9 → D01 34.1 43.1 127.1 trucks

With R05 open C01 is the bottleneck of the ore system: 3.6 loads per hour of truck capacity can only wait. Every ore truck therefore waits once per cycle, somewhere. With N ore trucks sharing one tipping bay that is busy all the time, each truck completes one cycle every NtD seconds, so the waiting per truck and cycle is

tw=NtD−tc=19×90−1564.1=145.9 s

for an EX02 truck — the "about 146 s" of ch-01-07. The waste system, in contrast, is truck-limited: its shovels idle because there are not enough trucks, and D01 is far from busy.

The consequence for dispatch is immediate. No ore dispatch policy can raise ore tonnes above 40 loads per hour while C01 is the bottleneck. It can only decide where the 145.9 s of waiting per cycle happen.

4.4 Fixed versus dynamic dispatch on the baseline shift

LAB-10-01-01 runs the DAY shift with three policies: fixed assignment, dynamic dispatch for all trucks, and dynamic dispatch for the ore trucks only (the waste trucks keep their home shovel). FIG-10-01-03 and the table show the result of the deterministic model.

Policy Ore (t) Waste (t) Ore trucks at EX01-Q/EX02-Q (truck-h) Ore trucks at C01-Q (truck-h) All ore waiting (truck-h) Waste waiting (truck-h) Shovel idle EX01 / EX02 / EX03 / EX04 (h) Moves
Fixed 103 180 88 000 4.34 16.55 20.89 1.88 0.28 / 1.41 / 2.52 / 2.40 0
Dynamic 103 180 87 780 7.07 13.88 20.95 3.28 0.85 / 0.84 / 4.60 / 0.42 449
Dynamic, ore trucks only 103 180 88 000 7.07 13.88 20.95 1.88 0.85 / 0.84 / 2.52 / 2.40 234

"Moves" counts decisions that sent a truck away from its home unit. Four results follow.

  1. Ore tonnes do not move. All three policies deliver exactly 103 180 t of ore: C01 tips 40 loads per hour once the shift has settled, whatever the trucks do before they reach it.
  2. Ore waiting does not shrink; it moves. Dynamic dispatch cuts the queue at C01-Q by 2.67 truck-hours and adds 2.73 truck-hours at the ore shovels. The total, 20.89 against 20.95 truck-hours, is conserved within 0.1 truck-hour, as the bound of section 4.3 predicts. The policy balances the two ore shovels (idle 0.28 h and 1.41 h become 0.85 h and 0.84 h) because it sends each truck to the shovel with the shorter line, but a balanced shovel is not a faster crusher.
  3. On waste, the greedy rule is short-sighted. EX04 is 15.6 s closer to D01 on the empty leg than EX03 (647.6 s against 663.2 s), so trucks that can start a few seconds earlier at EX04 go there. EX04's idle time falls to 0.42 h, EX03's rises to 4.60 h, waste waiting rises from 1.88 to 3.28 truck-hours and waste tonnage falls by one load. The rule optimises each truck's next start, not the circuit.
  4. Moves cost attention. 449 moves in twelve hours are 37 per hour. If every reassignment needed approval, dynamic dispatch would occupy the AHS Controller for no tonnes; even as automatic moves each one is a new mission and a new set of reservations for the AMS.

This verifies — in a stronger form — the expectation stated in Volume 01 that dispatch improvements on the ore shovels would cut waiting without adding ore tonnes: in the deterministic model they do not even cut waiting. The KPI picture still changes. Queues at C01-Q (DLY-NP-02) and at the ore shovels (DLY-NP-01) are both non-productive time, so production effectiveness of the ore trucks is unchanged; what changes is where the controller sees trucks waiting, and which shovel the Shovel Operator sees idle.

4.5 With variability, the bottleneck has to be protected

Real cycles vary. LAB-10-01-01 repeats the comparison with gamma-distributed service and travel times for 20 seeds, pairing the policies seed by seed [A]:

Seeded, 20 runs Fixed Dynamic
Ore, mean (t) 99 902 100 848
Waste, mean (t) 85 118 84 436
Ore trucks waiting, mean (truck-h) 27.47 25.62
Ore trucks at C01-Q, mean (truck-h) 14.49 15.94
Ore gain of dynamic over fixed, same seed (t) — mean 946, min 220, max 1760; positive in 20 of 20

Two things change. First, both policies lose 2 to 3 % of ore against the deterministic shift: variability sometimes leaves C01 without a truck to tip, and a minute lost at the bottleneck is lost for the shift. Second, dynamic dispatch now gains ore in every one of the 20 runs — on average 946 t, about four loads. It gains by keeping the queue at C01-Q fuller (15.94 against 14.49 truck-hours): a truck that would have waited behind a slow loading at EX01 is sent to EX02, arrives at C01 sooner, and fills a gap that would otherwise have starved the crusher.

The principle is general: when the bottleneck is fixed, dispatch helps only by keeping the bottleneck busy. In a deterministic world C01 is never starved after the first hour, so there is nothing to gain. In a variable world the gain is real but bounded by the bottleneck's lost time; it can never exceed the deterministic 103 180 t. The waste result (−682 t on average) repeats the short-sightedness of section 4.4.

4.6 Loss of a loading unit: EX02 relocates

SCN-SHOVEL-EX02-MOVE takes EX02 out for one hour: relocating at T+0 s, new loading area active at T+3600 s (scenario registry). LAB-10-01-02 places T+0 at 10:00 [A] and compares three policies, each against its own undisturbed shift.

Policy (EX02 out 10:00–11:00) Ore lost (t) Extra DLY-NP-01 (truck-h) Extra DLY-NP-02 (truck-h) DLY-OD-11 (truck-h) Extra waiting, total (truck-h)
Fixed 3740 +2.12 −1.73 6.99 7.38
Dynamic 3960 +3.67 +0.97 2.77 7.41
Look-ahead 3960 +6.25 +1.16 0 7.41

While EX02 relocates the ore bottleneck moves from C01 to EX01: one shovel can load only 21.6 trucks per hour, against C01's 40. Sending more trucks to EX01 cannot make EX01 load faster. The ore lost is therefore the same for every policy within one load (220 t), and so is the total extra waiting. The policies differ only in the code of the waiting:

  • Fixed: EX02's trucks park at EX02-Q for the hour — operating delay, DLY-OD-11.
  • Dynamic: trucks that tip after 10:00 go to EX01 and queue there — non-productive time, DLY-NP-01. Three trucks that were already travelling empty to EX02 at 10:00 cannot be redirected in the model and park for the hour (2.77 truck-hours of DLY-OD-11).
  • Look-ahead: trucks are sent to EX02 only if they will arrive after 11:00, so no truck parks at all.

Operating efficiency (working ÷ operating time) is highest for look-ahead and lowest for fixed; production effectiveness and tonnes are the same. This is ch-01-05's warning (section 6.3) seen in a fleet model: a KPI that improves when trucks are moved from parking into a queue is not a production KPI on its own.

The outage sweep confirms the pattern:

EX02 out (min) Ore lost, fixed (t) Ore lost, dynamic (t) DLY-OD-11, fixed (truck-h) DLY-OD-11, dynamic (truck-h)
15 880 880 0.68 0.52
30 1760 1760 2.49 1.27
60 3740 3960 6.99 2.77
120 7920 7920 15.99 5.77

What would have saved tonnes? Not a different ore policy, but a different material. ch-01-05 (section 6.3) computed with deterministic loops that filling EX03 and EX04 with EX02's trucks during the hour moves 1978 t more waste than parking them [A]. That is a reassignment across materials — a change of the hour's plan that the Pit Supervisor approves — and ahs_sim.fms deliberately does not make it automatically.

4.7 Dispatch inputs and stale state

Every term of Su(t) is an input with an age. The inputs a dispatch decision depends on [H]:

Input Source If stale or missing
Truck availability and position AMS mission state, ADS reports A truck counted as available that is stopped, or not counted when it could work
Loading-unit state (loading, relocating, down; planned end) Shovel Operator via HMI or loading-unit terminal; AMS loading-area definition Trucks sent to a unit that cannot load, or kept away from one that can
Destination readiness (C01 READY / STOPPING / NOT_READY) Plant via ICD-C01-STATUS (ch-02-06) Ore trucks sent to a crusher that will hold them, or held from one that is ready
Road closures and restrictions AMS route network (Survey, AHS Controller) Cycle estimates and trucks needed computed on a route the trucks cannot drive
Cycle and service times FMS history of cycle events Wrong expected starts; a slow drift rather than a step

LAB-10-01-02 measures what a stale loading-unit state costs. The trucks and the shovel follow the truth; only the FMS's view of EX02 lags [A]:

FMS learns late that … Lag (s) Ore lost (t) DLY-OD-11 (truck-h) EX02 idle (h)
— (current view) 0 3960 2.77 0.74
EX02 went down 300 3740 4.40 0.71
EX02 went down 900 3960 6.47 0.72
EX02 went down 1800 5280 8.90 0.68
EX02 is back 300 4180 2.77 0.83
EX02 is back 900 4840 2.77 0.95
EX02 is back 1800 5940 2.77 1.21

The two directions behave differently. A stale "available" first costs waiting: trucks park at a shovel that is not loading, while the working shovel is the bottleneck anyway. Only when the lag is long enough for those parked trucks to starve EX01 (1800 s here) does it cost ore. A stale "down" costs ore at once: EX02 is back at 11:00 but has no trucks, and every 300 s of delay costs one to two loads. The return of a loading unit to service is the input dispatch can least afford to learn late. It should be an explicit, time-stamped event, not an inference from a missing alarm (REQ-10-01-003).

What should the FMS do with a stale input? It cannot stop the trucks — that is not its role, and the AMS already fails closed on every safety-relevant input (ch-02-06). Treating a stale "available" as unavailable would turn waiting into lost ore if the unit is in fact working; treating a stale "down" as available would park trucks. The handbook's rule is neither [H]: a stale input is shown as stale, the FMS makes no new reassignment that depends on it, existing assignments continue, and the AHS Controller confirms the state with its source — the Shovel Operator by radio, the plant, or Survey (REQ-10-01-004). The age limit that makes an input stale is a design parameter for each input [A]; this chapter recommends none.

4.8 R05 closed: the bottleneck moves to the trucks

SCN-MAP-R05 closes R05 for rework. The AMS routes every truck over the remaining network, and M-20481 climbs the bench ramp R09 to Intersection W, the west ramp R24 to crest P3, R25 to Intersection V and R12 to Intersection X (FIG-10-01-04).

LAB-10-01-03 recomputes the free cycles on the restricted network:

Loading unit Loaded route, R05 open → closed (km) Free cycle, open → closed (s) Trucks for MF = 1, open → closed
EX01 (ore) 4.52 → 5.88 1572.4 → 1808.4 9.42 → 10.83
EX02 (ore) 4.50 → 5.87 1564.1 → 1800.1 9.37 → 10.78
EX03 (waste) 6.55 → 6.99 1914.7 → 1875.3 11.47 → 11.23

The ore cycles grow by 236 s; the waste cycles shrink by 39 s although their loaded routes grow, because bench traffic no longer descends R09 to climb R05 (ch-01-07, section 4.7). With the baseline allocation the ore trucks can now bring only 37.9 loads per hour, below C01's 40.0: the ore system changes from crusher-limited to truck-limited. C01 would be saturated again with 20.0 trucks on EX02's cycle (1800.1 / 90), one more than the 19 ore trucks of the plan.

The fleet model gives the consequences for a whole shift with R05 closed:

Policy and allocation, R05 closed Ore (t) Waste (t) Total (t) Ore trucks at C01-Q (truck-h)

Read with the bound of section 4.3:

  1. The closure costs 5940 t of ore and adds 2200 t of waste under fixed assignment, as ch-01-07 found for ore. The queue at C01-Q almost disappears (0.97 truck-hours): the trucks the crusher used to make wait are now spending that time on the west ramp.
  2. Dynamic dispatch does not help. It loses two more loads of ore and three of waste. In a truck-limited system there is no queue to balance; sending a truck to the other ore shovel only changes its empty route.
  3. One more ore truck helps a lot; a second hardly. Moving one truck from EX03 to EX02 recovers 5060 t of the 5940 t ore loss for 4840 t of waste. The second truck adds only 660 t of ore — C01 is saturated again — and costs 5060 t of waste. With R05 open, moving trucks to ore adds no ore at all (103 180 t for zero to three trucks moved) and loses about 4840 t of waste per truck.

Whether to move the truck is a production-plan decision: it trades waste for ore at roughly one to one, and only the Pit Supervisor knows whether the crusher feed is worth more than the stripping that day [R]. Dispatch's job is to put the numbers in front of the decision — and to move the truck back when R05 reopens, because the same truck on ore with R05 open produces nothing but queue at C01-Q.

4.9 Trade-offs and model limits

  • Greedy is explainable, not optimal. Su(t) can be shown on an HMI in one line (section 8.1). It ignores the next truck, the bottleneck and the plan; at this mine it never added deterministic tonnes.
  • Decision epoch at tipping. Trucks already travelling keep their destination. A real FMS could redirect them, at the price of mission cancellations (ch-10-08) and more AMS load.
  • One material per truck. Cross-material reassignment is a plan decision here (section 4.6); ch-01-07 and ch-10-07 model it.
  • No road interaction. The model ignores intersections and passing. With R05 closed every truck passes Intersection V twice per cycle (ch-01-07); the reservations and waiting this creates are in ch-10-04.
  • Assumed variability. CVs 0.15 / 0.10 [A]; the 946 t gain of section 4.5 depends on them.
  • Whole loads. Results move in steps of 220 t; differences of one or two loads are within the model's resolution.

5. Responsibilities and interface boundaries

Element Responsibility in dispatch
FMS Owns the production plan's execution: dispatch policy, assignments, reassignment proposals, cycle-time estimates, the decision log; pre-codes waiting from system events
AMS Turns assignments into missions; computes routes on the network as it is (closures, restrictions); grants reservations; holds trucks at queue points; publishes mission, queue and reservation state; may reject an assignment
ADS Executes the mission within its granted path; reports cycle events, position and stop reasons; takes its own safety decisions, which no assignment can override
VCS Actuation; reports faults that remove a truck from dispatch
Independent safety functions Stop trucks regardless of FMS and AMS state (Volume 04); a stopped truck is not dispatchable until released
Onboard safety-related systems Fire, payload and tyre alarms can end a cycle and remove a truck from dispatch (ch-08-05)
People Pit Supervisor owns the plan and the allocation and is accountable for policy changes; AHS Controller approves proposals, overrides with a delay code, confirms stale inputs; Shovel Operator reports loading-unit state and its planned end; plant reports C01 state; Survey and the AHS Controller close and open roads

The FMS → AMS boundary is the boundary between what we want and what is safe and possible now (ch-01-04). For M-20481 the FMS sends an ASSIGNMENT request in ICD-FMS-AMS 1.0 (ch-02-06) [H]. The FMS owns its fields: assignment_id, truck_id (DT17), loading_unit (EX02), destination (C01) and material (ore). The AMS owns everything that follows: mission_id (M-20481), the route (R18, R08, R06, R05, R01, R02, R21 — or the west-ramp route when R05 is closed), the mission legs, the granted path and the reservations, the holding location, and the decision to reject the assignment (ROUTE_UNAVAILABLE, DESTINATION_NOT_READY, TRUCK_UNAVAILABLE). An FMS that specified a route, a speed or a reservation would be making traffic decisions without the information to make them safely; an FMS that silently re-sent a rejected assignment would be arguing with the AMS. ISO 23725:2024, Autonomous system and fleet management system interoperability (ISO/TC 82/SC 8, edition 1), defines interfaces between fleet management systems and autonomous haulage systems for dispatching haul trucks and coordinating production information in surface mining [F] (SRC-STD-ISO23725, catalogue level). The handbook's ICD is its own teaching interface and does not reproduce the standard's clauses.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — the baseline shift

With R05 open and every machine available, the ore circuit runs at C01's pace: 40 tips per hour, each ore truck waiting about 146 s per cycle (section 4.3). Fixed and dynamic dispatch deliver the same 103 180 t; the policy decides whether the Shovel Operator at EX02 or the AHS Controller watching C01-Q sees the waiting. Normal operation is also the moment to check the dispatch inputs (section 8.3, step 1): a policy fed with a wrong loading-unit state is wrong all shift.

6.4 Other states in brief

SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN: while C01 is not ready, no ore dispatch policy produces ore; the fleet model's hold case loses 6380 t in 45 minutes, as ch-01-07. The useful decision is across materials (ch-01-07, ch-10-07). A truck stopped by a remote stop, a fire alarm or a fault leaves dispatch until it is released by the role that owns that state; dispatch never releases it.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Local rule or system objective? The greedy rule optimises each truck's next start. It is easy to implement, to test and to explain, and at a crusher-limited mine it adds no deterministic tonnes. A policy that optimises the plan's tonnes needs a model of the bottleneck and of the flows (ch-21-02). The handbook's position [R]: start with an explainable rule, and judge every policy change by tonnes by material in a fleet model with shared destinations, never by waiting alone (REQ-10-01-002).

When to decide. Deciding at tipping is simple and needs no cancellation. Deciding mid-leg could have redirected the three trucks that parked at EX02 in section 6.2, but each redirect is a cancellation and a new mission (ch-10-03, ch-10-08). The gain must be weighed against AMS load and the chance of a cancellation that does not complete.

Stability. Dynamic dispatch made 449 moves in the baseline shift for no ore. A real implementation adds hysteresis — move only if the expected start improves by more than a margin [A] — or limits moves per truck. Neither changes tonnes here; both reduce the controller's and the AMS's load.

Which inputs to trust. The look-ahead policy needs the planned end of an outage; the stale-input study shows the return to service is worth more than any policy refinement. Designing the loading-unit and destination state messages with timestamps and planned ends (ch-02-06's since_s and stop_at_s pattern) is the cheapest dispatch improvement.

Material as a boundary. The automatic policy keeps trucks on their material. Crossing it changes the plan's ore/waste split and the crusher feed, which is the Pit Supervisor's decision (ch-01-07, REQ-01-07-003 for loaded trucks).

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

Walk the timeline of section 6.2 and ask which message must exist for dispatch to be right:

  • T+0 s — loading-unit state for EX02: relocating, with since 10:00 and planned end 11:00, from the Shovel Operator's terminal or the FMS's own plan. Without the planned end, look-ahead is impossible; without the state, trucks park.
  • T+34 s — ADS arrived at queue point EX02-Q for DT16 while EX02 is relocating: the FMS pre-codes DLY-OD-11, not DLY-NP-01, because the cause is a loading unit that is not loading.
  • T+71 s — FMS → AMS ASSIGNMENT for DT19 to EX01, and the AMS's ASSIGNMENT_RESPONSE. Until the response the assignment is pending; the FMS must not count DT19 as pending at EX01 twice if it re-sends.
  • T+0 s to T+3600 s — trucks already travelling to EX02 at T+0 keep their destination. Redirecting them would need a CANCEL with disposition HOLD and a new assignment (ch-02-06, ch-10-08).
  • T+3600 s — AMS loading-area definition activated (registered event) and loading-unit state available. The stale-input study prices this message: each 300 s it arrives late costs one to two loads.
  • T+3724 s — the first dynamic assignment to EX02. With look-ahead it would have been sent before 11:00, timed to arrive after it.

Every message needs a producer, a timestamp from a synchronised clock (ch-02-07) and the asset ID. Second-level accuracy is enough for dispatch; the age of each input matters more than its precision.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

On the reference mine's dispatch board [H]:

  • Loading units — state (loading, relocating, down) with its age and planned end; trucks assigned, travelling, queued; shovel idle minutes this hour.
  • Destinations — C01 state from the plant (READY, STOPPING, NOT_READY) with age; trucks queued and held at C01-Q and D01-Q.
  • Recommendations — each proposed move with its reason. For DT17 at 07:32:41 the board shows: EX02 — expected loading start in 503 s (on arrival; 2 trucks ahead, shovel free in 117 s). EX01 — 635 s (3 trucks ahead, shovel free in 134 s, empty travel 507 s). The controller can see that the choice of EX02 rests on one fewer truck in its line, not on a shorter road.
  • Stale inputs — a banner per input older than its limit, naming the source to call.
  • Shift totals — tonnes by material against plan, ore and waste trucks' waiting by place and code, idle time per shovel.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — dispatch during an EX02 relocation

The steps follow the shared timeline of section 6.2.

  1. AHS Controller (shift start): check on the dispatch board that every loading unit's state, C01's state and every road closure match the shift plan and the handover; confirm any input marked stale with its source before accepting the FMS's assignments.
  2. Shovel Operator (T+0 s): report EX02 relocating with the planned end (11:00) on the terminal; confirm by radio.
  3. AHS Controller (T+0 s to T+60 s): confirm that EX02 shows relocating with a planned end and that trucks arriving at EX02-Q are pre-coded DLY-OD-11.
  4. AHS Controller (T+300 s): decide with the Pit Supervisor whether the ore policy simply continues (no ore can be gained while EX01 is the bottleneck) or EX02's trucks move to waste for the hour (a plan change). Record the decision.
  5. AHS Controller (during the hour): if overriding a recommendation — for example holding trucks at EX02-Q rather than adding to the line at EX01-Q — record the override with its reason; the waiting stays DLY-OD-11.
  6. Shovel Operator (T+3600 s): report EX02 available at once; the controller confirms the board shows it within the minute.
  7. AHS Controller (T+3600 s onwards): check that trucks are being dispatched to EX02 and that its idle time is not growing; close the event when the first EX02 load has tipped (T+4662 s).

For an R05 closure (SCN-MAP-R05) the same roles apply: the Pit Supervisor decides on moving one waste truck to ore when the closure starts, and the AHS Controller restores the baseline allocation at T+2400 s, when R05 reopens, and checks at T+2700 s that no truck is still routed over R24.

8.5 Training and competency implications

AHS Controllers must be able to read a recommendation's reason and say in one sentence why the FMS chose a unit, and must know that a balanced line of trucks is not the same as more ore. They should recognise the two bottleneck states of the reference mine — crusher-limited with R05 open, truck-limited with R05 closed — and what each implies for an extra truck. Pit Supervisors need the habit of asking "tonnes by material, against which baseline?" before accepting a policy change. Shovel Operators must know that reporting the return of their shovel promptly is worth one to two loads per five minutes. LAB-10-01-02's stale-input runs suit a controller training session.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-10-01-001 [H] The FMS shall record every dispatch decision with the truck, the time, the policy, the chosen loading unit, the expected loading start at each candidate unit and the value and age of every input used. Rationale: a decision that cannot be explained cannot be checked by the controller or analysed afterwards (sections 4.7, 8.1). Verification: inspection of the decision log; test (LAB-10-01-01 records DT17's candidates and choice).

REQ-10-01-002 [H] A change of dispatch policy shall be evaluated before use by its effect on tonnes by material over a shift, computed with a fleet model in which loading units share destinations, and the evaluation shall report waiting by place and delay code and idle time per loading unit alongside the tonnes. Rationale: at a crusher-limited mine a policy can move waiting between places and codes without changing tonnes (sections 4.4, 4.6). Verification: analysis (LAB-10-01-01, LAB-10-01-02).

REQ-10-01-003 [H] The state of each loading unit shall be reported to the FMS with the time it was entered and, for planned unavailability, the planned end; the return of a loading unit to service shall be reported as an explicit, time-stamped event. Rationale: a late return costs ore directly, one to two loads per 300 s at the reference mine (section 4.7). Verification: test (LAB-10-01-02 stale-input cases); inspection of the loading-unit state message.

REQ-10-01-004 [H] The FMS shall mark any dispatch input older than its configurable age limit as stale on the controller HMI, shall make no new reassignment that depends on a stale input until the AHS Controller has confirmed it, and shall keep existing assignments meanwhile. Rationale: treating a stale input as either available or unavailable can cost waiting or ore; confirmation with the source resolves it (section 4.7). Verification: test (stale-view policy, LAB-10-01-02); demonstration.

REQ-10-01-005 [H] An FMS assignment shall specify only the assignment ID, truck, loading unit, destination and material. Routes, granted paths, reservations, speeds and holding locations shall remain AMS decisions, and an assignment rejected by the AMS shall not be re-sent unchanged. Rationale: traffic decisions need information only the AMS has; the FMS–AMS boundary separates what is wanted from what is safe and possible (section 5). Verification: inspection of ICD-FMS-AMS; test with an assignment over a closed route.

REQ-10-01-006 [H] When a road segment is closed or reopened, the FMS shall recompute cycle times and trucks needed per loading unit from the AMS's routes on the current network before proposing an allocation, and shall state with the proposal whether the trucks, a loading unit or a destination limits each circuit. Rationale: closing R05 changes the ore system from crusher-limited to truck-limited, which reverses the value of an extra ore truck (section 4.8). Verification: test (LAB-10-01-03 route table and rebalancing); analysis.

REQ-10-01-007 [H] Every override of a dispatch recommendation by the AHS Controller shall be recorded with the person, time, reason and the delay code that applies to the resulting waiting. Rationale: overrides change where trucks wait and must remain visible to delay analysis (sections 4.6, 8.3). Verification: inspection; demonstration.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Optimising the visible symptom. A policy that balances queues or eliminates parking can leave tonnes unchanged.
  • Evaluating a policy on a single loop. Shared destinations decide where waiting goes; a loop with a private crusher hides the bottleneck.
  • Inferring a return to service. "No alarm" is not "available"; the return must be reported.
  • Treating stale as true. Either default — available or unavailable — has a cost; show it as stale and confirm.
  • Letting the FMS decide traffic. Routes, reservations and speeds belong to the AMS.
  • No hysteresis. A greedy rule without a margin produces hundreds of moves for no tonnes.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Adding ore trucks when C01 is the bottleneck. With R05 open a fourth truck moved to ore adds no ore and costs about 4840 t of waste per shift.
  • Leaving the extra truck on ore after R05 reopens. The rebalancing for the closure must be undone.
  • Reporting a policy success by operating efficiency. Moving trucks from parking to a queue improves the KPI, not the tonnes.
  • Forgetting to report EX02 back. Every five minutes costs one to two loads.
  • Overriding by radio only. An override that is not recorded makes the delay analysis wrong.

12. Worked examples

All values [A], computed by LAB-10-01-01 to LAB-10-01-03 with ahs_sim 0.7.0 on mine version 0.3.0.

Example 1 — the bottleneck bound. Ore trucks could bring 10 × 3600 / 1572.4 + 9 × 3600 / 1564.1 = 22.9 + 20.7 = 43.6 loads per hour; the ore shovels load 2 × 3600 / 167 = 43.1; C01 accepts 3600 / 90 = 40.0. C01 limits.

Example 2 — waiting per cycle at a saturated crusher. 19 ore trucks share the tipping bay: one cycle per truck every 19 × 90 = 1710 s, so an EX02 truck waits 1710 − 1564.1 = 145.9 s per cycle, wherever the policy puts it.

Example 3 — EX02 out for an hour. EX02 normally delivers 19 loads per hour in the fleet model; one hour without it is about 19 loads, 4180 t. The queue at C01-Q (1.42 trucks on average, ch-10-02) feeds C01 for the first minutes, so the fleet model loses 17 loads (3740 t) under fixed assignment and 18 (3960 t) under dynamic dispatch.

Example 4 — trucks to saturate C01 with R05 closed. 1800.1 / 90 = 20.0 trucks on EX02's cycle; the plan has 19. One more ore truck: +5060 t ore, −4840 t waste. A second: +660 t ore, −5060 t waste.

Example 5 — the price of a late return. EX02's return learned 900 s late: 4840 t of ore lost instead of 3960 t, four loads for fifteen minutes.

Example 6 — DT17's decision at 07:32:41. Empty travel from C01: EX01 507.3 s, EX02 503.1 s. EX01's shovel is free in 134.3 s with three trucks pending: 134.3 + 3 × 167 = 635.3 s. EX02's is free in 117.1 s with two pending: 117.1 + 2 × 167 = 451.1 s, earlier than DT17 can arrive, so the start is on arrival at 503.1 s. EX02 wins by 132 s.

15. Key takeaways

  • Dispatch decides where trucks wait; the bottleneck decides how much they produce. Find the bottleneck first.
  • With R05 open the ore system is crusher-limited: fixed, dynamic and ore-only dynamic dispatch all deliver 103 180 t of ore in the deterministic model, and total ore waiting is conserved — it only moves between C01-Q and the shovels.
  • With variability, dynamic dispatch gains about 946 t of ore per shift (all 20 seeds) by keeping C01 fed; it can never pass the deterministic ceiling.
  • While EX02 relocates, EX01 is the bottleneck: every policy loses the same ore; the policy changes only the delay code.
  • With R05 closed the ore system becomes truck-limited: one more ore truck recovers 5060 t of ore for 4840 t of waste; with R05 open the same truck adds nothing.
  • The return of a loading unit to service is the dispatch input least affordable to learn late.
  • The FMS assigns truck, loading unit, destination and material; routes, reservations and safety stay with the AMS and the vehicle.
