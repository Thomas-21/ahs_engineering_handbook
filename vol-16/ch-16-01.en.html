1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

On a Tuesday DAY shift the reference mine runs a simulator drill of SCN-FIRE-DT17: DT17, loaded on mission M-20481, reports a fire while climbing R05. The drill goes well by the clock. The controller acknowledges at T+15 s, the exclusion zone is in place, the ERT reaches the zone boundary at T+600 s. The review afterwards is less comfortable.

  • The pit was rerouted over the west ramp R24, but nobody can say who decided it. The controller remembers a radio call from the Pit Supervisor; the Pit Supervisor remembers saying "do what you need to".
  • The ERT leader asked the controller on the radio to "clear the zone, we're done here". The controller could have done it: the command was on the screen, one click away.
  • The night before, the incoming AHS Controller had found that the Survey account could deploy a map version and that the "maintenance" profile used by Field Technicians in CR1 could resume a truck.
  • The controller held every permission and answered every call. A second controller was called in, but nobody said which areas they should take.

None of this is a fault of FMS, AMS, ADS or VCS. Each is a question of organisation: which role decides, which role may press which button, how the system knows who pressed it, and what happens when the one person who holds everything is busy. ch-01-06 introduced roles, CR1 and the handover at overview level; this chapter goes down to the commands themselves:

  • the CR1 command catalogue and which role may issue each command to FMS, AMS, the vehicle or the independent safety function — and which command no role may issue from CR1;
  • deriving HMI and device rights from a RACI, and checking them (LAB-16-01-01);
  • logins, sessions and role switches, so that every safety-relevant command is traced to a person, role and time;
  • the span of control of one AHS Controller, and when the work is split or escalated;
  • how decision rights move from the controller to the Pit Supervisor and the ERT during SCN-FIRE-DT17;
  • the communication lines between CR1, the field, the MIA workshop and the ERT;
  • single points of failure in the organisation itself.

Workflow and task allocation inside the control room — what the controller does when, and how tasks are scheduled — belong to ch-17-07; this chapter defines roles and authority. Controller workload, alarms and the HMI are ch-16-10.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Map each control-room and field role at the reference mine to the FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS and independent safety-function commands it may issue, and identify commands that no role should be able to issue from CR1.
  2. Specify role-based HMI access rights and audit logging so that permission grants, remote stops and map deployments are restricted to authorised roles and attributable to a named user.
  3. Explain how CR1 logins, sessions and role switches are recorded so that every safety-relevant command can be traced to a person, role and time during a later investigation.
  4. Analyse single points of failure in the control-room organisation — one controller holding all active permissions, a single CR1 workstation, one radio channel — and propose system or staffing mitigations.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Construct a RACI matrix for CR1 covering dispatch changes, digital access permissions, remote stop, map deployment and emergency notification across the seven handbook roles.
  2. Describe the span of control of one AHS Controller across AA-North, AA-Central and AA-South, and state the conditions under which work is split between controllers or escalated to the Pit Supervisor.
  3. Distinguish the decisions taken by the AHS Controller, the Pit Supervisor and the ERT during SCN-FIRE-DT17, and explain where each hands over to the next.
  4. Explain the communication lines between CR1, the field (radio, field terminal) and the workshop at the MIA.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

An organisation chart says who reports to whom. A control room needs something more precise: for every command that changes what the autonomous fleet does, who may issue it, who answers for it, and how the system enforces and records that. At the reference mine three gaps between the paper organisation and the running system show up again and again [A].

The RACI and the HMI were written by different people. The RACI comes out of a workshop of supervisors and controllers; the HMI's user profiles come from commissioning, built for convenience during testing. Nobody compares the two, and when they differ the HMI wins, because the HMI is what executes. LAB-16-01-01 compares such as-delivered profiles [A] with the reference RACI of this chapter and finds ten findings in six of the seven roles (section 12, example 2).

Accountability without a button is invisible. Under the conventions of ch-01-06, the Pit Supervisor is accountable for imposing restrictions, choosing manual recovery, clearing exclusion zones and restarting areas, but the AHS Controller executes them in AMS. If the system records only the person who pressed the button, the Pit Supervisor's decision exists only in memory. The drill review of section 1.1 cannot find out who decided the reroute because nothing recorded it.

One controller is one person. The baseline staffing of CR1 is one AHS Controller per shift for all three autonomous areas, with a second workstation for a controller called in by the Pit Supervisor (ch-01-06) [A]. In normal operation this works because AMS does the driving and the controller handles events. In an emergency the same person must run the first response, keep every active permission under control, answer the field and watch AA-Central and AA-South. Whether one person is enough is a workload question (ch-16-10); which decisions and states are tied to that one person is this chapter's question.

SCN-FIRE-DT17 is the walkthrough for sections 6, 7 and 8, because in it decision rights move fastest: from the truck to AMS in ten seconds, to the Pit Supervisor within a quarter of a minute and to the ERT at the zone boundary.

3. Terminology and foundations

The terms of ch-01-06 — role, decision rights, RACI matrix, authorisation, role-based authorisation, least privilege, span of control, escalation, permission owner, open item — are used here without change. A short reminder of RACI: Responsible does the work, Accountable owns the outcome and is exactly one role, Consulted is asked before, Informed is told after. A role marked A/R is accountable and does the work itself.

Command. An instruction issued from CR1, or from a device carried by a role, that changes the state of FMS, AMS, a truck or the independent safety function. The command catalogue is the closed list of such commands that the site recognises [H]. In this chapter it is the catalogue of ahs_sim.control_room.COMMANDS plus clearing an exclusion zone (section 4.1).

Command right. The ability, granted to a user account or to the holder of a device, to issue a command — a menu item in the HMI or a remote-stop transmitter in someone's custody. It is not a decision right: the Pit Supervisor decides to clear an exclusion zone but, in the reference design, cannot issue the command.

Issuer and authoriser. The issuer is the person who executes a command. The authoriser is the person, in the accountable role, whose decision the command carries out. For many safety-relevant commands they are different people; both are recorded [H].

Session. The period during which one person works at one workstation or device under one role, from login to logout. A role switch — the same person changing to another role — ends one session and starts another [H]. Every command belongs to exactly one session.

Area scope. The autonomous areas a controller's session supervises. In the baseline the single session on duty covers AA-North, AA-Central and AA-South; when a second controller is called in, the areas are divided between two sessions (section 4.5).

Decision-rights handover. The point in an event at which the accountable role for the next decision changes, for example from the AHS Controller to the Pit Supervisor when an emergency is declared. Unlike a shift handover, it moves specific decisions, not everything.

Communication line. A defined channel between two parties with a stated purpose, such as the AHS radio channel, the telephone between CR1 and the MIA workshop, or the stop radio RSR (section 4.6).

Statutory role. A position that mining safety legislation requires a site to appoint, with duties set by law. The handbook roles are teaching conventions, not statutory roles (section 4.8).

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The roles and the command catalogue

The seven roles of the reference mine are those of mine.yaml v0.3.0: AHS Controller (AHS-CTRL), Pit Supervisor (PIT-SUP), Shovel Operator (SHV-OP), Field Technician (FIELD-TECH), Maintenance Planner (MAINT-PLN), Survey (SURVEY) and Emergency Response Team (ERT). ch-01-06 describes where each works, what it decides and to whom it escalates. Three further roles appear in the handbook where a topic needs them. They are not mine.yaml roles and hold no command in the catalogue; they are listed here so that the command RACI can stay to the seven [A]:

Role outside mine.yaml Where it appears Relation to CR1 commands
Planner [H] (glossary, ch-01-06) ch-01-06: mine plan and shift targets Informed of dispatch changes and production decisions; no AMS command
Safety-case owner [A] ch-04-10: accountable for the evidence register Informed when the RACI or the command rights change, because controls such as CTL-06 ("zone cleared only by an authorised role") rest on them
IT-security [A] Account and role administration; in ch-04-09 these duties sit with site AHS engineering Executes account and role changes on a recorded authorisation; issues no operational command

The command catalogue is the list of everything CR1 can make the fleet do. The reference catalogue has thirteen commands, of which one is forbidden. The thirteenth, clearing an exclusion zone, is included because SCN-FIRE-DT17 needs it (ch-02-03, function F18); it is also part of the ahs_sim.control_room model:

Command Executed by Safety-relevant Function in ch-02-03
Dispatch change FMS no F01–F03 (dispatch rules, assignment)
Grant an access permission AMS yes F10
Confirm a permission release AMS yes F10
Speed restriction AMS yes F09
Close a road segment AMS yes F09
Deploy a map version AMS yes map version (ch-12-09)
Switch a truck to manual (at the truck) VCS yes recovery (ch-16-05)

Remote-stop release (reset at the source) Independent safety function yes F17
Emergency notification People yes —
Clear an exclusion zone AMS yes F18
Direct driving (steer, propel, brake) ADS yes none — forbidden from CR1

All entries are handbook conventions [H]. "Resume a truck" covers a single truck after a fault stop or a recovery; restarting traffic after a remote stop or an exclusion zone is covered by the remote-stop release and the clearing command, whose accountable role is the Pit Supervisor.

The last row matters most. No role may steer, propel or brake an autonomous truck from CR1 [H]. The ADS drives; AMS turns the controller's missions, areas and permissions into offers that the ADS may refuse (REQ-02-03-003). A remote "nudge" to reposition a truck, convenient during commissioning, would put a person in the driving loop without a view of the truck's surroundings. Teleoperation is a different system with its own safety case.

FIG-16-01-02 groups the commands by the element that executes them and shows who may issue each under the reference rights of section 4.3. The AHS Controller issues every AMS and FMS command. The Pit Supervisor issues only remote stop, its release and emergency notifications — RST-P1 is in the Pit Supervisor's custody (equipment.yaml). The Field Technician switches a truck to manual, at the truck. Four roles have no command right at all: they request by radio and field terminal.

4.2 From the RACI to command rights

A RACI on paper and command rights in a system describe the same thing in two places. The reference mine keeps the RACI as the master and derives the rights from it [H], using the rules that ahs_sim.control_room.check_authority checks:

  1. Exactly one accountable role per command. No accountable role means nobody owns the outcome; two means each believes the other does.
  2. At least one responsible role per command. A command that nobody is responsible for is never issued, or is issued by whoever is nearest.
  3. A role may hold a command right only if it is R or A for that command. A right held by a role that is only consulted or informed is a right the RACI does not intend.
  4. No role may hold a right to a forbidden command. Direct driving from CR1 is the only forbidden command in the reference catalogue.

The rules are necessary, not sufficient. Rule 3 allows an accountable role to hold the right, but the reference design does not give the Pit Supervisor the right to apply restrictions, close segments, deploy maps or clear exclusion zones, although the Pit Supervisor is accountable for all of them. As in ch-01-06, the Pit Supervisor sees AMS read-only and decides; the controller executes, so every change to AMS goes through one pair of hands. The cost is that the decision must be recorded separately, because the button-presser is not the decider. In the reference RACI this is the case for six of the twelve commands (section 12, example 4). For these authorise-only commands the HMI asks the issuer for the authorising person and role before execution and records both [H] (REQ-16-01-003).

Rule 3 also misses a right that is within the RACI but wider than intended. The as-delivered "supervisor" profile of LAB-16-01-01 lets the Pit Supervisor apply restrictions, close segments and clear exclusion zones; the checker accepts this, and only the comparison with the least-privilege profile shows it. A rights review therefore has two parts: the rules, which find contradictions, and the comparison with the intended profile, which finds generosity.

Separation of duties. A map change is prepared and validated by Survey and deployed by the AHS Controller on the Pit Supervisor's authority [H]. Survey is consulted on deployment and must not hold the deployment right — exactly what the as-delivered profile gets wrong. The same principle forbids self-authorisation: nobody can be both the issuer and the recorded authoriser of a command (REQ-16-01-003).

Stop rights are broad, go rights are narrow. Remote stop is issued by the Pit Supervisor or the AHS Controller — from the console RST-CR1 or the portable transmitters RST-P1 and RST-P2 — and acts through the independent stop radio regardless of AMS and ADS state (ch-02-04, ch-04-05). The release is reset at the source that issued it, on the Pit Supervisor's authority, and the staged restart is the controller's (ch-04-05). Commands that let trucks move — resume, remote-stop release, clearing an exclusion zone, grant — are narrow and recorded with their authoriser. This asymmetry is ch-01-06's fifth principle, "stopping is open to every authorised controller; restarting requires authority", made concrete in the rights table.

Inside a declared incident the incident's own decision table applies: in SCN-FIRE-DT17 the grant of the ERT's access is still issued by the controller, but the Pit Supervisor is accountable for it (ch-04-07 section 8.2).

4.4 Sessions, role switches and the audit record

REQ-01-06-002 already requires AMS to record, for every command, the person, role, workstation or device, time, command, target, parameters, result and — for commands that need authorisation — the authorising person and role. To reconstruct decisions after an incident, the record must also show how the person came to hold the role at that moment. The reference design records sessions as events in their own right [H]:

Session event Recorded content [H]
Login Person, role requested, workstation, area scope, time; result (accepted or refused, and why — for example no valid authorisation, ch-16-09)
Area-scope change Areas added or removed, the other session involved, ownership transfers of permissions and open items in the moved areas
Role switch End of the old session, start of a new one; never a change of role inside a running session
Command Session ID, command, target, parameters, issuer, authoriser (person and role) where required, result
Refused command Session ID, command, reason (no right, missing authoriser, self-authorisation, area outside scope)
Logout Time; refused while the session owns an active permission or open item (REQ-01-06-005)
Session loss Workstation failure or timeout; permissions and locks persist, ownership flagged for takeover

Two design choices follow. First, a role switch is a new session. A Pit Supervisor who is also authorised as an AHS Controller and sits down at workstation 2 as second controller logs in as AHS Controller in a new session, and cannot authorise that session's commands as Pit Supervisor; the deputy must (REQ-16-01-003). Otherwise one person could decide and execute a principle-3 decision with nothing in the record to show that the check of two people had collapsed into one.

Second, the record answers the investigator's question: for every safety-relevant command in an incident window, who issued it, in which role and session, on whose authority and when, on the synchronised clock that AMS, FMS and the voice recorder share (ch-02-07). Shared accounts defeat this (ch-04-09), and so would a "break-glass" account. The reference design has none: stop-direction commands need no session (the remote stop is a device), and go-direction commands are never urgent enough to bypass the record (REQ-16-01-006).

4.5 Span of control: one controller, three areas

The span of control of the AHS Controller on duty is the whole autonomous fleet in AA-North, AA-Central and AA-South. ch-01-06 measures it in events and active states rather than trucks. This chapter adds the organisational question: which states are tied to the person, and how they move when the span has to be split [H].

At any moment the controller owns every active permission, restriction, closure, protected stopped truck and open item. If the controller is absorbed by one event, these states still need an owner: LV07's permission still has an expiry, and a Field Technician at a stopped truck still needs a contact.

The work can be split in two ways:

  • By area. The second controller takes one or two areas, for example AA-Central and AA-South, with every permission, restriction and open item in them. The first controller keeps AA-North and the event. Ownership moves item by item, by acceptance, as at a shift handover (REQ-01-06-003). This is the reference choice [H], because AMS can enforce it: a command outside a session's area scope is refused, and no area is ever without a supervising session (REQ-16-01-005).
  • By function. One controller runs the emergency; the other takes everything else, including normal traffic in the same area. This matches ch-01-06 section 6.3, but AMS cannot easily enforce it: to whom does a permission request at AP-02 during the fire on R05 belong? The reference design uses it only for the first minutes, while the second controller is arriving, and splits by area once they have logged in.

The conditions for splitting are events, not ratios [H]: an emergency in any area; abnormal events in two areas at once (SCN-COMMS-R05 in AA-North while a truck is down on R03); a planned high-load task such as a map deployment or a staged restart; or the controller reporting that the structured scan of areas, alarms and permissions can no longer be completed. Workload estimation is ch-16-10's subject; the handbook states no number of trucks or events per controller, which would need a verified source [TBV].

Escalation follows ch-01-06 section 4.4. This chapter adds the case of an unreachable Pit Supervisor. The rule [H] is that an unmade decision leaves the protective state in place: the pit stays held, the zone closed, the area locked. The controller calls the deputy named on the roster (REQ-16-01-007) and does not take the decision in the Pit Supervisor's place. Waiting has a production cost (section 12, example 3) but, while the protective state holds, creates no new hazard, which is why it is the default.

4.7 Single points of failure in the organisation

A single point of failure in a technical system is an element whose failure alone stops the whole function. Control rooms have organisational ones as well [H]:

Single point What fails with it System mitigation Staffing or procedure mitigation
One controller holds every active permission and open item Every permission and open item loses its contact when that person is absorbed, ill or called away Ownership persists in AMS, session loss keeps the lock and raises a takeover alarm (ch-01-06 section 7.1); area-scoped sessions allow a split Second controller called in (CTL-44); split by area with item-by-item acceptance
One operations workstation (workstation 1) Supervision of the whole fleet while it is down Second workstation with the same functions; remote stop independent of both (RST-CR1, RST-P2) Degraded procedure from ch-01-06 section 6.4
One AHS radio channel All voice exchanges in all three areas during an emergency Field terminal carries the digital half of every permission, so a congested channel delays entry but never grants it Emergency priority on the channel; routine requests deferred [R]
One Pit Supervisor reachable Nine of twelve commands carry a decision that only the Pit Supervisor takes Authorise-only commands cannot be executed without a recorded authoriser — the decision waits, the protective state stays Named deputy on every roster (REQ-16-01-007; checked in ch-16-09)
One person authorised for a critical task on a shift That task cannot be done if the person is unavailable Roster check against competency records (ch-16-09) At least two authorised people per critical task on each roster [A]
CR1 itself All central supervision Trucks stop on their own on loss of their links; remote stop through the portable transmitters The handbook defines no backup control room; a site sets this in its emergency response plan [A] (ch-16-06)

Under the reference rights the AHS Controller is the only role that can issue seven safety-relevant commands (section 12, example 4), and with one controller per shift that role is one person. The answer is not to give other roles those rights, which would undo least privilege, but a second controller who is quick to call and to take over, and an AMS that keeps states safe while the person is busy.

4.8 Statutory roles

Australian mining safety legislation requires sites to appoint certain positions with duties defined by law. For Western Australia, for example, the Work Health and Safety (Mines) Regulations 2022 are in force [F] (SRC-REG-WA-WHS-MINES-REGS-2022, title and in-force status only). Which statutory positions apply to an AHS operation, what they require, and how handbook roles relate to them is not verified and not stated here [TBV] (SRC-REG-AU-AHS-STATUTORY-ROLES). The handbook's RACI is a teaching convention, not a statement of legal duties.

5. Responsibilities and interface boundaries

Element Responsibility for roles and authority
FMS Executes dispatch changes from the controller's session; holds equipment status from the Maintenance Planner; records the issuer of each change
AMS Holds the command catalogue and the command rights per role; refuses commands outside the session's rights or area scope; requires and records the authoriser for authorise-only commands; records sessions, area-scope changes and role switches; keeps ownership of permissions and open items through session loss
ADS Accepts resumes and granted paths only as offers; accepts no direct driving command from CR1
VCS Accepts the switch to manual only at the truck; reports mode changes to AMS
Independent safety functions Executes remote stops from RST-CR1, RST-P1 and RST-P2 regardless of AMS and ADS state; records issuing device and time; reset at the source
Onboard safety-related systems Start the event (fire on DT17) that moves decision rights; decide the vehicle's own safe state
Manned-vehicle devices Carry requests, acknowledgements, releases and positions with the holder's identity; give no command right over trucks
People Hold the roles; decide according to the RACI; issue or authorise commands; keep the shift log that gives each decision its reason
Site administration (IT-security [A]) Creates accounts, assigns roles only on a recorded authorisation (ch-16-09), reviews rights against the RACI

The boundary that matters most runs between deciding and executing. AMS executes a correct-looking command exactly; it cannot tell whether the right person decided it. Only three things connect the RACI on paper with the state in the system: the command right, the recorded authoriser and the session record. Remove any one and the RACI becomes a document nobody can check.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — who grants, who requests, what enforces (SCN-POSCOM-LV07)

In SCN-POSCOM-LV07 the three parts of authority are split cleanly between people and systems [H]:

Time Event Requests Decides Enforces
T+0 s Radio request at AP-02: location, purpose, area LV07's driver (radio, field terminal) — —
T+20 s Area selected, permission request submitted — AHS Controller (A/R for the grant) AMS starts area clearing
T+25 s Trucks in the area stopped or rerouted, area locked — — AMS
T+90 s Area confirmed clear, permission granted pending acknowledgement — — AMS
T+95 s Permission by radio, read-back, acknowledgement on the field terminal Driver reads back and acknowledges AHS Controller confirms the read-back Field terminal and AMS (ACTIVE)
T+100 s LV07 enters — — AMS keeps trucks out

The driver has no command right; the request is a request. The controller holds the only command right and is accountable for the grant. AMS enforces the area lock, which no person maintains by hand. The Pit Supervisor is informed through the permission list, not asked. The exchange itself is ch-16-03's and ch-10-06's subject.

6.2 Scheduled transition — the role persists, the person changes (SCN-SHIFT-HANDOVER)

At 18:00 the AHS Controller role continues and the person in it changes. Decision rights belong to the role; ownership of running states belongs to a person and moves item by item (ch-01-06, ch-16-02). Organisationally the handover is two sessions in the AHS Controller role overlapping from T+0 s to T+900 s, both scoped to all three areas, while every permission and open item has exactly one owner. A command issued during the overlap belongs to the session that issued it, so the record shows which controller did what although both were "the controller".

The accountable column changes four times, and each change is a decision-rights handover:

  1. From the truck to AMS (T+10 s). No person decides the stop or the containment; both are by design, and the controller is accountable for containment working as designed.
  2. From the controller to the Pit Supervisor (T+15 s). From the emergency notification onwards, the Pit Supervisor is accountable for the incident on the shift. The controller remains responsible for executing everything in AMS and stays with the event; the controller does not hand the emergency over mid-flow (ch-01-06 section 6.3).
  3. From the Pit Supervisor to the ERT for the scene (T+600 s). The ERT decides how to fight the fire inside the zone and when the scene is safe. It does not decide anything about traffic, access or the zone in AMS.
  4. Back to the Pit Supervisor for clearance, then to the Maintenance Planner for DT17. The ERT's "scene safe" is evidence for the Pit Supervisor's clearance decision, not the decision itself.

The drill of section 1.1 failed at handovers 2 and 4. "Do what you need to" is not a decision on hold or reroute, and the ERT leader's "clear the zone" asked the controller to execute a Pit Supervisor's decision. Under REQ-16-01-003 AMS would have asked for an authoriser in both cases and refused the ERT leader as one.

6.4 Degraded operation — the accountable role cannot be reached

Suppose the Pit Supervisor is in a radio shadow in the pit at T+15 s, and the telephone goes unanswered [A]. At T+15 s the system is in its protective state: DT17 stopped, zone established, R05 held. What is missing is a decision on production and the start of the incident command.

The reference rule [H]: the controller continues every step for which they are responsible (acknowledge, call the ERT, confirm containment), keeps the pit held and calls the deputy named on the shift roster. The controller does not decide the reroute alone: the route change is authorise-only, and AMS asks for an authoriser that the controller cannot supply. When the deputy answers, the deputy is recorded as authoriser. The wait costs production (section 12, example 3); a controller trading risk against production without the role that holds that right would save tonnes and remove the check that principle 3 of ch-01-06 exists to provide.

A second degraded case: workstation 1 freezes at T+20 s. The first controller moves to workstation 2 and logs in under a new session; ownership of the session's states moves with the person, by acceptance, and the remote stop on the console RST-CR1 and the portable RST-P2 never depended on either workstation. If no workstation is available, the controller keeps the pit held, works by radio and asks the Pit Supervisor whether an area remote stop is needed as an additional latched stop — a decision with a production cost, as ch-04-07 section 8.3 notes.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

The RACI as a configuration item. ch-02-03 requires the function allocation of the AHS to be maintained as a configuration item and checked for gaps and overlaps before every release (REQ-02-03-001). The command RACI and the rights derived from it are the human end of the same allocation and get the same treatment [H]: a versioned table, checked with the rules of section 4.2 on every change of roles, every change of the command catalogue and every release of the AMS or FMS software (REQ-16-01-001). A new release that adds a command — a "resume all" button, a new map tool — is a change to the catalogue, and the command has no right holder until the RACI says who it is.

Accountable without the button. Giving the Pit Supervisor the HMI rights they are accountable for would make the record simpler. The reference design rejects it: two people changing AMS state in parallel breaks the controller's picture of the fleet, and a Pit Supervisor in the field has no HMI in front of them. The price is the authorise-only mechanism, which must be quick enough for an emergency: the HMI fills the authoriser from the on-shift roster (Pit Supervisor and deputy), records the radio or telephone time of the decision and needs no second login.

No direct driving, no break-glass. Both would make some situations easier, and both put a person into a loop the architecture keeps them out of — the driving loop or the authority loop. A truck that AMS cannot move is recovered at the truck (ch-16-05); a decision that cannot be authorised waits.

Sessions per person, scope per area. Personal accounts with the role as a session attribute make every command attributable (ch-01-06, ch-04-09); area scope makes the split of section 4.5 enforceable. Because scope changes happen when nobody has time, the reference HMI offers one action, "move AA-Central and AA-South to workstation 2", that lists the permissions and open items in those areas for the receiving controller to accept one by one.

Device rights count as rights. LAB-16-01-01 derives the remote-stop rights from equipment.yaml: RST-P1 is carried by the Pit Supervisor, the console RST-CR1 and RST-P2 serve the AHS Controller. A transmitter lent to another role is a rights change and is recorded as one [R].

Two timing points follow. The authoriser's decision and the issuer's command have different times, often tens of seconds apart, and both are kept: the authoriser's time is the radio time recorded by the issuer, the command time comes from AMS. Area-scope transfer during an event must not leave a gap: the areas move only when the receiving session accepts them, and until then the first session keeps them, as with any ownership transfer.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

Each workstation shows, in addition to the fleet, alarm, permission and area views of ch-01-06 [H]:

  • Who is on: the sessions open in CR1 — person, role, workstation, area scope — and the on-shift Pit Supervisor and deputy from the roster, with how to reach them;
  • What is mine: the permissions and open items owned by this session, distinct from those of the other session;
  • Waiting for authority: authorise-only commands prepared but not yet executed, with the role whose decision is needed and how long they have waited;
  • Refusals: commands the system refused in this session and why.

During SCN-FIRE-DT17, from T+15 s to T+120 s, the reroute stands in "waiting for authority" as a prepared command with "authoriser: Pit Supervisor — pending".

8.2 Who decides what

FIG-16-01-03 and section 4.3 give the RACI for the command catalogue. For SCN-FIRE-DT17 the decision table of ch-04-07 section 8.2 applies unchanged; LAB-16-01-01 checks it with the same rules and finds no finding. The decision tree for splitting or escalating the controller's work is [H]:

  1. Is there a risk to people (fire, person in an area, collision, near miss)? → Notify the Pit Supervisor at once; the Pit Supervisor is accountable for the incident; the controller keeps the event.
  2. Does the next step need a decision of the Pit Supervisor (restriction, closure, reroute, clearance, restart, manual recovery)? → Prepare the command, obtain the decision, record the authoriser. No answer → keep the protective state, call the deputy.
  3. Are abnormal events active in two areas, or is a planned high-load task due, or can the scan of areas, alarms and permissions no longer be completed? → Ask the Pit Supervisor for the second controller; split by area.
  4. Does a field role ask you to execute a decision that is not theirs ("clear the zone")? → Treat it as information for the accountable role, not as a decision.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — calling in the second controller during SCN-FIRE-DT17

The steps follow the shared timeline of section 6.3 and concern only the organisation; the emergency procedure itself is ch-16-06's and ch-04-07's [H].

  1. AHS Controller (T+15 s): acknowledge the incident, confirm containment, call the ERT and the Pit Supervisor. Say explicitly that the Pit Supervisor is now accountable for the incident: "Pit Supervisor, fire on DT17, R05 mid-ramp, zone established, R05 held — you have the incident."
  2. Pit Supervisor (by T+90 s): call the second controller to CR1. State which areas they will take: "Take AA-Central and AA-South at workstation 2."
  3. Second controller (on arrival): log in under your own identity as AHS Controller at workstation 2. Request the area scope stated by the Pit Supervisor.
  4. AHS Controller (first): move the areas in one action. Read each permission and open item in the moved areas to the second controller; the second controller accepts each one on the HMI. Keep AA-North and the event.
  5. Second controller: for every permission accepted, call the holder by radio, give your name as the controller now responsible and have the holder read back (as at a shift handover, ch-16-02).
  6. Pit Supervisor (T+120 s): give the hold-or-reroute decision in words that the controller can record: "Run the pit via R24 — Pit Supervisor authorises." AHS Controller: enter the Pit Supervisor as authoriser and execute.
  7. ERT leader (after T+600 s): report "fire out, scene safe" to the controller. Do not ask for the zone to be cleared.
  8. Pit Supervisor: decide the clearance on the recorded evidence and say so on the radio. AHS Controller: enter the authoriser and clear the zone.
  9. Pit Supervisor (end of the event): decide when the areas return to one controller; the transfer back follows step 4 in reverse.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

Organisation has no delay code of its own; its effects show in the codes of the events it handles [H]. Trucks held by the exclusion zone, including pit trucks held while the hold-or-reroute decision is pending, are Operating Delay, DLY-OD-14; EX02 without trucks is Non-Productive Time, DLY-NP-03; trucks stopped by an area remote stop are DLY-OD-13 (ch-04-07, ch-04-05; GMG Time Usage Model [F], SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020). A slow decision lengthens these intervals without changing their codes.

The organisation can be measured with indicators of its own [H], read per shift or month, with no target set here:

  • decision latency for authorise-only commands: from "waiting for authority" to the recorded decision;
  • authorise-only commands executed without a recorded authoriser — the target is zero, and the system prevents it;
  • refused commands by reason;
  • open rights-review findings and their age;
  • second-controller call-outs and the time from call to accepted area scope.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Every role learns the RACI by command, not by job title: the controller, that a prepared command waits for its authoriser; the Pit Supervisor, to state decisions in recordable words and to stay reachable or hand over to the deputy; the ERT, that "scene safe" is a report, not a clearance. Second controllers practise the area split in drills. Linking accounts to competency records, expiry and re-authorisation is ch-16-09's subject.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-16-01-001 [H] The command rights of every role, in the HMI and in devices, shall be derived from a RACI of the site's command catalogue that is maintained as a configuration item. The RACI and the rights shall be checked, on every change of roles or catalogue and before every AMS or FMS release, for: exactly one accountable and at least one responsible role per command, no right held by a role that is not responsible or accountable, and no right to a forbidden command. Rights wider than the site's least-privilege profile shall be reported for review. Rationale: when the HMI and the RACI differ, the HMI is what executes (sections 2 and 4.2). Verification: test (LAB-16-01-01: draft RACI 4 findings, as-delivered rights 10 findings, reference 0); inspection of the configuration record.

REQ-16-01-002 [H] No role shall hold a command right, from CR1 or any remote device, that steers, propels or brakes an autonomous truck directly. Movement commands from CR1 shall be offers that the ADS applies only under REQ-02-03-003. Rationale: direct driving bypasses the ADS's checks and puts a person into the driving loop without a view of the truck's surroundings (section 4.1). Verification: inspection of the command catalogue and every user profile; test (LAB-16-01-01 reports FORBIDDEN_RIGHT for the as-delivered controller profile).

REQ-16-01-003 [H] For every command whose accountable role does not issue it, the AMS shall require the authorising person and role before execution and shall record both with the issuer. It shall refuse the command when the authoriser is the issuer, or when the authoriser does not hold the accountable role on the current roster. Rationale: an accountable role without the button leaves no trace unless the authoriser is recorded (sections 4.2 and 6.3). Verification: test with each of the six authorise-only commands of the reference RACI: missing authoriser, self- authorisation and an ERT member as authoriser are refused; inspection of the records.

REQ-16-01-004 [H] The AMS shall record logins, logouts, session losses, area-scope changes and role switches as events with person, role, workstation, area scope and time. A change of role shall end the current session and start a new one, and every command shall be recorded against exactly one session. Rationale: every safety-relevant command must be traceable to a person, role and time, including how the person came to hold the role (section 4.4). Verification: inspection of the event schema; replay of SCN-FIRE-DT17 with a second controller and a role switch.

REQ-16-01-005 [H] The AMS shall support the division of the autonomous areas between two controller sessions. Moving an area shall transfer each active permission and open item in it only on acceptance by the receiving session; until accepted, the giving session shall remain the owner. No autonomous area shall be without a supervising session, and commands outside a session's area scope shall be refused. Rationale: the second controller can take part of the span of control only if the split is enforceable (section 4.5). Verification: test (move AA-Central and AA-South during SCN-FIRE-DT17; refused acceptance keeps the owner; command outside scope refused).

REQ-16-01-006 [H] Stop-direction commands (remote stop) shall remain available to their authorised issuers independently of any AMS session. No emergency mode, shared account or override account shall widen the rights to go-direction commands (grant, resume, release, clear, restart). Rationale: stopping must never wait for a login; starting must never skip the check (sections 4.2 and 4.4). Verification: inspection of the account list and of emergency functions; test of the remote stop with no AMS session open.

REQ-16-01-007 [H] Every shift roster shall name, for each role that is accountable for a command in the RACI, the person on shift and a reachable deputy. When the accountable person cannot be reached, the AHS Controller shall keep the protective state and contact the deputy, and shall not take the decision in the accountable role's place. Rationale: nine of twelve commands carry a decision only the Pit Supervisor takes; an unreachable decider must not turn into a silent transfer of authority (sections 4.5 and 6.4). Verification: inspection of rosters (roster coverage check in ch-16-09); drill of SCN-FIRE-DT17 with the Pit Supervisor unreachable.

REQ-16-01-008 [H] Remote-stop transmitters shall be included in the rights review: each transmitter's custodian role shall be recorded and shall be one of the authorised issuers of the remote stop. Rationale: a device is a command right (section 7.1). Verification: inspection against equipment.yaml (LAB-16-01-01 derives the issuers AHS-CTRL and PIT-SUP); custody records.

10. Verification and validation

Verification shows that the system enforces the organisation:

  • the rights check of LAB-16-01-01 runs on the site's real RACI and real profiles before every release (REQ-16-01-001);
  • negative tests: for every command and every role without the right, one attempt, refused and logged — twelve commands and seven roles give 84 cases, of which the reference rights allow 15 and 69 must be refused;
  • authorise-only tests: each of the six commands is tried without authoriser, with the issuer as authoriser and with a consulted role as authoriser, and each is refused (REQ-16-01-003);
  • a replay of SCN-FIRE-DT17 with a second controller checks the area move, item-by-item acceptance, refused commands outside scope and the records of section 7.3;
  • the remote stop is exercised with no AMS session open and with both workstations unavailable.

Validation asks whether the organisation works for the people in it. It needs drills built on registered scenarios — SCN-FIRE-DT17 with an unreachable Pit Supervisor, the area split during SCN-COMMS-R05, an emergency during SCN-SHIFT-HANDOVER — and a review of real decision latencies and refusals. The drill of section 1.1 found what verification could not: every command in it was allowed by the as-delivered profiles.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Accepting supplier profiles as the rights model. They are built for commissioning, not for operation.
  • Checking only for contradictions. The rules find rights the RACI does not allow; only the least-privilege profile finds rights it allows but does not intend.
  • Recording only the button-presser. For half of the reference catalogue, the decider is someone else.
  • A remote nudge "for commissioning only" that stays in the production profile.
  • Role switches inside a session, or one account per workstation.
  • An emergency mode that unlocks everything.
  • A second workstation with no area scope, so that two controllers act on one area.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • "Do what you need to." A delegation without content leaves the decision unrecorded and unowned.
  • Executing a field role's request as if it were a decision ("clear the zone").
  • Taking the Pit Supervisor's decision because the Pit Supervisor is out of reach, instead of holding and calling the deputy.
  • Calling in a second controller without saying what they take.
  • Lending a remote-stop transmitter to whoever is going into the pit.
  • Treating the RACI as a document for audits rather than the source of the HMI rights.

Example 4 — where the organisation concentrates. The AHS Controller is the only role that can issue seven safety-relevant commands: grant, release, restriction, closure, map deployment, resume and clearing the zone. With one AHS Controller per shift [A], seven commands rest on one person and nine decisions on another — the two single points that the second controller and the named deputy cover.

Example 5 — the fire decision table. The nine decisions of ch-04-07 section 8.2 pass the same four rules with no finding. Ordered by time, the accountable roles are: engineering design authority (T+0 s), AHS Controller (T+10 s), Pit Supervisor (T+15 s, T+120 s, T+300.7 s, T+600 s), ERT (scene safe), Pit Supervisor (clearance), Maintenance Planner (DT17's return to service). The Pit Supervisor holds the accountable role for five of the nine decisions; the ERT for one.

15. Key takeaways

  • Authority in a control room is a property of commands: who may issue each, who answers for it, how both are recorded.
  • Derive command rights from the RACI and check them: one accountable, at least one responsible, rights only for R or A, nothing forbidden — then compare with the least-privilege profile.
  • No role drives a truck from CR1. Stop rights are broad; go rights are narrow and recorded with their authoriser.
  • Sessions per person, a new session for a new role, area scope for every session: then every command has a person, role and time.
  • In SCN-FIRE-DT17 decision rights move from the truck to AMS, to the Pit Supervisor and, for the scene, to the ERT; clearance returns to the Pit Supervisor.
  • An unmade decision leaves the protective state in place; the answer to an unreachable decider is a reachable deputy.
