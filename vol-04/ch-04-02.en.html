1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

At 14:20 on a DAY shift DT17 is climbing the lower part of R05 with 220 t of ore on mission M-20481. Its localisation integrity drops near the highwall and its ADS slows it down. Forty seconds later a second truck, descending empty, stops at the same place. At 14:21 the AHS Controller restricts R05 to 20 km/h, the Pit Supervisor agrees, and Survey is called. Production continues more slowly. Nobody is hurt, and the shift report records an operating delay for the truck that stopped (ch-01-06).

At the handover the night Pit Supervisor asks questions that nobody on the day shift can answer from a document:

  • Were the trucks on R05 outside the conditions they were designed for, or inside them and merely slower?
  • Who decided that 20 km/h makes R05 acceptable, and on what basis? Would 25 km/h have been acceptable?
  • Rain is forecast for the night. Can the trucks use R05 at all on a wet road? What about the west ramp R24?
  • When the pit-floor trailer CT3 failed last month, was any route out of the pit still usable?

Each question is about the operational design domain (ODD): the conditions under which the truck system is designed to operate. This chapter makes the ODD explicit for the reference mine and separates it from two things it is often confused with — the site's operational boundaries and the operating conditions of the moment. It covers:

  • the ODD as a set of attributes taken from mine.yaml, with rules that decide whether each road segment is inside;
  • an ODD map of the reference mine under normal and degraded conditions, computed by LAB-04-02-01;
  • how an ODD exit is detected (on the truck and in the control room) and the graded responses to it;
  • how ODD restrictions live in the AHS map and the AMS configuration, so that a segment can be restricted or closed without a software change;
  • what the control room sees, who decides, and how the time is coded.

The truck-side modes (derated, restricted, stopped) are the subject of ch-04-06, which walks the same scenarios from the truck's side.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Specify the ODD of the reference mine's autonomous trucks as a set of attributes (area, segment, speed limit, wireless coverage class, GNSS multipath risk, visibility, presence of manned equipment) taken from mine.yaml.
  2. Distinguish the ODD (a design property of the system), the site's operational boundaries (AA-North, AA-Central, AA-South, access points AP-01 to AP-04) and the momentary operating conditions.
  3. Design ODD-exit detection and responses (derate, stop, restrict area, hand back to manual) for R05, whose attributes combine marginal coverage and high multipath risk.
  4. Analyse how the west ramp R24 and its access road R25 differ from R05 in ODD attributes (grade, coverage class, multipath risk, manned traffic near V and PK1), and whether the fallback route stays inside the ODD.
  5. Specify how ODD-related restrictions are represented in the AHS map and AMS configuration so that a segment can be restricted or closed without a software change.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Explain which site conditions (heavy dust, rain, night, coverage loss on lower R05) can take trucks outside their ODD and how this appears on the HMI.
  2. Apply an ODD-based decision to restrict or close a segment such as R05, communicate it to the Pit Supervisor and record the restriction with a delay code.
  3. Monitor weather and visibility (rain, dust, night) through a shift and decide with the Pit Supervisor when conditions require restricting AA-North or AA-South.
  4. Explain to manned-equipment operators where the autonomous-area boundaries and access points AP-01 to AP-04 are, and what changes for them when an area or segment is restricted.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Every autonomous truck is designed for a limited set of conditions: its localisation needs satellites and corrections, its link needs coverage, its perception needs visibility, its braking assumes a surface, a grade and a speed. A human driver adapts continuously and mostly without saying so; an autonomous truck adapts only in the ways it was designed to, and the people who run it must know what those ways are.

At the reference mine the limits are known in fragments — the supplier's documents, Survey's knowledge of the afternoon geometry near the R05 highwall, the network engineer's knowledge of CT3, the Pit Supervisors' experience of dust on R03 — and none of it is in one place. Three conversations go wrong:

  • "Is 20 km/h on R05 safe?" The value came from the previous restriction, not from a statement of what the system needs. It may cost production needlessly, or give false comfort while trucks keep stopping.
  • "Can we run in the rain?" The supplier's documents mention a wet-road grade limit; nobody has compared it with the grades of R05 and R24.
  • "Is the pit isolated?" When CT3 failed (SCN-COMMS-R05), the controller had to decide whether trucks could use the west ramp R24. R24 also has marginal coverage, and nobody could say whether CT3 serves it.

Each conversation lacks a written ODD, mapped onto the mine's roads, with a clear line between what the system is designed for, what the site permits and what the conditions are now. With that line drawn the questions become checkable, and the rule for "unknown" is decided before the event, not during it.

The concept of an ODD comes from road-vehicle automation [TBV] (SRC-STD-SAEJ3016, SRC-STD-ISO34503, proposed). ISO 17757:2019, Earth-moving machinery and mining — Autonomous and semi-autonomous machine system safety, covers safety requirements for autonomous and semi-autonomous machines and their systems and infrastructure across the life cycle [F] (SRC-STD-ISO17757, catalogue level). Whether and how it or other mining documents use the term ODD has not been verified [TBV] (SRC-PUB-ODD-MINING, proposed). The Western Australian code of practice Safe mobile autonomous mining in Western Australia gives guidance on risk management, hazard controls and emergency preparedness for mobile autonomous systems [F] (SRC-COP-WA-SMAM-2015, scope only). This chapter therefore uses ODD as a handbook term [H] with a handbook definition, and every attribute limit in it is an illustrative assumption [A].

3. Terminology and foundations

Operational design domain (ODD) [H]. The set of conditions under which the truck system — vehicle, ADS, AMS and the infrastructure they rely on — is designed to operate autonomously. It is a design property: it is stated by the system supplier, agreed with the mine, and changes only through design change. In this handbook it is written as named ODD attributes, each with a rule that decides whether a given condition is inside.

Operational boundaries [H]. The site's decisions about where and how the system is used: the autonomous areas AA-North, AA-Central and AA-South, the access points AP-01 to AP-04 through which manned vehicles enter, and the restrictions and closures of individual segments in the AHS map. The boundaries are owned by the mine. They must lie inside the ODD; they may be narrower.

Operating conditions [H]. The state of the world at a moment: satellite geometry, which comms sites are up, the visibility on each road, whether the road is wet, day or night, which manned vehicles are where. Conditions change during a shift and are observed, forecast or reported, not decided.

ODD attribute. One dimension of the ODD. Some are static segment attributes held in mine.yaml and the AHS map (area, site speed limit, grade, coverage class, GNSS multipath risk class, rolling resistance). Others are dynamic conditions that apply to a segment for a time (visibility, surface, coverage lost because a comms site is down). The ODD rule for an attribute may depend on a condition: high multipath risk is acceptable with good satellite geometry and not with poor geometry.

ODD map [H]. For a given ODD and given conditions, the verdict for every segment: inside, inside only derated (inside the ODD only below a speed cap), or outside. It is computed, not drawn: the inputs are the segment attributes and the conditions.

ODD exit. The transition of a truck, segment or area from inside to outside the ODD, because the conditions changed (rain begins, CT3 fails) or because the truck moved into a segment whose verdict is outside. An ODD exit is a condition to be detected and responded to; it is not itself a fault.

Derate, restriction, closure. A derate (speed derating) lowers the speed of one truck, decided by its ADS. A speed restriction lowers the speed allowed on a segment for all trucks, set by people in the AHS map. A road closure removes a segment from routing. The ODD map tells which response a condition needs.

Hand back to manual operation. Ending autonomous operation of an area or a truck for a period and continuing, if at all, with manned equipment under the site's procedures. It is the response when a condition outside the ODD is expected to last.

Coverage class and GNSS multipath risk class. The infrastructure attributes typed into mine.yaml for every segment [A] (ch-01-03, section 4.7): teaching shorthands for the models of Volumes 11 and 05.

Satellite geometry. How the visible GNSS satellites are spread across the sky. A pit wall hides part of it, so geometry near a highwall is worse and changes with the time of day. Here it is a condition with three levels — good, poor, very poor [A] — that the site can forecast (Volume 05).

4. Technical explanation

4.1 Three layers: design domain, site boundaries, conditions now

FIG-04-02-01 shows how the three concepts relate. The ODD is the outer region. The operational boundaries are drawn inside it. The operating conditions are a point that moves during the shift. For conditions whose permitted range depends on speed (here satellite geometry with multipath risk, and visibility), derating may keep the system inside near the edge of the ODD; limits that a lower speed cannot satisfy (a wet grade, no coverage) require a closure, a hold or a stop (section 4.5). Outside the ODD, the system must detect the exit and respond.

The three layers have different owners and change at different speeds:

Layer Example at the reference mine Owner Changes
ODD Wet-road grade limit; poor coverage accepted only on slow links; high multipath only with good geometry [A] System supplier, agreed with the mine By design change and re-verification
Operational boundaries AA-North, AA-Central, AA-South; AP-01 … AP-04; MIA excluded; R05 restricted to 20 km/h The mine (Pit Supervisor, mine management) By a recorded site decision, in minutes to months
Operating conditions Afternoon satellite geometry poor; CT3 down; dust on R03; rain Nobody: observed, forecast, reported Continuously

Two consequences follow. First, a site cannot extend the ODD by a site decision. If the ODD excludes a wet road steeper than 8 % [A], no procedure or approval makes wet R05 acceptable; only a design change can. The Pit Supervisor may decide not to use R05 in conditions the ODD allows, never to use it in conditions the ODD excludes. Second, the conditions must be watched, because they are what moves.

Three remarks on the table.

What mine.yaml has, and what it does not. The five static attributes come from the reference-mine data; LAB-04-02-01 reads them through ahs_sim.load_mine() and adds none. Visibility, surface and manned traffic are conditions, not segment attributes. One dependency is missing: mine.yaml records a coverage class per segment but not the comms site that serves it, so when CT3 fails the lab takes the affected segments from the scenario registry (SCN-COMMS-R05: R05, R09, R10, R11) rather than guessing. The serving sites per segment are recorded in network.yaml in Volume 11 (ch-11-03); the lab of this chapter uses only mine.yaml and the scenario registry, so it keeps R19, R20 and R24 unresolved when CT3 is down.

Conditional rules are normal. "Poor coverage only on slow links" accepts the tip link R22 (15 km/h), where a truck that loses its link stops within a short grant at low speed. "High multipath only with good geometry" makes R05's verdict depend on the sky. Fixed single-attribute limits would exclude R05 always or include it always; neither is right.

Manned equipment belongs in the ODD, but its evaluation depends on where each manned vehicle is (ch-04-08). LAB-04-02-01 evaluates the road attributes only.

4.3 The ODD map

For segment s with attributes as under conditions c, each ODD rule k gives a verdict rk(as,c) in the order inside < inside only derated < outside, and derate rules give a speed cap v¯k. The segment's verdict is the most severe rule verdict, and its cap is the lowest cap among the derate rules that apply:

V(s,c)=maxkrk(as,c),vcap(s,c)=mink: rk=deratev¯k

A cap at or above the site speed limit is no restriction, so the segment is then inside. Every reason is kept with the verdict: "grade 9.0 % on wet road" and "coverage lost" lead to different actions and different people.

FIG-04-02-02 shows the infrastructure attributes. Coverage is marginal on R04, R05, R09, R10, R11, R19, R20 and R24 and poor on R22; multipath risk is medium on R07, R08, R17, R18 and R24 and high on R05. Only R05 combines marginal coverage, high multipath risk and the steepest grade (9.0 %).

Under the baseline conditions — dry, good geometry, good visibility, all comms sites up — LAB-04-02-01 finds one segment outside: R16, the MIA road, in the manned area where trucks go only in manual or maintenance mode. R22 is inside by the slow-link rule. At night the map is the same.

Three results deserve attention before the scenarios.

Poor geometry keeps R05 inside, slowly. The 120.6 s falls entirely on the empty descent (40 → 20 km/h): a loaded HT220 climbs R05 at its power-limited 11.4 km/h, below the cap.

CT3 down isolates the whole pit. With R05 and R09 outside, no route leaves the pit floor; with R10 and R11 outside, EX03 and EX04 are cut off too. R24 does not help, because R09 is the only link to it. This agrees with ch-02-08: trucks inside stop within their granted paths, and trucks arriving at AP-02 and AP-04 are held.

Rain closes the main ramp. The wet-road limit (8 %) is below R05's grade (9.0 %), while R24 (6.3 %) and R09 (6.5 %) stay inside. Whether a site runs over R24 or stops under a site-wide weather outage (DLY-SE-01) is its decision; the ODD only says that R05 cannot be part of it.

4.5 Detecting an ODD exit and responding to it

An ODD exit is noticed in one of two places (FIG-04-02-03), and the design must use both.

On the truck. The ADS monitors its localisation integrity, its link and its perception. On a degraded condition it acts at once, within its granted path — derate or controlled stop — without waiting for the control room or knowing the cause (ch-02-08, section 4.5). This is reactive: the first truck to enter a degraded section is the detector.

In the control room. People and the AMS see what one truck cannot: a geometry forecast, a comms site reported down, a rain front, derates at one place on several trucks. This is predictive or pattern-based and can act on a segment before the next truck reaches it.

The two paths meet at the ODD map. A vehicle event arrives at the AMS with its reason and position; the AHS Controller checks the affected segments against the map for the current conditions; the verdict selects the response:

Verdict Response Decided by Effect on trucks
Inside Continue; record the condition AHS Controller none
Inside only derated Speed restriction on the segment at or below the cap AHS Controller, Pit Supervisor informed or approving Slower cycle; no delay code
Outside, route around exists inside the ODD Close the segment; AMS reroutes AHS Controller, Pit Supervisor approves Longer cycle
Outside, no route inside the ODD Hold or stop trucks in the area Pit Supervisor Operating delay (or standby if site-wide)
Outside and expected to last Hand back to manual operation Pit Supervisor, mine management Area worked manually or not at all

The graded response follows four rules [H].

  1. The vehicle-side response is never overridden centrally. A central restriction can make trucks slower or stop them, never make a derated or stopped truck faster or start it (central permissions never override vehicle-side safety decisions, ch-01-04).
  2. A central restriction never exceeds the ODD. Under poor geometry R05 may be restricted to 20 km/h or lower, never higher (REQ-04-02-003).
  3. Unknown is outside. If a condition cannot be established — no geometry forecast, or a comms site down and nobody knows which segments it serves — the segment is outside until it can (REQ-04-02-006). This is fail-closed applied to the ODD. With CT3 down, R19, R20 and R24 are unresolved for exactly this reason.
  4. A restriction is lifted only when the condition is back inside, with authorisation, never by a timer (REQ-04-02-007).

From the pit floor the fallback route of EX02's loaded trucks is R18, R08, R09, R24, R25, R12, R02, R21: it adds R09 (6.5 %, marginal coverage) to the climb and joins the main haul road at V. Against the illustrative ODD:

  • Poor satellite geometry: R24's medium multipath risk keeps it inside without a derate; the fallback route is inside.
  • Rain: R24 at 6.3 % and R09 at 6.5 % are below the wet-road limit of 8 %; the fallback route is inside, derated by visibility, while R05 is outside.
  • Very poor geometry: R24 is inside only derated (20 km/h), R05 outside; the fallback route is still usable.
  • CT3 down: R09 is outside and R24 is unresolved; there is no fallback.

In rain and in very poor geometry, two of the four conditions, R24 stays inside the ODD where R05 does not — worth knowing before the rain, not during it. The cost of using it is not only cycle time. R25 ends at Intersection V, where the MIA gate road R13 (AP-01), the park-up spur R14 and the fuel-bay spur R15 meet; manned vehicles from the MIA and trucks bound for PK1 and FB1 all pass V. Sending all pit traffic through V is a change to be hazard-assessed before production use (ch-04-01): the ODD map says the road is inside, not that the traffic pattern is acceptable.

One result is easy to misread. Closing R05 costs the ore circuits 236.0 s per cycle (27 → 23 loads in 12 h) but saves the waste circuits 39.4 s (22 → 23 loads): loaded trucks from the RL 330 bench climb R24's 6.3 % faster than R05's 9.0 %. ahs_sim routes by shortest distance, which is why the baseline sends them over R05; time-based routing belongs to Volume 10.

4.7 ODD restrictions in the AHS map and the AMS configuration

A segment can be restricted or closed without a software change if the ODD and the boundaries are data with an owner and a lifecycle [H]:

  • Segment attributes in the AHS map, versioned with it (Volume 12). A new grade after a ramp is re-cut or a new coverage class after a comms site moves is a map change, and it triggers a recomputation of the ODD map.
  • The ODD as a configuration document: the rules of Table 4.2, owned by the supplier, approved by the mine, read by the AMS, not edited in the control room.
  • Conditions as inputs with a source and an age (forecast, network monitor, Pit Supervisor, truck events). A silent source makes the condition unknown, and unknown is outside.
  • Restrictions and closures as records: segment, cap or closure, reason (ODD attribute and condition), applying and approving roles, start and review time. The AMS applies them at once to routing and speed limits.
  • Validation at entry: the AMS accepts restrictions stricter than the ODD requires and rejects looser ones.

The record answers "who decided 20 km/h?"; the ODD rule it cites answers "on what basis?".

4.8 Trade-offs and model limits

Granularity. The lab evaluates whole segments; a restriction slows all 1340 m of R05 although the degradation is on its lower part. Finer segments save cycle time and cost map maintenance.

Prediction against detection. A forecast-based closure may close a ramp that would have been fine; a purely reactive design costs a stop each time. Use both; the balance is a site decision.

What the model leaves out. Steady-state speeds, no acceleration, no interaction between trucks, so the cycle deltas understate the effect on a busy ramp. Coverage and multipath classes are shorthands (Volumes 05, 11). The ODD limits are chosen to make R05 and R24 differ instructively.

5. Responsibilities and interface boundaries

Element Responsibility for the ODD and operational boundaries
FMS Re-plans assignments when a closure or hold changes cycle times or isolates a loading unit; records production loss against the restriction
AMS Holds the ODD map, the conditions and the restriction records; applies restrictions and closures to routing and speed limits; rejects restrictions looser than the ODD; treats unknown conditions as outside; publishes ODD state to the HMI
ADS Monitors localisation integrity, link and perception on the truck; derates or stops within its granted path on a degraded condition; reports event, reason and position; never relaxes on central instruction
VCS Executes the speed and stop commands of the ADS; reports its own limits (for example traction or brake faults) that reduce what the truck can do
Independent safety functions Unaffected by the ODD map: the remote stop acts regardless of ODD state (ch-02-04, ch-04-05)
Onboard safety-related systems Report conditions that reduce one truck's capability (tyre, payload), handled as single-truck restrictions (ch-04-06)
People System supplier states and maintains the ODD; mine approves it; AHS Controller watches conditions, applies restrictions and closures, informs the Pit Supervisor; Pit Supervisor approves restrictions and closures with production effect, decides holds and hand-back to manual; Survey reports satellite geometry and base-station status; Field Technician restores comms sites; Shovel Operators and water-truck operators report visibility

Two boundaries matter most. The ODD belongs to the design; the boundaries belong to the site. The supplier cannot tell the mine where to run, and the mine cannot tell the supplier that the trucks will be fine on a wet 9 % ramp. Both must sign the same ODD document. Detection is shared; the vehicle acts first. The truck finds an exit by experiencing it and acts within its grant; the control room finds it by seeing a pattern or a forecast and acts on segments. Neither can replace the other.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — the baseline map

On a dry day with good satellite geometry, all comms sites up and good visibility, the ODD map of the reference mine has one segment outside — the MIA road R16 — and no segment derated. The operational boundaries (the three autonomous areas and the four access points) lie inside the ODD. The control room's job in normal operation is to watch the conditions: the geometry forecast for the afternoon, the status of CT1, CT2 and CT3, the weather, the watering of R03 and R04.

6.2 Degraded operation — SCN-GNSS-R05 (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

The registry scenario starts with poor afternoon satellite geometry and RTK corrections from GB1 available. T+0 s is 14:20:00, as in ch-01-06. The registry gives the events at T+0, 40, 60 and 300 s; rows marked [A] are added by this chapter. ch-04-06 follows the same timeline from the trucks' side, including DT13's stop and resume.

At T+120 s both options keep the trucks inside the ODD. The Pit Supervisor keeps R05 open because the derate is cheaper for the ore circuits and does not change traffic at V. A mixed option — waste over R24, ore over derated R05 — needs per-circuit routing, which ahs_sim 0.6.0 does not model.

The question at T+300 s is not routine. If GB1 or its corrections have failed, the condition is not "poor geometry", which the ODD covers, but a loss of corrections, which it does not. Until Survey answers, the condition is partly unknown; the procedure of section 8.3 keeps the restriction and is ready to close R05.

6.3 Degraded operation — SCN-COMMS-R05, the pit isolated

When CT3 loses power (registry T+0 s), trucks on lower R05 and R09–R11 detect the loss at T+3 s and stop within their granted paths; the AMS marks them communication-lost at T+10 s; CT3 returns at T+900 s and the AMS reconciles (ch-02-08: eight trucks, the last restarted at T+1172 s). In ODD terms no loading unit has a route inside the ODD: all four are on hold, and trucks arriving at AP-02 and AP-04 are held.

The map adds the three unresolved segments. A controller who reasons "R24 is not in the list, so the west ramp is fine" is relying on an absence of data. Here it changes nothing — R09, R10 and R11, the only roads to R24, are lost anyway — but if another comms site failed, the same reasoning would decide whether trucks are routed into a coverage gap.

6.4 At the edge of the ODD — SCN-WATER-WT02

WT02 waters R03 on a hot, dry shift. Shortly afterwards a waste truck passing through residual spray and dust stops for an obstacle that is not there (registry T+0 s); the controller reviews the perception snapshot, classifies the stop as a nuisance stop (T+30 s) and authorises a resume (T+45 s), and the delay is coded DLY-OD-03 (ch-01-05).

In ODD terms the truck's perception met reduced visibility, which the illustrative ODD accepts at reduced speed: R03 inside only derated to 30 km/h, costing the waste circuits 128.0 s per cycle (22 → 21 loads in 12 h). Poor visibility would put R03 outside, and because R03 is the only road to D01, EX03 and EX04 would be on hold.

Here the condition is produced by the operation itself: the watering pattern moves the operating point towards or away from the edge of the ODD. The response can be operational — water so that trucks do not meet fresh spray — rather than a restriction. The Pit Supervisor decides with the water-truck operator, guided by the nuisance-stop pattern on the HMI (ch-14-05).

6.5 A condition outside the ODD — rain

Rain is not a registered scenario but is what the night Pit Supervisor asked about. With wet roads and reduced visibility everywhere [A], R05 is outside on grade alone and eight segments are capped at 30 km/h. Rerouting over R09 and R24 costs the ore circuits 484.6 s per cycle (27 → 21 loads in 12 h) and the waste circuits 340.5 s (22 → 19). Running that way, stopping the pit or stopping the site (DLY-SE-01) is the Pit Supervisor's and mine management's decision; the ODD settles in advance that "as usual over R05" is not an option.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Walk the SCN-GNSS-R05 timeline and ask what the design had to decide in advance for each step to work.

T+0 s — the truck acts on its own measurement, not on the map. The forecast may have said good geometry; if DT17's monitor says "degraded", DT17 derates.

T+40 s — derate or stop is the truck's decision. Which integrity level calls for which response belongs to the localisation and ADS design (ch-05-06); no threshold is given here. The chapter requires only that the decision is made on the truck, within its grant, and reported with its reason.

T+60 s — what a restriction may say. An AMS that checks restrictions against the ODD map makes rule 2 a property of the system rather than of the controller's memory. A free-text field is simpler and produces "20 km/h because we used it last time".

T+300 s — conditions the ODD does not list. The illustrative ODD says nothing about a loss of RTK corrections. A real ODD must say what happens for every condition it does not list; the handbook's answer is "outside".

Static classes, dynamic causes. Coverage and multipath classes in the map are checkable and versioned, but they summarise conditions that vary: R24's marginal coverage depends on which comms sites are up. The map must record what a class depends on — which is what the missing served_by field is.

Who owns the ODD. A mine-written ODD may describe conditions the system was never designed for; a supplier-only ODD may be stated in terms (sensor ranges, integrity levels) that cannot be mapped onto roads. The handbook's position [H]: a supplier-owned document, translated into segment rules with the mine and approved by both.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

For each step of the shared timeline, what has to cross an interface:

  • T+0 s. ADS → AMS: event localisation integrity degraded, with truck, segment, position, mode (derated), and the integrity indicator. The ADS acts before the message is sent; the message is information, not a request.
  • T+40 s. ADS → AMS: event stopped — localisation, with the same fields. The AMS holds traffic behind DT13 within the reservations it already manages. AMS → FMS: DT13's mission paused, so that the FMS can start the operating-delay interval (DLY-OD-06).
  • T+60 s. HMI → AMS: restriction record (R05, 20 km/h, reason GNSS multipath high, geometry poor, applied by the AHS Controller, approved by the Pit Supervisor, review time). AMS: check against the ODD map, accept, apply to speed limits in new and extended granted paths; trucks already on R05 receive the lower limit with their next path extension.
  • T+120 s. AMS → HMI: the fallback route for each circuit with its ODD verdict and the cycle-time effect, so that the comparison takes a minute, not a meeting.
  • T+300 s. People: Survey's report becomes a condition input (geometry poor, corrections normal) with a source and time. AMS: re-evaluates; nothing changes if the report confirms the condition.

The time that matters most is between T+0 s and T+60 s: one minute in which the inputs of the ODD map already hold the forecast of poor geometry but the restriction on R05 is not yet applied. A design in which the forecast applies the restriction at 14:00, before any truck derates, removes that minute; DT13 would then descend R05 at 20 km/h or less, which the illustrative ODD treats as inside — at the cost of restricting R05 on afternoons when the geometry would have been good enough. That is the prediction-against-detection trade-off of section 4.8 in concrete form.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

The reference mine's HMI [H] shows the ODD in three places:

  • ODD layer on the map. Every segment coloured by verdict — inside, inside only derated (with the cap), outside — and, on hover, the reasons. Unresolved segments are shown as outside with the reason unknown. At T+60 s of SCN-GNSS-R05 the controller sees R05 amber with multipath high, geometry poor: ≤ 20 km/h.
  • Conditions panel. Satellite geometry level and its source (forecast or Survey), status of CT1, CT2, CT3 and GB1, surface (dry or wet, set by the Pit Supervisor), visibility reports per segment, each with its time. A condition older than its configured age is shown as unknown.
  • Restriction list. Every active restriction and closure with segment, cap, reason, who applied and approved it, start and review time. Restrictions past their review time are highlighted.

Alarms come from events, not from the map: DT17 derated — localisation at T+0 s, DT13 stopped — localisation at T+40 s. The pattern — two trucks at the same place within a minute — is what the controller must recognise at T+60 s; a good HMI groups such events by location (ch-16-10).

8.3 Procedure excerpt — degraded localisation on R05

The steps follow the shared timeline of section 6.2.

  1. AHS Controller (T+0 s): acknowledge DT17 derated — localisation. Check the conditions panel: is poor geometry forecast for R05 now? Note the event; no action yet if it is a single truck.
  2. AHS Controller (T+40 s): acknowledge DT13 stopped — localisation. Do not command DT13 to move. Confirm on the HMI that traffic behind DT13 is held by the AMS.
  3. AHS Controller (T+60 s): two trucks at one place within a minute is a pattern, not a truck fault. Open the ODD layer for R05; apply a restriction at the ODD cap (20 km/h [A]) with reason and review time; call the Pit Supervisor for approval.
  4. Pit Supervisor (T+60 s): approve or tighten the restriction. Tell the Shovel Operators at EX01 and EX02 to expect longer cycles.
  5. AHS Controller with Pit Supervisor (T+120 s): open the fallback comparison. Decide whether to keep R05 derated or close it; if closing, check traffic at Intersection V with the Pit Supervisor before the AMS reroutes.
  6. AHS Controller (T+300 s): call Survey to check GB1 and satellite availability. Until Survey reports, keep the restriction; if Survey finds a correction fault, treat R05 as outside and close it.
  7. AHS Controller (after Survey): record Survey's finding as a condition input; set the review time (20:00 in ch-01-06); include the restriction in the shift handover with its reason.
  8. AHS Controller (DT13): follow the recovery of DT13 under ch-04-06 (resume only after its own integrity is restored and the controller's checks are done).

Two points need care. A restriction carries no delay code: under the GMG model the trucks are still working, and the cost appears as a longer cycle (ch-02-04, section 8.4). It must therefore be recorded as a restriction with its start and end times, or nobody will find the 120.6 s per cycle in the shift report. A truck held because the only route to its destination is outside the ODD for a local condition is coded DLY-OD-15 (held because the only route is outside the ODD), an Operating Delay [H].

8.5 Training and competency implications

AHS Controllers must read the ODD layer and say for any segment and condition whether it is inside, inside only derated or outside, and why; know the four rules of section 4.5; and recognise a pattern of vehicle events at one place as an ODD question rather than a set of truck faults. Pit Supervisors own the boundaries and the decision to hold or hand back, not the ODD. Survey's report is a time-stamped condition input. Manned-equipment operators need to know where the autonomous areas and access points are, and that a closure moves trucks: with R05 closed they appear at Intersection V and on R25 in unusual numbers. LAB-04-02-01 suits a controller session: change one condition, predict the map, run it.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-04-02-001 [H] The ODD of the autonomous truck system shall be documented as a set of named attributes, each with its data source, its rule (including conditional rules and speed caps) and the means by which an exit is detected, and shall state that any condition it does not list is outside. Rationale: an ODD that exists only as experience cannot be mapped onto roads or checked (sections 2, 4.2 and 7.1). Verification: inspection of the ODD document; review by the system supplier and the mine.

REQ-04-02-002 [H] The AHS map shall hold for every segment the static ODD attributes (area, site speed limit, grade, coverage class, GNSS multipath risk class), and the ODD map shall be recomputed whenever the AHS map, the ODD or a condition changes. Rationale: a map computed from stale attributes shows segments inside that are outside (section 7.2). Verification: test (LAB-04-02-01 reads the attributes from mine.yaml and recomputes the map for each condition); inspection of the map-change workflow (Volume 12).

REQ-04-02-003 [H] The AMS shall reject any operational boundary, speed restriction or route that permits autonomous operation on a segment outside the ODD, or at a speed above the ODD cap of a segment that is inside only derated, under the current conditions. Rationale: a site may be stricter than the ODD, never looser (sections 4.1 and 4.5, rule 2). Verification: test (restriction above the cap rejected; route over a segment outside the ODD not issued); demonstration.

REQ-04-02-004 [H] Speed restrictions and road closures shall be configuration records, applied without a software change, each recording segment, cap or closure, reason (ODD attribute and condition), applying role, approving role, start time and review time. Rationale: the control room must respond in seconds, and every restriction must answer "who decided, and on what basis" (sections 1.1 and 4.7). Verification: inspection of the record schema; demonstration of a restriction applied and lifted during operation.

REQ-04-02-005 [H] On a degraded condition detected on the truck, the ADS shall derate or stop within its granted path without waiting for a central decision, and no central restriction, lifting of a restriction or command shall make a derated or stopped truck faster or move. Rationale: the vehicle detects the exit first, and central permissions never override vehicle-side safety decisions (section 4.5, rule 1). Verification: test (fault injection on the truck while a looser central restriction is active; planned for the integrated simulator, Volume 23); inspection of the AMS command set.

REQ-04-02-006 [H] The AMS shall treat a segment as outside the ODD when a condition needed for its verdict is unknown, including a condition input older than its configured age and a segment whose comms dependency is unknown while a comms site is down. Rationale: fail closed applied to the ODD (section 4.5, rule 3; SCN-COMMS-R05 in section 6.3). Verification: test (LAB-04-02-01 lists R19, R20 and R24 as unresolved with CT3 down); demonstration with a silenced condition source.

REQ-04-02-007 [H] A speed restriction or closure shall be lifted only by a named role, with the reason that the condition is back inside the ODD, and the HMI shall list every restriction past its review time. Rationale: restrictions lifted by default return trucks to degraded segments; restrictions never lifted cost production for hours (section 7.2). Verification: inspection; demonstration.

REQ-04-02-008 [H] The HMI shall show the ODD verdict of every segment with its reasons and the conditions with their sources and ages, and shall group vehicle events (derate, stop) by location so that a pattern at one segment is visible. Rationale: the controller's decision at T+60 s of SCN-GNSS-R05 depends on recognising a pattern (sections 6.2 and 8.1). Verification: demonstration with the SCN-GNSS-R05 timeline; usability review (ch-16-10).

REQ-04-02-009 [H] Before closing a segment that carries haul traffic, the AMS shall present, for each affected loading unit, the fallback route, its ODD verdict under the current conditions and the change in cycle time, and shall not reroute over a segment whose verdict is outside or unknown. Rationale: a fallback is useful only if it is inside the ODD; R24 is inside in rain and in poor geometry but unresolved when CT3 is down (section 4.6). Verification: test (LAB-04-02-01 route impact for each condition); demonstration.

10. Verification and validation

Verification asks whether the model and the map compute what they claim. For this chapter (run by make test CH=ch-04-02):

  • the segment attributes are read from mine.yaml and match it (25 segments; R05 marginal and high at 9.0 %; R24 marginal and medium at 6.3 %; eight marginal segments);
  • under baseline and night conditions only R16 is outside and no cycle changes;
  • the slow-link rule accepts R22's poor coverage;
  • every condition of Table 4.4 gives the listed outside and derated segments and the listed cycle-time changes;
  • with CT3 down the map lists exactly R19, R20 and R24 as unresolved, read from the scenario registry rather than assumed;
  • a segment with two failing rules is outside with both reasons, and a cap above the site limit is no derate.

Validation asks whether the map predicts what happens: do integrity derates occur where and when the map says the geometry is poor; do link losses follow comms-site outages on the segments the map associates with that site? A map that misses many vehicle events, or predicts many that never occur, needs better conditions or finer segments. The reference mine is fictional, so this is left to Volume 22 (event analytics) and Volumes 05 and 11 (models).

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • No written ODD. Limits held as the supplier's slides and the controllers' experience cannot be mapped or checked.
  • ODD as a list of single-attribute limits. Without conditional rules, R05 is either always excluded or always included; neither is true.
  • Attributes without dependencies. A coverage class without the comms site behind it cannot say what a site failure does.
  • Free-text restrictions. A restriction field that accepts any number lets the site run looser than the design.
  • Missing input read as normal. A missing forecast or monitor shown as "inside" instead of "unknown".
  • Only reactive detection. Waiting for trucks to find every exit costs a stop each time; forecasts and patterns are cheaper.
  • Only predictive detection. A map is never complete; the truck's own monitors must still act.
  • Connectivity mistaken for a fallback. A road that connects is not a fallback unless it is inside the ODD under the same conditions.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Negotiating the ODD. "We'll run R05 wet, carefully" is a site decision the site cannot make.
  • Restriction by habit. Using last week's 20 km/h without checking the cap for today's condition.
  • Treating a pattern as truck faults. Resetting each stopped truck in turn while the condition persists.
  • Assuming an unlisted segment is fine. R24 not being in SCN-COMMS-R05's list is not evidence that it has coverage.
  • Forgetting the restriction at handover. A restriction without reason and review time becomes permanent.
  • Rerouting without looking at the new traffic. Closing R05 sends ore trucks through Intersection V past PK1, FB1 and the MIA gate road.
  • Restricting a road when the cause is the watering. On R03 the cheaper response may be a different watering pattern.

12. Worked examples

All values [A], computed by LAB-04-02-01 with ahs_sim 0.6.0 on mine version 0.3.0.

Example 1 — R05 against the wet-road limit. R05 runs from P1 (RL 420) to P2 (RL 300): a rise of 120 m over a horizontal 1334 m, a grade of 120 / 1334 = 9.0 %. The illustrative ODD allows 10 % on a dry road and 8 % on a wet road: dry R05 is inside, wet R05 is outside. R24 rises 90 m over 1422 m horizontally (6.3 %) and R09 30 m over 461 m (6.5 %): both inside when wet.

Example 2 — derate or close R05 under poor geometry. Derate: EX02 1564.1 → 1684.6 s (27 → 25 loads in 12 h). Close: EX02 +236.0 s (23 loads), EX03 −39.4 s (22 → 23 loads).

Example 3 — why the derate does not slow the loaded climb. On R05 (grade 9.0 %, rolling resistance 3 %) a loaded HT220 (385 t) has a power-limited speed of 0.8×1800 kW / (385 000 kg × 9.81 m/s² × 0.12) ≈ 3.2 m/s ≈ 11.4 km/h, below both the site limit (40 km/h) and the cap (20 km/h). The whole 120.6 s falls on the empty descent, where the retarding limit for an empty truck (40 km/h) is halved.

Example 4 — rain and dust. Rain: EX02 1564.1 → 2048.7 s, EX03 1914.7 → 2255.2 s. Dust on R03: EX03 1914.7 → 2042.7 s.

15. Key takeaways

  • The ODD is what the truck system is designed for; the operational boundaries are where the site uses it; the operating conditions are where things stand now. The boundaries must fit inside the ODD, and the conditions are what must be watched.
  • A site can be stricter than the ODD, never looser. A restriction above the ODD cap should be impossible to enter.
  • The ODD map is computed from segment attributes and conditions: inside, inside only derated, or outside, each with reasons. At the reference mine the baseline excludes only the MIA road R16.
  • An exit is detected on the truck (reactive, immediate) and in the control room (forecast, pattern). The truck acts first within its grant; central decisions act on segments and never relax a truck's decision.
  • Unknown is outside. With CT3 down, R19, R20 and R24 are unresolved until coverage is confirmed.
  • R05 is the main ramp and the most fragile: high multipath, marginal coverage and the steepest grade. In rain or very poor geometry the west ramp R24 stays inside the ODD where R05 does not, at the price of more traffic at Intersection V.
  • Restrictions carry no delay code but cost cycle time; record them with reason and review time so that the cost is visible.
