1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

At 14:05 on a DAY shift, DT17 is climbing the main ramp R05 with ore on mission M-20481 when its fire detection system raises an alarm (SCN-FIRE-DT17). Five seconds later DT17 begins a controlled stop. At ten seconds the AMS has created an exclusion zone around it and holds the other traffic on R05. The AHS Controller acknowledges the alarm and calls the Emergency Response Team (ERT) and the Pit Supervisor. Two minutes later the Pit Supervisor agrees to send pit traffic out over the west ramp R24 while R05 is blocked. The ERT reaches the edge of the zone ten minutes after the alarm. Nobody is hurt.

The next morning the Pit Supervisor opens the hazard log. HZ-01, "fire on a loaded truck on the main ramp", is there with six controls, and the event used four of them in the order the log predicts. But the review raises questions that the log does not answer well:

  • The loaded trucks rerouted over R24 passed Intersection V, where light vehicles from the MIA gate cross to the fuel bay. The log has an entry for that, but its only controls are a radio broadcast and a briefing. Is that enough?
  • Which of the six controls are engineered, and which work only if somebody remembers a procedure?
  • Who owns each control, and who checks in the field that it still exists?
  • The fire happened on R05. What would have happened if R24 had been closed for maintenance at the time?
  • What must happen to the log now that a real event has tested it?

This chapter is about the work that produces such a log and keeps it true. It covers:

  • a structured way of finding hazards (guide-word HAZOP and FMEA) on the M-20481 route and in its operations;
  • the hazard-log entry: cause, consequence, owner, controls and the architecture element that provides each control;
  • risk matrices, and why their scales are site decisions that cannot be copied from a standard;
  • the bow-tie of the fire-on-ramp hazard;
  • the events that force a review of the log, and how a review feeds change management.

The hazard log built here is the input of the safety-case evidence mapping in ch-04-10. Every rating, scale and band in this chapter is an illustrative assumption [A]; none is a risk acceptance criterion.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Apply a structured hazard identification method (guide-word HAZOP and FMEA) to the M-20481 route from EX02-L to C01 and record each hazard with cause, consequence, existing controls and owner in a hazard log.
  2. Construct a risk matrix whose likelihood and severity scales are explicitly labelled [A] site decisions, and explain why such scales cannot be copied from a standard as thresholds.
  3. Trace every hazard-log entry to the architecture element that controls it (FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS, independent safety function, onboard safety-related system, people) and flag hazards controlled only by procedure.
  4. Analyse a bow-tie for the fire-on-ramp hazard, separating preventive controls (inspection, leak and hot-surface management) from mitigating controls (suppression, stop-location logic, exclusion zone, ERT response).
  5. Specify the triggers that force a hazard-log review (design change, map change such as the addition of the west ramp R24, new equipment class, incident) and link them to change management.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Identify operational hazards at Intersection X, the EX02 loading area and access point AP-02 that arise from mixed traffic, shift changes and maintenance access, and contribute them to a hazard workshop.
  2. Explain how a hazard-log control becomes a step in a site procedure (for example positive communication before entering AA-North) and who owns its periodic review.
  3. Report a near miss with enough detail (location, assets, time, conditions, roles) that it can update the hazard log.
  4. Assess the hazards of an operational change before it is used in production, such as routing pit traffic over the west ramp R24 through Intersection V past the park-up PK1 and fuel bay FB1.
  5. Verify in the field that controls recorded in the hazard log (windrows, signage and radio channels at access points AP-02 and AP-04, gate control at AP-01) exist and work, and record the findings.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

In a manned haul fleet, one control appears in almost every hazard: the driver. The driver sees the person at the shovel, hears the radio call, smells the hot brakes and decides. A hazard analysis of a manned fleet can lean on that control, sometimes too much. In an autonomous fleet the driver is gone, and the functions that the driver performed are spread over several systems and several people. Perception in the ADS detects the person. The AMS keeps a light vehicle and a truck apart. The VCS monitors the brakes. The onboard fire system detects the fire. The AHS Controller answers the radio. The Pit Supervisor decides whether pit traffic may use the west ramp. No one of them sees the whole picture.

Three conversations at the reference mine show what happens when hazard analysis does not follow that spread:

  • After a hot-surface finding, the Maintenance Planner says the fire hazard is "covered by the suppression system". Suppression acts after a fire has started. It does not make a fire less likely, and it does nothing about the ERT's exposure on a ramp that is still in use.
  • After a person was seen near EX01, a controller says that pedestrians are "the ADS's problem". Perception is one mitigating control. Access control at the access points, induction and the area remote stop are others, and they belong to other elements and other people.
  • The planner wants to send pit traffic over R24 next week while Survey reworks R05 (SCN-MAP-R05). Nobody can say which hazard-log entries that change touches, because the log records hazards by name, not by place.

Each conversation is a missing link: between a hazard and its causes, between a control and the element that provides it, between a place and the hazards that live there. The method of this chapter keeps those links in the log.

The legal and normative frame of hazard management is the site's, not the handbook's. ISO 17757:2019, Earth-moving machinery and mining — Autonomous and semi-autonomous machine system safety, addresses the safety of autonomous and semi-autonomous machine systems across their life cycle [F] (SRC-STD-ISO17757). The Western Australian code of practice Safe mobile autonomous mining in Western Australia covers risk management, hazard controls and emergency preparedness for mobile autonomous systems [F] (SRC-COP-WA-SMAM-2015), and the NSW Resources Regulator's Guideline - Autonomous Mobile Mining Plant covers the risks of autonomous mobile plant and their control [F] (SRC-COP-NSW-AMMP-2020). What these documents, or the mining work health and safety legislation of a given state, require of a hazard log, a risk matrix or a review cycle is not stated in this handbook [TBV]. The method, the field names and the reference-mine content below are the handbook's own [H].

3. Terminology and foundations

Hazard. A potential source of harm: the loaded truck that can catch fire, the truck that can strike a person in a loading area. Harm is injury or damage to health; the handbook also records damage to equipment and loss of the haul route as consequences, because they change what people must do next.

Hazardous event (top event). The moment control is lost: "fire on a loaded truck on R05". It sits in the middle of a bow-tie. Causes (threats) lead to it; consequences follow from it.

Risk. The combination of how likely harm is and how severe it would be. This chapter treats risk as an ordinal class read from a matrix, not as a number (section 4.4).

Control. Anything that makes a hazardous event less likely or its consequences less severe. A preventive control acts on the causes, on the left of the bow-tie. A mitigating control acts after the event, on the right. An engineered control works without anyone having to remember it: the suppression system, the AMS area lock, a windrow. A procedural control (administrative control) works only when a person follows a procedure: an inspection, a read-back, a briefing.

Hazard log. The living record of hazards, their causes, consequences, controls, owners, ratings and status. It is "living" because it changes whenever the operation changes.

Initial and residual risk. In this handbook the initial risk is the rating judged without the controls listed in the entry; the residual risk is the rating judged with them [H]. Both are judgements, and both are recorded so that a reviewer can see how much the controls are being credited with.

Risk matrix. A table that turns a likelihood class and a severity class into a risk class. Its scales and bands are site decisions [A].

Hazard identification (HAZID). The structured search for hazards. HAZOP (hazard and operability study) applies guide words such as NO, MORE, LESS or LATE to the parameters of a design or operation. FMEA (failure modes and effects analysis) works bottom-up from the failure modes of components. Bow-tie analysis draws one hazardous event with its causes and preventive controls on the left and its consequences and mitigating controls on the right. These are established methods with their own published guidance; which guidance and edition a site uses is outside this handbook's source register [TBV].

Near miss. An event that could have caused harm but did not. In this chapter it is mainly an input to the log.

Owner. Each hazard has one hazard owner, the role accountable for the entry being true and reviewed. Each control has a control owner, the role that keeps it working. At the reference mine owners are roles from mine.yaml, not named persons [H].

Review trigger. An event that reopens the log before its periodic review: a design change, a map change, a new equipment class, an incident or a near miss [H].

4. Technical explanation

4.1 Scope: what is being analysed

A hazard analysis starts with a description of what it covers. FIG-04-01-01 shows the process this chapter follows. Its first step, scope, is the one most often skipped.

The scope of the reference-mine log [H] is autonomous haulage on mission M-20481 — EX02-L to C01 via R08, R06, R05, R01 and R02, 4.50 km loaded — and the operations around it: the four loading areas of AA-North, Intersection X, the access points AP-01, AP-02 and AP-04, and the control room CR1. The scope names the architecture elements in play (FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS, the remote-stop path, the onboard safety-related systems, the manned-vehicle devices and the people) and the roles of mine.yaml. It also states what is outside: the waste route beyond Intersection Y and the dump D01, and the crusher plant itself. Those need their own entries before anyone relies on the log there.

The scope is written down because it decides what a missing hazard means. If the log says nothing about AP-04, is that because AP-04 was analysed and found harmless, or because nobody looked? Section 4.7 shows how a change moves the boundary.

4.2 Finding hazards: guide words and failure modes

No single method finds every hazard. The handbook combines four sources [H]:

  1. Guide-word HAZOP on the route and its operations. The route is split into nodes (loading area, approach, pit floor, main ramp, crest and access point, central road, Intersection X, crusher pad). For each node the team takes the parameters that matter — speed, position, braking, permission, communication, visibility — and applies each guide word in turn.
  2. FMEA on the components that carry a control: the position reporting unit, the stop receiver, the fire suppression system.
  3. Field walks with the people who work there: Shovel Operators at EX02, Field Technicians, Survey at the crest.
  4. Records: near-miss reports, incident reports, the scenarios of scenario-registry.yml used as structured "what-if" questions.

A few rows of the HAZOP on the main-ramp node show how guide words turn into entries:

Node Parameter Guide word Deviation Hazard-log entry
R05, loaded uphill Heat MORE Hot surface contacts leaking fluid HZ-01 fire on a loaded truck
R05, empty downhill Braking LESS Brake or retarder cannot hold the descent HZ-06 degraded braking
R05, lower ramp Position OTHER THAN Truck believes it is somewhere it is not HZ-05 integrity loss
R05 / R06 Permission AS WELL AS Light vehicle and truck in the same area HZ-03 manned vehicle under a permission
R05 / R06 Permission LATE Grant arrives after the request was withdrawn HZ-03 (cause: stale grant)
Crest P1, AP-02 Communication NO Driver does not call the controller HZ-12 at AP-04, the same deviation at the new access point
CR1, 18:00 Information PART OF Handover transfers some open items, not all HZ-08 open item lost

The guide words are prompts. A deviation that leads nowhere is recorded as "considered, no hazard" so that a reviewer can see it was looked at. A deviation that leads to an existing entry adds a cause to it rather than a duplicate.

FMEA works from the other end. For LV07's position reporting unit (PRU):

Reports stale Old position shown as current Same, but less visible Message age CTL-16
Wrong position Vehicle shown elsewhere AMS may grant a path near LV07 Radio check by the controller CTL-14 read-back; CTL-09 perception
Intermittent Position jumps Nuisance holds; controller workload Pattern on the HMI HZ-13 alarm flood

The third row is the dangerous one: a failure that looks like normal operation. It is controlled only by a procedure and by perception, which is why the read-back stays in the log even though the digital permission exists (ch-02-07).

Two choices in this structure are deliberate. Controls are kept in a catalogue with their own IDs, and an entry refers to them. "The ramp" cannot be searched when R24 opens; "R05" can.

The log of LAB-04-01-01 holds 14 hazards and a catalogue of 44 controls in use, with 48 links between them; two controls — the induction rule CTL-08 and the perception stop CTL-09 — serve more than one hazard. Twenty-nine controls are engineered and fifteen procedural.

4.4 Risk matrices and why their scales are site decisions

A risk matrix has two ordinal scales and a table of bands. With likelihood class ℓ and severity class s, the risk class is a lookup, not a product:

R(ℓ,s)=Bs,ℓ,ℓ∈{0,…,4}, s∈{0,…,3}

The base matrix of the lab [A] has five likelihood classes (Rare, Unlikely, Possible, Likely, Almost certain), four severity classes (Minor, Moderate, Major, Catastrophic) and four risk classes (low, medium, high, very high). FIG-04-01-03 places the 14 hazards on it twice: before and after their controls.

Initially three hazards are very high, nine high and two medium. With the listed controls none is very high, three are high, ten medium and one low. The picture says something the table hides: the controls mostly move hazards to the left (less likely), rarely down (less severe). A truck that strikes a person is catastrophic whether perception is fitted or not; what the controls change is how often it is expected to happen.

Why can these scales not be copied from a standard? Four reasons.

  1. The words have no meaning until a site defines them. "Unlikely" per truck-hour, per shift or per year of a 40-truck fleet are different classes. "Major" must say whose harm and how much. Only the site knows its exposure.
  2. The bands are the site's acceptance decision. Which cell is "high", and what "high" obliges people to do, is a statement of what the site will tolerate. Whether and how any standard or regulator defines such criteria for autonomous mining is [TBV], and the handbook asserts none.
  3. The result depends on the bands. The lab's alternative matrix [A] is stricter only in the two top severity rows. It changes the class of 11 of the 14 hazards (worked example 3). A hazard that is "medium" under one matrix and "high" under another has not changed; only the rule has.
  4. Matrices compress. Ten hazards share "medium". The matrix does not say which of them to work on first; the bow-tie and the evidence (ch-04-10) do.

Two practices follow [R]. Never multiply the indices (ℓ×s) and treat the product as a risk number: the classes are ordinal, so "2 × 3 = 6" carries no information that the cell does not. And always record the scales, their definitions and the matrix version with the log, so that a rating can be read the same way a year later.

4.5 Tracing controls to architecture elements

Each control in the catalogue names the element that provides it, using the eight columns of the function allocation matrix of ch-02-03: FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS, independent safety function (ISF), onboard safety-related system (OSS), manned-vehicle device (MVD) and people — plus a ninth element, SITE, for fixed infrastructure such as windrows, signage and barriers [H] (the allocation matrix of ch-02-03 counts them under people). The log marks a windrow as engineered and a sign as procedural, because a sign works only through a driver's compliance [H].

Counting by element (LAB-04-01-01):

Element Hazards with a control there Controls Engineered Procedural Preventive Mitigating
FMS 0 0 0 0 0 0
AMS 10 13 13 0 10 3
ADS 8 5 5 0 3 2
VCS 3 3 3 0 3 0
ISF 1 1 1 0 0 1
OSS 2 2 2 0 0 2
MVD 3 4 4 0 3 1
People 11 13 0 13 10 3
SITE 3 3 1 2 2 1

Three readings matter. The FMS controls no hazard. It plans production; at the reference mine it has no safety function [H], and a log that credited it with one would contradict the allocation of ch-02-03. People appear in 11 of 14 hazards, always through procedural controls. That is not a weakness in itself, but it is where the log depends on attention, training and staffing (ch-04-10). The AMS provides more engineered controls than any other element — area locks, fail-closed permissions, exclusion zones, handover summaries — so an AMS defect or a wrong AMS configuration touches many entries at once.

Four flags turn the log into a work list [H]:

Flag Meaning Hazards
Procedure only Every control is procedural HZ-11, HZ-12
Uncontrolled No control recorded yet HZ-14
No preventive control Nothing acts on the causes HZ-10, HZ-14
No mitigating control Nothing acts once the event has happened HZ-06, HZ-07, HZ-11, HZ-12, HZ-14

A flag is a question, not a verdict. HZ-07 (a stopped truck moves while a Field Technician works at it) has four preventive controls — protective reservation, isolation, personal isolation, staged restart — and no mitigating one, because nothing can usefully happen after the truck has moved. HZ-06 is different: if the stopping capability is lost, every stop path is lost with it, because the VCS and the brakes are common to all of them (ch-02-04). The flag says that the remote stop must not be credited as a mitigation for HZ-06, and the log does not credit it. HZ-10 (tyre failure) has only mitigating controls; the missing preventive control — inspection and pressure management — is a real gap in this log [A].

4.6 The bow-tie of the fire-on-ramp hazard

The bow-tie draws HZ-01 with its causes on the left and its consequences on the right, each path crossed by the controls that act on it:

Left: causes Preventive controls Top event Mitigating controls Right: consequences
Fuel or hydraulic leak onto a hot surface CTL-01 inspection (people, procedural); CTL-02 shielding and hose routing (VCS, engineered) Fire on a loaded truck on R05 CTL-03 detection and suppression (OSS); CTL-04 controlled stop with stop-location logic (ADS); CTL-05 exclusion zone, hold or reroute (AMS); CTL-06 ERT response under positive communication (people) Fire grows; truck lost
Electrical fault CTL-01 CTL-03, CTL-04 Responders exposed on a live ramp
CTL-05, CTL-06 Ramp blocked; pit isolated if R24 is unavailable

Reading the bow-tie left to right is reading the scenario timeline: the preventive controls act months before the event, the mitigating ones in the seconds and minutes after it. The order of the mitigating controls matters, and so does what each one depends on:

  • CTL-03 and CTL-04 act on the truck and need neither the network nor any person. Nothing central can delay them (ch-02-03, section 6.4).
  • CTL-05 needs the AMS, the site wireless network to the trucks behind DT17, and a correct map of who is where.
  • CTL-06 needs people, radios, a permission to enter, and an ERT that knows how to approach an unmanned truck.

Two escalation factors — conditions that weaken a control — belong in the entry: R24 closed while R05 is blocked, which removes the reroute and isolates the pit (section 6.3); and a suppression system in fault on the truck concerned. A bow-tie that lists controls without their escalation factors invites more credit than the controls deserve.

Bow-ties are qualitative here. Fault trees and event trees that put numbers on the right-hand side are the subject of ch-04-04 and ch-04-08; the fire response itself, including where DT17 should stop, is the subject of ch-04-07.

4.7 Review triggers and change management

A log is reviewed on a calendar, but it goes out of date on events. The handbook names five triggers [H]:

Trigger Example at the reference mine What the review asks
Design change New AMS release changes how permissions expire Which controls changed behaviour?
Map change R24 and R25 added (mine 0.2.0); R05 closed for rework (SCN-MAP-R05) Which places are newly used, and which entries cover them?
New equipment class Battery-electric trucks (Volume 09) Which causes and consequences are new?
Incident SCN-FIRE-DT17 Did the controls act as recorded? Are ratings still credible?
Near miss Dust at EX02 hides a light vehicle (HZ-14) Is this a new hazard or a new cause?

The lab makes the map trigger concrete. Close R05 and reroute every loading unit to its default destination with ahs_sim.mine. Pit traffic now uses R09, W, R24, P3 (with AP-04), R25, V and R12. Eight entries are up for review, because their location is on the new routes or on the closed segment (HZ-01, HZ-03, HZ-04, HZ-05, HZ-06, HZ-10, HZ-11, HZ-12). Two newly used places, the bench ramp R09 and Intersection W, are in no entry at all. The exposure also changes: ch-01-07 counts 145.5 haul-truck passes per hour through Intersection V with R05 closed, against none with R05 open (LAB-01-07-01), which is the exposure behind HZ-11's likelihood rating.

The check is necessary but not sufficient. R24 is "covered" by HZ-11 and HZ-12, but neither is about a fire on a loaded truck climbing a ramp. HZ-01 lists R05 only. Once R24 carries loaded trucks, the review must add R24 to HZ-01's location and ask whether its controls work there — for example whether the reroute option still exists when the fire is on R24 itself. A location check finds where to look; people still have to look.

Every trigger enters change management (ch-03-08 for configuration and change management; ch-12-09 for map changes): the change is not used in production until the review is complete and the residual risks are accepted by the hazard owners. For a temporary change, such as the R24 reroute during the R05 rework, the review may accept procedural controls for a defined period, with the period and its end written into the entry [R].

4.8 Trade-offs and limits

  • Granularity. One entry per hazardous event keeps the log readable; one entry per cause makes it traceable but long. The lab uses one per event, with causes listed.
  • Shared controls. A catalogue makes common dependence visible but tempts reuse of a control in an entry where it does not really act. Each link must be justified in the entry.
  • Location by ID. It makes map changes searchable but misses hazards that are not tied to a place, such as an alarm flood in CR1. The lab records CR1 as covering every area.
  • Qualitative ratings. They are quick and transparent and can be inconsistent between teams. Calibration workshops with worked examples reduce, but do not remove, the spread.
  • The log is not the safety case. It says what is believed to control each hazard. Whether that belief is backed by evidence is the question of ch-04-10.

5. Responsibilities and interface boundaries

Element Role in hazard identification and the log [H]
FMS Supplies production plans and assignments that define exposure (how many trucks, where, how often); provides no control in this log
AMS Provides most engineered controls (permissions, locks, zones, holds, handover summary); its event log is the record of controls acting
ADS Provides vehicle-side controls (perception stop, integrity monitoring, controlled stop, descent speed); reports stop reasons
VCS Provides platform controls (brake monitoring, isolation state); its stopping capability is common to every stop path
Independent safety function Provides the area remote stop as a mitigating control that does not depend on the FMS → AMS → ADS → VCS chain
Onboard safety-related systems Provide fire and tyre detection and suppression; their events start the mitigating sequence
Manned-vehicle devices Provide position reports and terminal confirmation; their failure modes are hazard causes
People Pit Supervisor: hazard owner for most entries, convenes reviews, accepts residual risk for the shift's operations; Maintenance Planner: owner of equipment hazards and of inspections; Survey: owner of localisation and road-edge hazards; AHS Controller: operates controls, reports near misses, raises review triggers; Field Technician, Shovel Operator, ERT: carry out procedural controls and verify field controls

The boundary that matters most is between owning a hazard and providing a control. The Pit Supervisor owns HZ-03; the AMS provides the area lock, LV07's terminal the confirmation, the controller the read-back. The owner cannot fix an AMS behaviour, and the AMS supplier does not know whether drivers read back. The log is where the two meet.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — the log between events

In normal operation the log is quiet but not idle [H]. Each entry has a periodic review date. Each control has an owner and a verification task: the windrows on R05 are inspected by Survey, the signage and radio channel at AP-02 and AP-04 and the gate at AP-01 are checked by the Pit Supervisor's field verification, the remote-stop receivers are checked by maintenance. A verification that finds a control missing is a finding against the entry, not a maintenance note. Near-miss reports are read against the log every week: a report that matches an existing cause updates the entry's history; one that does not becomes a candidate entry, as HZ-14 did.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-FIRE-DT17 through the hazard log

This timeline is shared by sections 6, 7 and 8. Registry key events are T+0, 5, 10, 15, 60 and 600 s; rows marked [A] are added by this chapter. The fourth column names the hazard-log control acting at each step.

T+5 s DT17 begins a controlled stop; stop-location logic evaluated ADS, VCS CTL-04 —

Three points stand out. Up to T+10 s every control that acted was engineered and onboard or in the AMS; the first person in the sequence, the AHS Controller, acknowledged the alarm at T+15 s, by which time the onboard and AMS controls (CTL-03 to CTL-05) had already acted. At T+120 s a second hazard became live — HZ-11, rerouted traffic meeting manned traffic at Intersection V — and its only controls are a radio broadcast and a pre-start briefing. The fire itself was handled by the controls of the fire entry (HZ-01); its side effect was handled by a procedure-only entry (HZ-11). And the review the next day is not optional: an incident is a trigger.

The incident review starts from the lab's list: hazards linked to SCN-FIRE-DT17 or sharing a segment or node with it — HZ-01, HZ-03, HZ-05, HZ-06 and HZ-10. For each, the review asks whether the controls acted as recorded (here they did for HZ-01), whether the event revealed a cause or consequence the entry lacks, and whether the residual rating is still credible. For HZ-01 the review adds an escalation factor (R24 unavailable) and, after the R24 reroute, it reopens HZ-11.

6.3 Degraded operation — the same fire with R24 closed

Repeat the event on a day when R24 is closed for windrow work [A]. The exclusion zone closes R05, and with R24 also closed there is no route out of AA-North: hold_or_reroute returns "hold" for all four loading units. Thirty-seven producing trucks (baseline_allocation) wait in the pit until R05 reopens, and the ERT reaches the zone through an isolated pit that still contains manned shovels, dozers and their operators.

None of this is in HZ-01 as first written. The entry assumed a reroute, because R24 exists. The degraded condition — one control's precondition missing — needs either its own entry or an escalation factor in HZ-01 with an operating rule, for example that R24 and R05 are not closed at the same time without a pit evacuation plan [R]. The review decides; the log records the decision and its owner.

A second degraded case is a control out of service: DT17's suppression system reports a fault before the shift. CTL-03 is then missing for that truck. The log does not decide what to do — withdraw the truck, or run it under conditions — but it tells the Maintenance Planner which hazard loses which control, and that the residual rating of HZ-01 no longer applies to DT17 until the fault is cleared.

6.4 Controls spread over elements — SCN-PERSON-EX01 and SCN-POSCOM-LV07

Two more registered scenarios show why the log traces controls to elements.

In SCN-PERSON-EX01 (HZ-02) a truck approaching EX01 detects an object classified as a person and stops (T+0 s, CTL-09, ADS); the controller finds no matching position report or permission (T+10 s); and escalates to a remote stop of the area (T+15 s, CTL-10, ISF). The preventive controls that failed are the access permission (CTL-07, AMS) and the induction rule (CTL-08, people). The mitigating controls that worked belong to two other elements. A log that recorded "pedestrian hazard — ADS" would have hidden that the failure was at the access point.

In SCN-POSCOM-LV07 (HZ-03) the controls form a chain across four elements: AMS locks the area and confirms it clear (CTL-12), AMS refuses stale or unacknowledged grants (CTL-13), the driver reads back (CTL-14, people), the terminal confirms (CTL-15, MVD), once inside, position reports keep the AMS informed (CTL-16, MVD), and perception remains as a last mitigation (CTL-09, ADS). Its residual likelihood is the lowest class in the log (Rare, shared with HZ-07) because six controls in four elements would all have to fail. That rating is only as good as the evidence that each of the six works — the question of ch-04-10.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

  • A machine-readable log. The log is YAML with stable IDs, not a spreadsheet of free text. That allows the checks of section 4.5, the change review of section 4.7 and the evidence mapping of ch-04-10. The price is discipline: every location must be a map ID, every owner a role.
  • Controls as catalogue items with an element. It makes the element table and the flags possible and shows common dependence. The price is the temptation to over-link (section 4.8).
  • Initial and residual ratings both recorded. The difference is the credit given to the controls. A reviewer who sees catastrophic hazards moved two likelihood classes by one procedural control knows where to push.
  • Engineered first. When a review finds a procedure-only hazard, the first question is whether an engineered control exists that the architecture can provide [R]. For HZ-11 it does: the AMS can reserve Intersection V while R05 is closed and admit manned crossings only under a permission (CTL-45, proposed). For HZ-12 the light vehicles already carry position reporting units, so the AMS can raise an alarm when one enters AA-North without a permission (CTL-46, proposed). Worked example 4 shows the effect on the flags.
  • No numbers in the matrix definition that look like thresholds. Class definitions are words with site-defined meaning [A]; the handbook gives no frequencies or harm thresholds.

7.2 Failure modes and mitigations

The analysis itself can fail. Its typical failure modes [H]:

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

On the shared timeline, each control depends on different interfaces [H]:

  • T+0 s to T+5 s — OSS → VCS and ADS onboard. The detection and the controlled stop need no network and no AMS. The interface that matters is the classification of the fire output as a safe-state request (REQ-02-04-006).
  • T+10 s — ADS → AMS over the site wireless network (R05 has marginal coverage in mine.yaml). The exclusion zone depends on that report arriving. If it does not, CTL-05 is late, and the trucks behind DT17 are protected only by their own perception and by fail-closed path ends. A log entry should say so.
  • T+15 s and T+600 s — people and radio. These controls have no timing guarantee at all; the drill records of ch-04-10 are the only evidence of how long they take.
  • After the event — the incident review needs the AMS event log with synchronised timestamps (ch-02-07) to see which control acted when.

No stop time, response time or distance is given here; they are site and design values (ch-04-05, ch-04-07).

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

The hazard log is not on the controller's HMI, and it should not be: during SCN-FIRE-DT17 the controller needs the alarm, the zone and the radio, not a document. What the controller does see is the log's controls at work: the fire alarm with its source, the exclusion zone on the map, the hold on R05, the permission requests of the ERT [H]. What the log needs from the control room afterwards is the record: alarm times, acknowledgements, commands with person and role (REQ-01-06-002), the shift log entry explaining the R24 decision, and near-miss reports. The procedures the controller follows refer to hazard IDs, so that a step can be traced back to the entry it implements.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — from the fire on R05 to a reviewed log

The steps follow the shared timeline of section 6.2 [H].

  1. AHS Controller (T+15 s): acknowledge the fire alarm; notify the ERT and the Pit Supervisor. From now on record decisions and their reasons in the shift log as they happen.
  2. Pit Supervisor (T+120 s): decide hold or reroute. Before approving the R24 reroute, open the log at HZ-11 and HZ-12 and confirm that their controls are in place now: the temporary rule at V broadcast, light-vehicle drivers told, AP-04 signage up. If they are not, hold pit traffic until they are.
  3. AHS Controller (T+120 s): broadcast the temporary rule at Intersection V on the AHS and site channels (CTL-40); record the broadcast time.
  4. AHS Controller (T+600 s): handle the ERT's entry under positive communication (CTL-07, CTL-12, CTL-14).
  5. AHS Controller (end of shift): file the incident record with times from the AMS event log; list the controls that acted, by ID; list any control that did not act as expected. Hand over the open items (ch-01-06).
  6. Anyone involved (within the shift): report each near miss seen during the event — location by map ID, assets, time, conditions (weather, light, dust), roles involved, what nearly happened. No names are needed for the log; the aim is the hazard, not the person.
  7. Pit Supervisor (next day): convene the incident review with the Maintenance Planner (owner of HZ-01) and the control owners. Review the entries the lab lists for the scenario (section 6.2) and HZ-11. Record changes to causes, consequences, escalation factors, controls and ratings, each with its reason.
  8. Hazard owners: accept or reject the updated residual risks; assign actions with owners and dates; set the end date of any temporary acceptance of procedure-only controls.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

The fire's operational cost is coded in the time usage model of ch-01-05 (GMG Time Usage Model [F], SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020). DT17 is in Downtime from the alarm (DLY-DN-01, breakdown). Trucks held outside the exclusion zone are in Operating Delay, DLY-OD-14 (held by an incident exclusion zone). Trucks rerouted over R24 have no delay code: their longer cycles are travel. The ore loops of EX01 and EX02 each lose 236.0 s per cycle via R24, while the waste loops of EX03 and EX04 gain 39.4 s, because the R24 route is shorter in time for them (hold_or_reroute). If R24 is also closed, every pit truck is held (DLY-OD-14) until R05 reopens.

The log itself has indicators [H], read monthly by the Pit Supervisor: hazards past their review date; procedure-only hazards and how long they have been so; field verifications done against planned; near-miss reports received and the share that changed an entry; actions from reviews overdue. None of these is a target in this handbook; a falling near-miss count may mean fewer near misses or fewer reports.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Controllers and Pit Supervisors need to read an entry and say which controls are theirs. Everyone who works in AA-North needs to know how to report a near miss and to see that reports change something. Workshop participants need the method (guide words, bow-tie) and calibration on the site's matrix with worked examples; ratings from untrained teams spread widely. Shovel Operators and Field Technicians are essential in workshops: they know the causes that never reach a report. Competency frameworks are the subject of ch-16-09.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-04-01-001 [H] The hazard log shall record, for every hazard, a stable ID, a title, its causes, its consequences, its location as reference-mine IDs, one hazard owner by role, the initial and residual ratings with the matrix version used, and its controls by ID. Rationale: each field supports a later step: controls act on causes and consequences, map changes find entries by location, reviews need an owner and ratings need their scale (sections 4.3 and 4.4). Verification: inspection; test (LAB-04-01-01 loads every entry into ahs_sim.safety.Hazard and checks the locations against mine.yaml).

REQ-04-01-002 [H] Every control shall be recorded once in a control catalogue with a stable ID, the architecture element that provides it, its kind (preventive or mitigating), whether it is engineered or procedural, and its control owner by role. Rationale: shared controls and common dependence are visible only when a control is one item (sections 4.3 and 4.5). Verification: inspection; test (the lab rejects unknown elements and kinds, and every catalogue item is used or proposed).

REQ-04-01-003 [H] The risk matrix shall be recorded with the log, with the definition of every likelihood and severity class and the bands, as a site decision with its version and approver. No class definition shall be taken from a standard as a threshold unless the site has verified the standard and the edition. Rationale: the classes have no meaning without the site's definitions, and the result depends on the bands (section 4.4). Verification: inspection; analysis (matrix sensitivity, LAB-04-01-01).

REQ-04-01-004 [H] The log shall report, at every review, the hazards with no control, with procedural controls only, with no preventive control and with no mitigating control. A hazard with procedural controls only shall not be accepted for production use without an end date for the acceptance and a recorded decision on engineered alternatives. Rationale: these hazards depend entirely on attention, or have an open side of the bow-tie (sections 4.5 and 7.1). Verification: test (LAB-04-01-01 flags); inspection of acceptance records.

REQ-04-01-005 [H] Each mitigating control credited in a hazard entry shall state the conditions it depends on (escalation factors), including any component it shares with the failure it is meant to mitigate. Rationale: a control that fails with the hazard gives no protection; the stopping capability is common to every stop path (sections 4.5 and 4.6). Verification: inspection; analysis.

REQ-04-01-006 [H] A design change, a map change, a new equipment class, an incident and a near miss that matches no entry shall each trigger a review of the affected entries before the change is used in production or, for an incident, within a period set by the site. For a map change the affected entries shall include every entry whose location is on a closed segment or on a route newly used by autonomous trucks, and every newly used place without an entry shall be reported. Rationale: logs go out of date on events, not on dates (section 4.7). Verification: test (LAB-04-01-01 change review with R05 closed; incident review for SCN-FIRE-DT17); inspection of change records.

REQ-04-01-007 [H] Every field control in the log (windrows, signage, radio channels, gate control) shall have a verification task with an owner and a frequency, and a missing or defective field control shall be recorded as a finding against the hazard entries that credit it. Rationale: a control that exists only in the log is not a control (section 6.1). Verification: inspection of verification records; demonstration during a field walk.

REQ-04-01-008 [H] Site procedures that implement a control shall cite the control ID, and incident and near-miss records shall identify the controls that acted or failed by ID. Rationale: without the IDs neither a procedure change nor an incident can be traced back to the log (sections 6.2 and 8.3). Verification: inspection.

10. Verification and validation

Verification asks whether the log and the tools compute what they claim. make test CH=ch-04-01 runs the lab tests: every entry loads into the ahs_sim.safety types; IDs are unique and every catalogue control is used or proposed; ratings lie on the matrix scales and no control makes a rating worse; every scenario is registered and overlaps the hazard's location; every location exists in the reference mine; the counts, rankings, flags, bow-tie, matrix sensitivity, proposals, change review and incident review equal the reference values of the lab README; and the reroute penalties come from ahs_sim.

Validation asks whether the log describes the real operation. No test can show that; three activities approach it. Field verification checks that recorded controls exist (section 6.1). Incident and near-miss reviews check that controls acted as recorded and that the causes were in the log (section 6.2). Independent review checks the reasoning and the ratings (ch-04-10). The reference mine is fictional, so none is possible here; the lab only shows the mechanics. ch-18-07 treats incident investigation in depth.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Analysing elements, not the operation. Each supplier's analysis can be complete while the hazards at the boundaries are missed.
  • Treating the matrix as physics. Multiplying indices, or importing a matrix from another site or a document, and then reporting the result as if it were measured.
  • Crediting a control twice, or where it does not act. The remote stop does not mitigate a brake failure.
  • Locations in prose. "The ramp" cannot be found when R24 opens.
  • No escalation factors. A bow-tie that lists suppression without "suppression in fault" invites overconfidence.
  • Ratings without the matrix version. A "high" from last year's matrix and a "high" from this year's are not comparable.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Using a change before reviewing it. Routing pit traffic over R24 because it is possible, not because its hazards have been looked at.
  • Accepting procedure-only controls indefinitely. A temporary rule becomes permanent by default.
  • Not reporting near misses because "nothing happened", or reporting them with no place, time or conditions.
  • Treating the log as the safety department's document. Owners who do not know they own an entry do not review it.
  • Verifying on paper. Ticking "signage at AP-04" without walking to AP-04.

12. Worked examples

All values are computed by LAB-04-01-01 with ahs_sim 0.6.0 on mine version 0.3.0. Every rating and every matrix is [A].

Example 1 — the log in numbers. 14 hazards; 44 controls in use, 29 engineered and 15 procedural; 48 hazard–control links; 2 shared controls (CTL-08, CTL-09). Initial: 3 very high, 9 high, 2 medium. Residual: 0 very high, 3 high (HZ-11, HZ-12, HZ-14), 10 medium, 1 low (HZ-05).

Example 2 — reading HZ-01 on the matrix. Initial: Possible (2) × Catastrophic (3) → B3,2 = high. Residual: Unlikely (1) × Major (2) → B2,1 = medium. The controls are credited with one likelihood class (preventive: inspection and shielding) and one severity class (mitigating: suppression, controlled stop, exclusion zone, ERT). The severity credit is the one to question: it assumes that CTL-03 to CTL-06 all act.

Example 3 — matrix sensitivity. Replace the base matrix by the alternative [A], which differs only in the Major and Catastrophic rows. Eleven of the fourteen hazards change class: eight from medium to high (HZ-02, HZ-03, HZ-04, HZ-06, HZ-07, HZ-08, HZ-09, HZ-13) and three from high to very high (HZ-11, HZ-12, HZ-14). The counts become 3 very high, 8 high, 2 medium, 1 low. HZ-01 (Unlikely × Major) stays medium, because both matrices put that cell in medium. Nothing about the mine changed.

Example 4 — removing the procedure-only flags. Add CTL-45 (AMS reserves Intersection V while R05 is closed; manned crossings only under a permission) to HZ-11, and CTL-46 (AMS alarm when a position report enters AA-North without a permission) to HZ-12, re-rated [A] to Unlikely × Catastrophic. Both move from high to medium, and the procedure-only list becomes empty. HZ-11 keeps its "no mitigating control" flag, because CTL-45 is preventive.

Example 5 — the R24 change. With R05 closed, pit traffic newly uses AP-04, P3, R09, R12, R24, R25, V and W. Eight entries need review (HZ-01, HZ-03, HZ-04, HZ-05, HZ-06, HZ-10, HZ-11, HZ-12); R09 and W are in no entry. Cycle effect from hold_or_reroute: EX01 and EX02 +236.0 s per cycle (M-20481 from 1564.1 s to 1800.1 s; 4.50 km to 5.87 km loaded), EX03 and EX04 −39.4 s. With R24 also closed, all four loading units are held.

Example 6 — incident trigger. For SCN-FIRE-DT17 (R05, P1, P2) the incident review covers HZ-01 (linked), and HZ-03, HZ-05, HZ-06 and HZ-10 (same segment or nodes). HZ-11 is added by the review because the event used the R24 reroute.

15. Key takeaways

  • In an autonomous fleet the driver's controls are spread over systems and people; the log must say which element provides each control.
  • A hazard-log entry needs cause, consequence, location by map ID, one owner, initial and residual ratings with the matrix version, and controls by ID.
  • Risk matrices are site decisions [A]. The lab's alternative matrix changes the class of 11 of 14 hazards without any change to the mine.
  • The FMS controls no hazard; people appear in 11 of 14, always through procedural controls; the AMS provides most engineered controls.
  • Procedure-only, uncontrolled, no-preventive and no-mitigating flags turn the log into a work list.
  • A bow-tie's mitigating controls must state what they depend on; the remote stop is no mitigation for lost brakes.
  • Design changes, map changes, new equipment classes, incidents and near misses trigger reviews. Closing R05 puts eight entries up for review and exposes two unanalysed places.
