1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is 10:40 on a DAY shift [A]. DT17 is climbing the main ramp R05 with a full load of ore when its fire detection system raises an alarm. Within a quarter of a minute the AHS Controller in CR1 has four new things on the screens: an onboard alarm from DT17, an exclusion zone drawn by the AMS around the truck, a row of trucks held on and around R05, and — a minute later — an FMS message that the assignments of the EX02 trucks have been re-planned. Ten minutes later two Emergency Response Team vehicles drive in from the MIA along the same central road on which the AMS is now sending loaded trucks around the blocked ramp.

No single system did all of this, and none was designed to. The fire system detects and suppresses; the ADS stops the truck in a suitable place; the AMS protects the road and coordinates traffic; the FMS keeps the shovels working. The combined behaviour — a pit run through its west ramp with emergency vehicles threading through the rerouted traffic — exists only because these systems act on each other, and the people in CR1 must coordinate one response to something that arrives as many separate messages.

That raises questions that ch-01-04 could not answer with a single chain of elements:

  • Who owns behaviour that belongs to no one system?
  • Where does the AHS end, and what lies outside it that it still depends on?
  • What happens when one of these systems is upgraded and the others are not?
  • Whom does the controller call when a problem appears between two systems?

This chapter covers:

  • the characteristics that make an autonomous mine a system of systems, and its constituent systems at the reference mine;
  • the context boundary of the AHS and a classification of its external interfaces;
  • emergent behaviour across constituents, walked through with SCN-FIRE-DT17;
  • the version-compatibility risk created by independent release cycles, and where compatibility is checked;
  • what this means in CR1: one incident view, the right owner to call, and procedure reviews after any upgrade.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Explain the characteristics that make an autonomous mine a system of systems (operational and managerial independence of constituents, evolutionary development, emergent behaviour) and identify the constituents at the reference mine.
  2. Analyse SCN-FIRE-DT17 as an emergent, cross-system behaviour: list every constituent system that must act and the information each needs from the others.
  3. Draw the context boundary of the AHS at the reference mine and classify each external interface (plant, manned vehicles, survey, enterprise systems).
  4. Explain how the independent evolution of constituents (separate releases of FMS, AMS and vehicle software) creates version-compatibility risk, and identify where compatibility must be checked before deployment.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Explain to a Pit Supervisor why a change in one system (for example a new shovel assignment rule in the FMS) can produce behaviour in another (queueing at Intersection X) that neither system was designed to show on its own.
  2. Identify which constituent system's owner must be contacted when a cross-system problem appears in the control room, using the cancellation of M-20481 as the example.
  3. Describe how the controller recognises on the HMI that one event spans several systems (the fire on DT17 shown as an onboard alarm, an AMS exclusion zone and an FMS assignment change) and coordinates a single response.
  4. Explain why site procedures must be reviewed when any constituent system is upgraded, even if only one system changes.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Ch-01-04 drew the AHS as a chain — FMS → AMS → ADS → VCS — with supporting elements beside it. That picture is correct about responsibilities, but it hides three facts that every site discovers in its first year of autonomous operation.

First, the boxes in the chain are not parts of one machine. They are separate systems, often from different suppliers, running on different computers in different places, each with its own release plan, support contract and engineering team. The FMS keeps running if the trucks are parked; the trucks keep driving their granted paths for a while if the AMS stops answering; the fire suppression system on DT17 discharges whether or not anyone in CR1 has noticed.

Second, the most important behaviour at the reference mine is a property of the combination. Tonnes per hour, the queue at C01-Q, the waiting at Intersection X and the response to a fire are not produced by any one system.

Third, the systems change at different times. In a typical year the site may install a new FMS version, a new AMS version, a new ADS release on 40 trucks over ten days, new truck firmware and a new map every week. Each change is tested by its supplier; the combination that is actually running in the pit on a given day may never have been tested by anyone.

At the reference mine these facts show up as four conversations:

  • The Pit Supervisor: "The FMS team changed the dispatch rule last week. Nobody touched the AMS. Why are trucks now queueing at Intersection X?"
  • The AHS Controller: "FMS shows M-20481 as cancelled and DT17 is still driving. Do I call the FMS people, the AHS supplier or the network team?"
  • The AHS Controller again, during the fire on DT17: "I have an onboard alarm, an AMS exclusion zone, a row of held trucks and an FMS re-plan. Is that one event or four?"
  • The Maintenance Planner: "The truck firmware update is only a VCS change. Why does the control room want to review its procedures?"

The method of this chapter: treat the mine as a system of systems, draw its boundary deliberately, name an owner for every constituent and every cross-system behaviour that matters, and check compatibility whenever anything changes.

3. Terminology and foundations

System of systems (SoS). A system whose elements are themselves systems that can operate, and are managed, on their own. The handbook uses the term as defined in its glossary. Definitions in the systems-engineering literature differ in wording; the characteristics used in this chapter follow a widely cited formulation by Maier (1998, Systems Engineering 1(4); SRC-PUB-SOS-MAIER-1998 — bibliographic data verified, the list of characteristics [TBV]), and the taxonomy follows ISO/IEC/IEEE 21841:2019 (SRC-STD-ISO21841). The handbook relies on the characteristics only as a teaching frame, not as a normative definition.

Constituent system. One of the systems that together form the SoS. At the reference mine: FMS, AMS, ADS, VCS, onboard safety-related systems, the independent safety function, manned-vehicle devices — and the people and organisations who operate them [H]. A constituent system may have many deployed instances: one ADS design runs on 40 trucks.

Context boundary. The line that separates what the AHS is responsible for and controls the configuration of from what it depends on or informs but does not control [H]. Systems outside the boundary are external systems; the connections across it are external interfaces.

Emergent behaviour. Behaviour of the SoS that no constituent shows on its own and that arises from the interaction of constituents. It may be intended (production, a coordinated fire response) or unintended (a queue at Intersection X that no system was designed to create).

System owner. The organisation accountable for a constituent system's configuration, releases and support: a supplier for the product, and a named site owner for its deployment [H]. Owners are organisations, not handbook roles; the operational roles (AHS Controller, Pit Supervisor, Shovel Operator, Field Technician, Maintenance Planner, Survey, Emergency Response Team) contact them.

Integration authority. The site organisation accountable for the AHS as a whole: the constituent register, the context boundary, the compatibility of versions and the end-to-end behaviour of cross-system scenarios [H]. At the reference mine it sits in site AHS engineering [A].

Compatibility matrix. The approved list of version combinations of constituent systems that may run together [H]. A combination not in the matrix is not approved, however small the change.

Three terms from Volume 01 are used throughout. The interface control document (ICD) specifies each interface (ch-02-06). Configuration management keeps track of what is deployed where. Change management is the technical process by which a change is assessed, approved and deployed; it is different from the organisational change management of Volume 20.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 What makes an autonomous mine a system of systems

The SoS literature describes several characteristics that distinguish a system of systems from a large, complex system [TBV]. Four of them matter at the reference mine, and each can be checked against the site rather than taken on trust.

Operational independence of constituents. Each constituent can do useful work on its own. The FMS can plan the shift and report production when no truck is moving. An ADS on DT17 can bring the truck to a controlled stop and hold it without any central system. The fire detection and suppression system detects and discharges by itself. The remote stop acts regardless of the AMS and ADS (ch-01-04, REQ-01-04-005). Several of the handbook's safety principles depend on this independence.

Managerial independence. Each constituent is acquired, configured and maintained by its own owner. At the reference mine the FMS is supplied by an FMS supplier and run by the site's production-systems team; the AMS and ADS by an AHS supplier with site AHS engineering; the VCS and its electronic control units by the truck manufacturer with site maintenance; the site wireless network by the communications team [A]. Nobody owns everything, and no supplier sees the whole site. That is why the integration authority exists.

Evolutionary development. The SoS is never finished. Constituents are added, replaced and upgraded on their own schedules, and the mine itself changes underneath them: shovels move, roads are reworked, CT3 is relocated. The reference mine has already grown a second ramp (R24, mine 0.2.0). FIG-02-01-02 (section 4.6) shows one illustrative year of releases.

Emergent behaviour. The behaviour that matters most — tonnes per hour, safety of the mixed fleet, response to an emergency — is produced by the interaction of the constituents (section 4.5).

Geographic distribution, also listed in the literature [TBV], holds trivially here; its consequences are the subject of ch-02-02.

ISO/IEC/IEEE 21841:2019 names four taxa of SoS — directed, acknowledged, collaborative and virtual [F] (SRC-STD-ISO21841, catalogue and table of contents only). In the usual reading they differ in how much central authority exists over the constituents — from directed (built and run centrally for one purpose) through acknowledged (a recognised SoS owner, but constituents keep their own owners) and collaborative to virtual (no central authority) [TBV]. The reference mine is closest to an acknowledged SoS [A]: the integration authority sets the boundary, the compatibility matrix and the cross-system requirements, but it cannot redesign a supplier's product. Its levers are requirements, ICDs, acceptance tests and release approval — the subjects of this chapter, of ch-02-06 and of Volume 03.

People and procedures are constituents in the same sense as software: they operate independently, are managed separately (by mine operations, not by suppliers), evolve (rosters, training, new procedures) and contribute to emergent behaviour. Leaving them out of the SoS model is how procedure gaps go unseen.

The instance count matters. One ADS release is one design decision but 40 deployments, rolled out truck by truck. For days at a time the reference mine runs two ADS versions side by side (section 4.6).

4.3 The context boundary

FIG-02-01-01 draws the AHS as one system with its external systems around it. Inside the boundary are the constituents of section 4.2. Outside are five groups of systems that the AHS depends on or informs but whose configuration it does not control.

The boundary is a design decision, not a fact of nature. The handbook draws it with one test [H]: a system is inside if the AHS integration authority approves its configuration and it takes part in movement authority, in stopping or in the representation of people and manned vehicles to the AHS. By this test:

  • The FMS is inside, although ISO 23725:2024 (Autonomous system and fleet management system interoperability, edition 1, ISO/TC 82/SC 8) treats the FMS and the AHS as two systems with an interface between them for dispatching haul trucks and coordinating production information in surface mining [F] (SRC-STD-ISO23725, catalogue level). The handbook keeps the FMS inside its AHS boundary because the FMS's assignments drive every mission; the FMS–AMS interface is nevertheless a supplier boundary, and ch-02-05 treats what the standard can be cited for.
  • Manned-vehicle devices are inside; the manned vehicles are outside. LV07's PRU and field terminal are part of how the AHS knows about LV07 and grants it access. LV07 itself, its driver, DZ03 and the shovel EX02 are external: the AHS must represent and protect them but does not control them.
  • The site wireless network is outside [A]. CT1–CT3 carry every AMS–vehicle message, so the AHS depends on them completely, but at the reference mine they are owned by the communications team and also serve other site users. The dependency is managed through an interface (service levels, change notices) and through the failure-domain analysis of ch-02-02. A site that dedicates its network to the AHS could draw the line the other way.
  • The processing plant is outside. The control system of crusher C01 reports ready and not ready; the AHS decides what to do about it but does not run the plant (SCN-CRUSHER-C01-DOWN, ch-02-06).

The value of the test is not that it gives the only right answer, but that it forces the question for every system the AHS touches, and records the answer.

4.4 Classifying the external interfaces

Each external interface is classified on four attributes [H]: the class of external system, the direction of information, the criticality of the interface for the AHS, and the timing class in which the AHS uses it (ch-01-04, section 4.5). Criticality has three levels: safety-related (a failure can affect who may move where), production-critical (a failure stops or misdirects production) and informational (a failure degrades reports).

Every class has at least one safety-related or production-critical interface, so none may be treated as "just IT". The fail-closed column is an obligation on the constituent that receives the data, not on the external system: the plant need not know how the AHS reacts to a lost status message. And the timing classes span four orders of magnitude, so "the interface is up" is not one question but five.

4.5 Emergent behaviour across constituents

Emergence in an autonomous mine is not mysterious. It arises from a small number of mechanisms that can be named and looked for [H]:

  • Shared resources. Constituents compete for, or depend on, the same road, intersection, tipping bay, comms site or person. The AMS allocates roads; the FMS creates the demand for them.
  • Feedback between decision loops. The FMS adjusts assignments based on cycle times that the AMS's traffic decisions have produced; the AMS's traffic responds to the assignments. Two loops that are each stable can together oscillate or bunch.
  • Different timing classes. The FMS decides in minutes, the AMS in seconds, the ADS in tenths of a second. A decision taken on one time scale meets the consequence of another taken on a different one.
  • Different information. Each constituent acts on its own copy of the state, of a different age.

An intended emergence: the response to a fire. SCN-FIRE-DT17 crosses seven element groups in the scenario registry: onboard safety-related systems, VCS, ADS, AMS, FMS, the independent safety function and people. The fire response is designed piece by piece, in ch-04-07, ch-08-05 and ch-16-06, but its overall behaviour exists only at SoS level. Section 6 follows it on one timeline.

An unintended emergence: the reroute meets the emergency route. When the AMS holds R05 around DT17, the shortest remaining routes out of the pit run over the west ramp R24, through Intersection V and along R12 to Intersection X. Computed with ahs_sim on the baseline allocation without DT17 (36 trucks), Intersection V goes from no haul traffic to 141.5 passes per hour — one every 25.4 s (section 12, example 2). The ERT, called out from the MIA, drives G01 → V → X → P1 on R13, R12 and R01 (2016.4 m). Neither the AMS routing rule nor the ERT callout procedure is wrong; together they put emergency vehicles and rerouted loaded trucks on the same road at the same time. This interaction is visible only to someone who looks across constituents — which is the integration authority's job before the event and the controller's during it.

An unintended emergence from a rule change: queueing at Intersection X. The AMS reserves Intersection X for one crossing movement at a time [H]. At the baseline 155.5 passes per hour and an assumed 12 s per crossing reservation [A], X is occupied 51.8 % of the time and the mean headway is 23.2 s, so trucks arriving evenly would hardly ever wait. Suppose the FMS team introduces a new dispatch rule that re-balances trucks between shovels by sending the next empty trucks from C01 and D01 together to the shovel with the longest wait [A]. The rule is sensible for shovel utilisation and passes its own tests. But it makes trucks travel in groups. A group of four arriving at X together waits 0, 12, 24 and 36 s: 72 s in total. At ten such groups an hour that is 0.20 truck-hours of waiting per hour, or 2.4 truck-hours per 12 h shift (section 12, example 3). The FMS sees slightly longer cycles; the AMS sees reservations working correctly. Only someone who looks at both sees the cause. This is the case the Pit Supervisor raised in section 2 and that the controller must be able to explain (section 8).

The lesson for design is that emergent behaviour must be owned. The handbook assigns each registered scenario that crosses three or more constituents to a named owner in the integration authority, who maintains its end-to-end expectations and reruns it after every relevant change (REQ-02-01-008).

4.6 Independent evolution and version compatibility

FIG-02-01-02 shows an illustrative year of releases at the reference mine [A]. Each row is a constituent with its owners, its location and its release cadence; each dot is a release.

Three features of the figure carry the engineering argument.

Releases do not line up. The internal constituents release 13 software or firmware versions in the year — one every 4.0 weeks on average — and in two months (May and August in the figure) two constituents change in the same month. The map changes every week. There is no natural moment at which "the system" is released; the system is whatever combination is running.

The fleet is not uniform. An ADS release is rolled out truck by truck. At four trucks per day [A], the 40 trucks run two ADS versions side by side for 10 days. During those days the AMS must work correctly with both versions, the controller's HMI must show which truck runs which, and any behaviour that differs between versions — for example where a truck chooses to stop after a fire alarm — differs between trucks on the same road.

Compatibility is more than message syntax. Two versions are compatible only if they agree on (1) the messages and their fields (syntax), (2) the meaning of those fields (semantics: what "loaded" or "stopped" means), (3) the timing (timeouts, rates) and (4) the behaviour each side expects of the other (for example that a truck stops at the end of its granted path, not after it). An ADS release that changes the stop-location logic after a fire alarm changes no message at all, yet it changes where the AMS's exclusion zone must be, where the ERT approaches and what the controller expects to see. That is a behavioural incompatibility, and it is the kind most often missed.

Compatibility is therefore checked at four points [H]:

  1. Integration test before release approval. The candidate version is integrated with the versions currently deployed (and with the next planned versions of its neighbours) and the registered cross-system scenarios are run against it — at least SCN-FIRE-DT17 and SCN-MISSION-CANCEL-M20481, and every scenario whose owner says it is affected. The result is an entry in the compatibility matrix (REQ-02-01-003).
  2. Deployment gate. The release is installed only if the resulting combination of versions is in the matrix. During a staged rollout, the mixed combination is itself a matrix entry.
  3. Runtime check. When a truck connects to the AMS, it reports its ADS software version and map version; the AMS issues no mission to a truck whose combination is not approved (REQ-02-01-004). This catches the truck that missed an update, or was repaired with an old software image. The rule fails closed: an unknown version is not approved.
  4. Operational impact review. Before deployment, the change is reviewed for its effect on procedures, HMI, training and KPIs (REQ-02-01-007; section 8.5).

The number of combinations explains why matrices are kept small. With two FMS, two AMS, three ADS, two VCS and two map versions in play, there are 48 possible combinations; testing each interface pair along the chain instead needs 26 pairings (section 12, example 4). Pairwise testing is cheaper but misses behaviour that needs three constituents to appear — such as the fire response — so the registered cross-system scenarios are run as well.

4.7 Governance: owners, the integration authority and change

Managerial independence means that the right person to fix a problem may work for another organisation. The handbook therefore keeps three artefacts [H]: the constituent register (REQ-02-01-001), the context-boundary record with an owner on both sides of every external interface (REQ-02-01-002), and a change process in which each change names the versions before and after, the scenarios affected and the result of the operational impact review, and is approved jointly by the integration authority and mine operations. The register is what makes the question of section 8.2 — whom do I call? — answerable at 03:00 on a NIGHT shift.

4.8 Trade-offs and limits

  • Where to draw the boundary. A wider boundary gives the integration authority more control over systems it does not specialise in; a narrow one places more safety weight on interfaces with external owners.
  • One supplier or several. A single supplier for FMS, AMS and ADS reduces integration effort but does not remove the SoS character: trucks, plant, network and people remain independent.
  • Freezing versus evolving. Freezing versions delays fixes, including safety fixes. The compatibility matrix is the compromise: change is allowed, untested combinations are not.
  • Limits. The SoS characteristics are a teaching frame [TBV], not a standard. Function allocation is ch-02-03; independent safety functions and onboard systems ch-02-04; interoperability ch-02-05; ICDs ch-02-06.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation: production as an emergent property

On an undisturbed DAY shift with the baseline allocation (37 producing trucks, equipment.yaml), the reference mine moves ore and waste through Intersection X at 155.5 passes per hour, with no haul traffic through V, because every shortest route uses R05. Each ore truck on EX02 moves 506.4 t/h at a cycle of 1564.1 s without queueing. None of these numbers belongs to a single constituent: the FMS chose the allocation, the AMS the routes and reservations, the ADS the speeds within them, the VCS the actual motion. Normal operation is already emergent; we only notice when it changes.

6.2 Abnormal operation: fire on DT17 as one event across constituents (SCN-FIRE-DT17)

And the ERT's approach at T+300 s meets a traffic pattern the reroute created. Each constituent behaves as designed; their coordination is the SoS behaviour that must be designed, tested and owned.

6.3 Degraded operation: the same event with a constituent unavailable

The SoS view asks what happens to the fire response when one constituent is missing. Three variants show the pattern [H]:

  • The DT17–AMS link is lost (lower R05 has marginal coverage, ch-01-03). DT17 still detects, suppresses and stops (REQ-01-04-006). The AMS sees only communication lost, freezes the reservations around DT17 (as in SCN-COMMS-R05) and alarms, but creates no exclusion zone. The fire reaches CR1 by radio from the field or when the link returns. Hence the onboard event must be stored and forwarded with its original time, and a comms-lost truck on a ramp treated as possibly abnormal (REQ-02-01-006).
  • The FMS is unavailable. Nothing up to T+15 s depends on it. The re-plan at T+60 s does not happen; trucks stay held, the Pit Supervisor decides production by radio and delay records are reconstructed afterwards.
  • The AMS is unavailable (SCN-AMS-RESTART, ch-02-02). Trucks stop within their granted paths; DT17 stops on its own; no zone and no digital permission can be created. The controller uses the remote stop where trucks must be stopped and radio for the ERT until the AMS has reconciled.

In every variant the safety reactions survive and the coordination degrades. That is the intended shape of the SoS.

6.4 A decision that crosses owners: cancelling M-20481 (SCN-MISSION-CANCEL-M20481)

Ch-01-04 followed the cancellation of M-20481 through the chain: the FMS sends the cancel at T+0 s, the AMS acknowledges at T+1 s, the ADS accepts the revised granted path, reservations behind DT17 are released by T+40 s and DT17 stops at C01-Q at T+102 s. In SoS terms one decision has been taken in the FMS (supplier A, production-systems team) and executed in the AMS and ADS (supplier B, AHS engineering), over a network owned by a third team. When the layers disagree, the disagreement tells the controller not only where the problem is, but whose it is — the operations use of the constituent register in section 8.2.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Who creates the exclusion zone. The AMS creates it at T+10 s from the vehicle's event and position. Drawing it by hand would add at least the 5 s to acknowledgement and depend on the controller's attention at the moment of greatest load. The price is that the AMS must know the zone geometry for each event type, and that geometry depends on where the ADS chooses to stop — a behavioural coupling that belongs in the ICD and the compatibility matrix.

Automatic containment, human decision on rerouting. The AMS holds R05 traffic automatically (containment). It does not reroute the whole pit over R24 automatically: at T+120 s that is a decision for the Pit Supervisor [H], because the reroute sends 141.5 loaded and empty passes per hour through V at the same time as the ERT is called out through V. An automatic reroute would be faster and would be right most of the time; it would also create the conflict of section 4.5 without anyone deciding to accept it.

FMS re-plans, AMS routes. At T+60 s the FMS knows that R05 is blocked but does not compute its own detour (ch-01-04: the FMS asks, the AMS decides). It re-plans only which truck serves which shovel and destination, and waits for the AMS's route and expected cycle (T+120 s) before it counts tonnes, so plan and traffic cannot diverge.

Correlation in one place. The four alarms of section 1.1 come from four constituents. The handbook requires them to be grouped in the control-room display by asset, place and time window, with the source of every item shown (REQ-02-01-005). The AMS is a natural host; the requirement is about the result, not the host.

Versions in the protocol. Every connection between constituents carries the versions of both sides, so that the runtime check of section 4.6 is possible and so that logs show which combination produced which behaviour.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — coordinating one response to a cross-system event (fire on DT17)

  1. AHS Controller (T+15 s): acknowledge the incident, not the individual alarms. Confirm in the incident view: fire on DT17, DT17 stopped, exclusion zone created, R05 traffic held.
  2. AHS Controller (T+15 s): call the ERT and inform the Pit Supervisor by radio: "Fire on DT17 on R05, mid-ramp, truck stopped, zone established, R05 held."
  3. AHS Controller (by T+60 s): check that every truck on R05 and within the zone is held and that the FMS has withdrawn DT17. If the FMS re-plan has not appeared, note it for the production-systems team; do not re-plan by hand in the FMS.
  4. Pit Supervisor (by T+120 s): decide whether to hold the pit or continue via R24, consulting the controller on the ERT's route. If continuing, the controller applies the decision in the AMS and confirms the first rerouted trucks on R24.
  5. AHS Controller (T+300 s): when the ERT requests entry, grant it under positive communication. Hold rerouted trucks at V and X while the ERT crosses, and tell the ERT where autonomous traffic is running.
  6. AHS Controller: keep one incident record: times, sources, decisions and who took them. Do not close individual alarms in different systems before the incident is closed.
  7. Pit Supervisor: when the ERT reports the scene safe, authorise clearing the zone (ch-16-06).

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

The handbook codes the event as follows [H]; categories follow the GMG Time Usage Model (SRC-PUB-GMG-TUM-2020) as in ch-01-05:

  • DT17: DLY-DN-01 (breakdown, Downtime) from T+0 s until it is returned to service.
  • Trucks held by the exclusion zone on and near R05: DLY-OD-14 (held by an incident exclusion zone), an Operating Delay, until rerouted or released [H].
  • EX02 while it has no trucks: DLY-NP-03 (loading unit waiting for trucks).
  • Rerouted trucks: no delay code; the effect appears as longer cycles. On EX02 the cycle grows from 1564.1 s to 1800.1 s (+236.0 s) and each truck's output falls from 506.4 to 440.0 t/h (section 12, example 2).

Two cross-system KPI effects deserve a line in the shift report: the change in traffic through V (from no haul traffic to 141.5 passes per hour) and the waiting at Intersection X, which no single delay code captures. The second is also how the dispatch-rule effect of section 4.5 first becomes visible: as intersection waiting in the AMS records that rises after an FMS change.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Controllers need SoS literacy: knowing which constituent each item on their screens comes from, who owns it, and how one event appears in several places. Pit Supervisors need to recognise that a change in one system can change behaviour elsewhere, and that they are consulted in every operational impact review. Field crews and the ERT need to know that the truck's own reaction does not depend on CR1, and that their access does.

Any constituent change can change what people see or must do: a VCS firmware update may change fault codes on the HMI, an ADS release where trucks stop after an alarm, an FMS rule where queues form, a new map the routes. Procedures, HMI training material and shift-report templates are therefore reviewed for every change (REQ-02-01-007), even when the supplier calls it "no functional change". The review may find nothing; it must still be done and recorded.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-02-01-001 [H] The AHS integration authority shall keep a register of every constituent system of the AHS stating, for each, its supplier and site owner, its deployed instances and their locations, its current version on each instance, its release process and its interfaces. Rationale: managerial independence means that no single organisation sees the whole SoS (sections 4.1, 4.7). Verification: inspection; audit of the register against the versions actually deployed.

REQ-02-01-002 [H] The context boundary of the AHS shall be documented with every external interface, each classified by class (plant, spatial data, enterprise, manned equipment and people, infrastructure), direction, criticality and timing class, and each with a named owner on both sides and an interface control document. Rationale: dependencies on external systems must be known before an incident reveals them (sections 4.3, 4.4). Verification: inspection.

REQ-02-01-003 [H] No release of a constituent system shall be deployed unless the resulting combination of versions, including any mixed combination during a staged rollout, is listed in an approved compatibility matrix supported by an integration test in which at least SCN-FIRE-DT17, SCN-MISSION-CANCEL-M20481 and every registered scenario declared affected by the change were run with that combination. Rationale: independent evolution creates untested combinations; behavioural incompatibility is not visible in message definitions (section 4.6). Verification: inspection of release records and test reports; test.

REQ-02-01-004 [H] When a truck connects, and before it issues a mission to the truck, the AMS shall check that the truck's ADS software version and map version form a combination listed in the approved compatibility matrix, and shall not issue a mission to a truck whose combination is not listed or cannot be determined. Rationale: a truck that missed an update or was restored from an old image must not run on assumptions the AMS does not share; fail closed (section 4.6). Verification: test (approved, unapproved and unreported versions).

REQ-02-01-005 [H] The control-room display shall group the alarms and state changes from different constituents that relate to one event — by asset, place and time window — into one incident, and shall show for each item its source constituent and the time at which the source observed it. Rationale: one response to one event (sections 7.1, 8.1). Verification: demonstration with the SCN-FIRE-DT17 timeline; inspection.

REQ-02-01-006 [H] Each constituent shall define, in its interface documentation, its behaviour when each interface partner is unavailable or its data is stale; for movement and permissions that behaviour shall be fail-closed. Safety-related events raised on a vehicle while its link is down shall be stored and forwarded with their original time when the link returns. Rationale: degraded variants of section 6.3. Verification: inspection; fault-injection test (link loss during SCN-FIRE-DT17).

REQ-02-01-007 [H] A change to any constituent system, including a configuration or rule change such as a new FMS dispatch rule, shall be preceded by an operational impact review that identifies the affected procedures, HMI displays, training material and KPIs, approved by mine operations and by the integration authority before deployment. Rationale: changes in one constituent change behaviour elsewhere (sections 4.5, 8.5). Verification: inspection of change records.

REQ-02-01-008 [H] Every registered scenario that involves three or more constituent systems shall have a named owner in the integration authority, who maintains its expected end-to-end behaviour and has it rerun after every change declared to affect it. Rationale: emergent behaviour that nobody owns is discovered during incidents (section 4.5). Verification: inspection of the scenario ownership list and regression records.

10. Verification and validation

Verification at SoS level asks whether the combination of constituents, as deployed, has the properties required of the whole. Four methods apply:

  • Inspection of the constituent register, the boundary record and the compatibility matrix against what is installed (REQ-02-01-001 to -003).
  • Cross-system scenario tests. The registered scenarios with their T+ timelines are ready-made SoS test cases. The SCN-FIRE-DT17 timeline of section 6.2, run against the integrated FMS, AMS and trucks (real or simulated), must produce the same events in the same order, with the information of section 7.3 arriving in time. The scenario is rerun for every change its owner declares relevant (REQ-02-01-008).
  • Fault injection across boundaries. Remove one constituent at a time during the scenario — the link, the FMS, the AMS (section 6.3) — and check that safety reactions survive and coordination degrades as specified.
  • Compatibility tests for mixed versions. During a staged rollout both ADS versions run the same scenarios on the same roads; differences in behaviour are reviewed before the rollout continues.

Validation asks whether the SoS serves the people who operate it. Table-top walkthroughs of SCN-FIRE-DT17 with controllers, the Pit Supervisor and the ERT check that the incident view, the procedure of section 8.3 and the owner table of section 8.2 are usable under time pressure. They also find emergent conflicts that no test case anticipated, such as the ERT route through rerouted traffic. Validation is repeated after significant changes (Volume 18).

This chapter has no executable model. Cycle times, intersection passes and route lengths are computed with ahs_sim from mine.yaml (figures/make_figures.py --numbers); message behaviour across constituents is modelled from ch-02-07 onwards.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

Most design mistakes at SoS level are the failure modes of section 7.2 built in by omission. Three more deserve naming:

  • Designing the chain and forgetting the system of systems. Each constituent meets its requirements; the emergent behaviour is left to chance.
  • Defining compatibility as message compatibility. A new stop-location logic passes every schema check.
  • Drawing the boundary by habit. The network or the plant is "someone else's problem" until an incident shows that the AHS depends on it.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Treating four alarms as four events. Acknowledging and closing them separately loses the order and the cause.
  • Calling every owner at once. Find the boundary where the layers first disagree and call its owner with IDs and times.
  • Re-planning by hand in the FMS during an AMS hold. The FMS then plans on roads the AMS has not opened.
  • Accepting "no functional change" as a reason to skip the procedure review. The review may be short; skipping it is how a changed alarm text or stop behaviour surprises the night shift.
  • Rerouting the pit without asking where the ERT will drive. The two decisions share V, R12 and X.

12. Worked examples

All values [A] unless stated; haul-cycle numbers computed with ahs_sim 0.5.0 on mine version 0.3.0 (steady state, as in ch-01-01) by figures/make_figures.py --numbers.

Example 1 — the vehicle reacts first. DT17 climbs R05 loaded at 11.4 km/h (3.18 m/s). Between the alarm (T+0 s) and the start of the controlled stop (T+5 s) it covers 15.9 m. Had the stop waited for the controller's acknowledgement at T+15 s, DT17 would have covered 47.7 m. The SoS lesson: the constituent closest to the hazard acts without waiting for any other.

Example 2 — what the reroute costs and where the traffic goes. With R05 held, the shortest routes from all four shovels use R24. Cycles change as follows:

Loading unit Cycle via R05 (s) Cycle via R24 (s) t/h per truck via R05 t/h per truck via R24
EX01 1572.4 1808.4 503.7 437.9
EX02 1564.1 1800.1 506.4 440.0
EX03 1914.7 1875.3 413.6 422.3
EX04 1883.4 1844.0 420.5 429.5

The ore circuits lose (EX02 +236.0 s per cycle); the waste circuits gain slightly because R24 is shorter for the bench. With the baseline allocation less DT17 (36 trucks), Intersection V goes from 0 to 141.5 passes per hour (one every 25.4 s) and X from 155.5 to 141.5. The ERT route G01 → V → X → P1 (R13, R12, R01) is 2016.4 m long and shares V and R12 with that traffic.

Example 3 — a platoon at Intersection X. Passes per hour 155.5; crossing reservation 12 s [A]; utilisation 155.5 × 12 / 3600 = 0.518; mean headway 23.2 s. A group of four trucks arriving together waits 0, 12, 24 and 36 s: 72 s in total, 18 s on average. With 10 such groups per hour [A], waiting is 720 s per hour, 0.20 truck-hours per hour, 2.4 truck-hours per 12 h shift — about the output of one ore truck for 2.4 hours, with no delay code to show it.

Example 4 — counting version combinations. FMS 2, AMS 2, ADS 3, VCS 2, map 2 versions [A]: 2 × 2 × 3 × 2 × 2 = 48 combinations. Interface pairs along the chain: FMS–AMS 4, AMS–ADS 6, ADS–VCS 6, AMS–map 4, ADS–map 6: 26 pairings. An ADS rollout of 40 trucks at 4 per day takes 10 days, during which the mixed combination is the one in service.

Example 5 — how often something changes. From the table behind FIG-02-01-02: 13 internal software or firmware releases a year, one every 4.0 weeks on average, plus 52 map releases. Operational impact reviews are therefore routine work, and their format must be short and standard.

15. Key takeaways

  • An autonomous mine is a system of systems: its constituents operate, are managed and evolve independently, and the behaviour that matters most is emergent.
  • The constituents at the reference mine include FMS, AMS, 40 ADS and VCS instances, onboard safety-related systems, the independent safety function, manned-vehicle devices, the map — and the people who run them.
  • Draw the context boundary deliberately and classify every external interface; every class has safety-related or production-critical interfaces.
  • Emergent behaviour must be owned. SCN-FIRE-DT17 crosses seven element groups; the reroute it triggers meets the ERT at V and on R12.
  • Independent releases create combinations nobody tested. Check compatibility at integration test, deployment gate, truck connection and operational impact review.
  • Safety reactions depend on nothing central; coordination depends on everything. Keep it that way.
  • When layers disagree, call the owner of the boundary where they first disagree.
