1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

It is 14:05 on a DAY shift [A]. The pit-floor trailer CT3 loses power. Within twenty seconds eight trucks on lower R05 and on the RL 330 bench turn to communication lost on the CR1 display, and within two minutes every circuit in the pit has stopped, because the stopped trucks sit on both exits from the pit floor. Two nights earlier, at 02:10, the AMS primary server had failed and the standby had taken over: every truck in the mine stopped within its granted path, and the fleet came back in stages a few minutes later.

Neither event changed a single line of the architecture of ch-01-04. The FMS still assigned, the AMS still owned missions and reservations, the ADS still drove. What failed was a box in a trailer and a server in a room — the physical deployment of that architecture. And each physical failure took with it a different, and not obvious, set of functions and trucks.

The two events raise practical questions:

  • Which trucks and which functions does a single trailer on the pit floor take with it when it fails?
  • What did the control room lose during the failover, and what kept working?
  • Where were the logs, and did they survive?
  • What must the Field Technician check before telling CR1 that the pit-floor network is back?

This chapter covers:

  • the difference between the logical architecture (functions and data flows) and the physical deployment (servers, comms sites, onboard computers, field terminals) at the reference mine;
  • failure domains: how to find what a component takes with it, worked through for CT3 (SCN-COMMS-R05);
  • redundancy and failover of the AMS in the control-room deployment (SCN-AMS-RESTART);
  • central, edge and onboard deployment of AMS functions, with their trade-offs;
  • time synchronisation and event logging across deployed components;
  • what CR1 and the field crew see and do while the deployment is degraded.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Distinguish the logical architecture (functions and data flows) from the physical deployment (control-room servers, comms sites CT1–CT3, onboard computers, field terminals) of the reference mine.
  2. Identify failure domains in the physical deployment and determine which trucks and functions are affected when CT3 loses power.
  3. Compare deployment options for AMS functions (central, edge, onboard) and state the trade-offs in latency, availability and safety responsibility.
  4. Explain the requirements for time synchronisation and event logging across deployed components, and where logs are kept so that they survive a server failure.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Describe what the control room loses and what continues to work when the AMS primary server fails over or when a single comms site fails.
  2. Identify the field equipment whose condition the Field Technician must check before declaring the pit-floor network restored.
  3. Locate the physical components of the AHS at the reference mine (CR1 servers, CT1–CT3, onboard units, field terminals) and name the role that maintains each.
  4. Decide, with the Field Technician, the order of checks after a CT3 power failure (power, link, truck reconnection) before traffic on the RL 330 bench resumes.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

Architecture diagrams are drawn without geography. FIG-01-04-01 places the AMS in a box between the FMS and the ADS; it does not say that the AMS runs on two servers in the MIA, that its messages reach DT17 through a tower at the MIA, a tower on the pit crest and a trailer on the pit floor, or that the trailer runs on its own power supply. Those facts decide what fails together, and that is what operations people meet first.

At the reference mine the questions arrive as four conversations:

  • A Field Technician at CT3: "The trailer has power again and the lights are green. Can I tell control the network is back?"
  • The AHS Controller after the failover: "The AMS failed over and every truck stopped, but the FMS was still up. Why couldn't I keep anything moving?"
  • An engineer reviewing the server installation: "The AMS standby is in the same rack as the primary. That's redundant, isn't it?"
  • The Pit Supervisor, reading the incident log after CT3 came back: "The log says one of the bench trucks stopped before its last path extension was issued. Which entry is wrong?"

Each has a precise answer. Green lights on a trailer are not a restored network: the link to the MIA, the reconnection of every truck and the AMS's reconciliation must follow. The AMS is the only source of movement authority, so during a failover nothing can receive new authority, whatever the FMS is doing. A standby in the same rack shares its power, cooling and switch. And a log that seems to reverse cause and effect is usually a log whose clocks disagree.

3. Terminology and foundations

Logical architecture. The functions of the AHS, the state each function owns and the data that flows between them, without regard to where anything runs [H]. FIG-02-02-01 is the logical architecture of the reference mine.

Physical deployment. The physical nodes on which those functions run — servers, comms sites, onboard computers, field terminals — with their locations, power supplies and network connections, and the allocation of each function to nodes [H]. FIG-02-02-02 is the physical deployment of the reference mine.

Failure domain. The set of functions, assets and places that lose service when one physical component fails [H]. The failure domain of CT3 is everything that depends on CT3: the AMS links of the trucks on the segments it serves, the reports of manned-vehicle devices there, and whatever else is routed through it.

Single point of failure and common-cause failure. A single point of failure is a component whose failure alone stops a function. A common-cause failure is one cause that defeats several components that were meant to be independent — for example two redundant servers on one power supply.

Redundancy. Provision of more than one means of performing a function, so that the failure of one does not stop the function [H]. Redundancy is only as good as the independence of its parts.

Primary, standby server and failover. The AMS at the reference mine runs on a primary server with a standby server ready to take over (SCN-AMS-RESTART). Failover is the transfer of the AMS role from primary to standby. The standby server must not be confused with Standby, the time category of ch-01-05.

Edge. Computing placed near the vehicles — at a comms site such as CT3, or at a node in the pit — rather than in the MIA or onboard [H].

Backhaul. The link that connects a comms site back to the MIA network. mine.yaml records where CT1–CT3 stand but not how they are connected; the handbook assumes CT3 → CT2 → CT1 [A].

Time synchronisation and event log. All deployed components take their time from a common site time reference so that their records can be put in order; an event log is the time-stamped record of what each component did and received (ch-01-06, section 4.6). The messaging consequences of clock offset are in ch-02-07; reconciliation using the logs is in ch-02-08.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The logical architecture

FIG-02-02-01 shows the functions of each element of the handbook's reference architecture, the state each owns and the data that flows between them. It contains no location, no server and no radio.

Three properties of the logical view matter for deployment [H]:

  • State has one owner. Missions, reservations, permissions and zones are owned by the AMS; each truck's own position estimate, trajectory and stop reason by its ADS; assignments and cycle records by the FMS. Wherever a function is deployed, its state must still have one owner; other elements hold only copies (ch-02-08).
  • Data flows have timing classes. Assignments move in minutes; granted paths and acknowledgements in about a second; setpoints in hundredths of a second (ch-01-04, section 4.5). A data flow's timing class limits where its two ends can be deployed.
  • Safety functions do not depend on central flows. The onboard safety-related functions and the independent safety function act on the VCS directly. The deployment must preserve this: nothing in the physical path of a safety reaction may depend on a central server.

4.3 From functions to nodes

Mapping FIG-02-02-01 onto FIG-02-02-02 gives three kinds of allocation [H]:

  • One function, many nodes. The AMS runs on a primary and a standby; the ADS on 40 trucks; the event log on the servers and on every truck's event recorder. The function's correctness then depends on the copies agreeing — on replication, versions (ch-02-01, section 4.6) and synchronised time.
  • One node, many functions. Server room A hosts the FMS servers, the AMS primary, the log store and the time source. A loss of room A therefore removes, at once, functions that the logical view keeps carefully apart. The logical boundary between FMS and AMS does not protect against a shared power feed.
  • A data flow that crosses many nodes. A granted path from the AMS to DT17 on R09 crosses server room A, the site LAN, CT1, the CT1–CT2 and CT2–CT3 backhaul, CT3's radio and DT17's onboard radio. Every one of these is in the failure domain of that flow.

The last point is the reason for the next section. A logical data flow is only as available as the chain of physical components it crosses.

The distance rings make a second point. CT3 is 158.2 m from Intersection Z and 291.6 m from the EX02 queue point, yet R07 and R08, the pit-floor roads to EX01 and EX02, have good coverage and are not in the scenario; EX04-Q, at 955.6 m, is (section 12, example 2). Coverage depends on line of sight, terrain and the other sites, not on distance from one site. A failure domain for a comms site must be derived from recorded coverage — measured, then held in the mine model — not drawn as a circle (REQ-02-02-002).

What the CT3 domain does to production. Only eight trucks lose their link, but every circuit at the reference mine crosses the named segments: EX01 and EX02 haul over R05, and EX03 and EX04 over R10 or R11, R09 and R05. With trucks stopped and reservations frozen on lower R05 and R09 — the first segments of both exits from the pit floor — all 37 producing trucks are affected (ch-01-03, section 6.2). The failure domain of a component for production is therefore larger than its failure domain for communication. Both must be in the deployment record.

What else may sit in the CT3 domain. Any function whose physical path crosses CT3 shares its fate: the manned-vehicle devices of a Field Technician working on the bench, the field terminal through which an access request would be sent, and — if the remote-stop path shares CT3's radio equipment — the remote stop for trucks on R09–R11. Whether the remote-stop path is independent of the site wireless network is analysed in ch-02-04; the deployment record must make the answer visible (REQ-02-02-010).

4.5 Redundancy and failover in the control-room deployment

The AMS is the single source of movement authority, so its availability is the availability of the autonomous fleet. The reference mine runs it as a primary–standby pair (SCN-AMS-RESTART). Redundancy of this kind protects against one thing: an independent failure of one server. It does not protect against:

  • Shared infrastructure. Two servers on one power feed, in one room, behind one network switch, fail together. With an illustrative unavailability of 0.001 per server and 0.0005 for a shared supply [A], a single server is unavailable 8.76 hours a year, an independent pair 0.0088 hours, and a pair on one supply 4.39 hours — half the single-server figure, not a thousandth of it (section 12, example 5). The common cause dominates.
  • Shared software. The same defect in the same AMS version runs on both servers. A failover into the same fault is a restart, not a recovery.
  • Shared bad state. If a corrupted state is replicated faithfully, the standby inherits it. Replication must carry a log of changes that can be checked and replayed, not only a copy of memory (ch-02-08).

What the standby needs. To take over, the standby needs the AMS's state at the moment of failure. The reference AMS writes every state change — mission, reservation, permission, closure, zone — to a log that is replicated to server room B before the change is sent to any vehicle or device [H] (REQ-02-02-004). Anything sent but not logged would be authority that the standby does not know it has given. Even so, the standby cannot trust the log alone: a truck may have stopped short of its path, a permission may have been released by radio. It therefore rebuilds the truth from the log and from fresh reports by the vehicles before it grants anything (ch-02-08).

Why every truck stops. Each truck holds a finite granted path. When the primary is lost at T+0 s, trucks stop within their granted paths by T+5 s (registry). That is the design working: a central outage becomes a stop, never an unsupervised continuation. At 50 km/h a truck covers 69.4 m in 5 s. As a teaching assumption, this chapter takes the granted-path horizon on the fastest roads to be at least that long [A]. The price is production: 37 trucks stopped from T+5 s to T+120 s is 4255 truck-seconds, 1.18 truck-hours, before the staged resume begins (section 12, example 3).

Failover speed. How quickly the standby takes over is a trade-off. A short heartbeat timeout fails over quickly but risks taking over from a primary that is only slow — two AMS instances both believing they own the reservations, the worst outcome there is. A long timeout leaves the fleet stopped longer. The reference mine's standby declares the primary lost at T+3 s [A] and is active at T+30 s (registry); it must also fence the old primary so that it cannot grant anything if it comes back [H].

4.6 Central, edge and onboard deployment of AMS functions

AMS functions can be deployed centrally (in the MIA server rooms), at the edge (for example at a comms site) or onboard the vehicles. The handbook's reference architecture fixes who owns a decision; it does not fix where the code runs. The trade-offs [H]:

Criterion Central (MIA servers) Edge (e.g. at CT3) Onboard (vehicle to vehicle)
Latency to the vehicle Longest: LAN, backhaul and radio Short: radio only Shortest, or none
Distance at 40 km/h for illustrative latencies [A] 0.3 s → 3.33 m 0.05 s → 0.56 m 0.01 s → 0.11 m
Failure domain Server rooms, backhaul, every comms site on the path The edge node, its power and its radio Each vehicle and the vehicle-to-vehicle link
Available during a backhaul loss No Yes, within the edge area Yes
Available during CT3 power loss (for R09–R11) No No — same power and site as the radio Only if the vehicles' own link does not use CT3
Single owner of reservations and permissions Natural Must be designed: the edge node acts for the central AMS Hardest: authority is distributed
Supervision and display in CR1 Direct Through the backhaul Indirect, through reports
Versions to manage One One per edge node One per vehicle

The table suggests a rule [H]: deploy a function as close to the vehicle as its timing class requires, and no closer. Driving decisions and safety reactions are onboard because their timing class is tenths of a second or less (ch-01-04). Reservations at Intersection X, permissions and exclusion zones work in seconds and need one owner and a view of the whole site, so they are central at the reference mine. An edge function at CT3 would help when the backhaul fails, but not when CT3 itself loses power, because it shares CT3's failure domain — so for SCN-COMMS-R05 it gains nothing.

Moving a function outward also moves safety responsibility. An edge node that grants reservations on R09–R11 becomes a holder of movement authority. It must fail closed when it loses contact with the central AMS — granting nothing that the central AMS had not already granted — and it must appear in the deployment record with its own failure domain (REQ-02-02-008). Behaviour of vehicles on link loss itself is a handbook convention [H] defined in the scenario registry: trucks stop within the granted path they already hold.

4.7 Time synchronisation and event logging

A deployed AHS is a distributed recorder. To reconstruct what happened — in an investigation, in a KPI dispute or in the reconciliation after a failure — the records from every node must be put in one order. Three requirements follow [H]:

  1. One site time reference. Servers, comms equipment, onboard computers and field terminals take their time from a common site source — at the reference mine the active AMS instance is the time reference (ch-02-07), with its time source in server room A [A], distributed over the network. Every node records whether it is synchronised.
  2. Every record carries its time, its source and its synchronisation status (REQ-02-02-006). A record from a node that is unsynchronised, or whose status is unknown, is not used to order events; the AMS then orders by the time it received the record. This is the rule ahs_sim.reconciliation applies (rule 6).
  3. No single failure destroys the only copy (REQ-02-02-007). The AMS log is written in room A and replicated to room B; the FMS log likewise. Each truck's event recorder keeps its own events and its AMS exchanges while the link is down and uploads them, with their original timestamps, when it returns. During the 900 s CT3 outage the eight trucks buffer 7200 position records at one report per second [A] — trivial in size; the difficulty is time, not storage.

Why time matters is easy to show. Suppose the AMS issued the last path extension to a bench truck at T−1 s (AMS clock) and the truck stopped on link loss at T+3 s (truck clock) [A]. The gap is 4 s. If the truck's clock is 2 s behind, its stop is stamped T+1 s and the order is kept. If it is 5 s behind, the stop is stamped T−2 s — before the extension was issued — and the log now says the truck stopped before it received its last authority (section 12, example 6). That is the Pit Supervisor's question of section 2. Any offset larger than the gap between two events can reverse them; for events that are seconds apart, offsets of seconds are enough.

4.8 Trade-offs and limits

  • Mobile versus fixed comms sites. CT3 is a trailer so that coverage can follow the pit as it deepens. The price is a site on its own power supply, in the pit, which moves: its position, and therefore its coverage, must be kept current in the deployment record.
  • Synchronous versus asynchronous replication. Writing to room B before acting costs latency on every state change; writing afterwards risks a standby that does not know about the last grants.
  • More redundancy versus more independence. A second standby in room A adds little; one in room B, on its own feed, adds a lot. Independence is worth more than numbers.
  • Limits. This chapter does not model networks or reliability; coverage, link budgets and network redundancy are in Volume 11 (network), reliability and availability calculations in Volume 18 (reliability). The numbers here are illustrative [A].

Two boundaries matter. Between logical and physical ownership: the AMS function belongs to site AHS engineering, but the path its messages take crosses the communications team's equipment, so a failure of authority may need to be fixed by someone who does not own the authority. And between operations and maintenance: the server rooms have no operational role in front of them. The AHS Controller notices their failure, but cannot diagnose it; the escalation path to site AHS engineering must be written into the procedures (section 8.3).

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation: a healthy deployment

In normal operation the deployment is invisible, but it can be watched. The CR1 site status shows each comms site and backhaul link up, both AMS servers healthy with the standby's replication up to date, the time source locked and every node synchronised, and every truck and manned-vehicle device reporting. A deployment that is working but not healthy — the standby lagging, CT2 running on battery, a truck with an unsynchronised clock — is the state from which the next incident starts, and it should be visible before it matters.

6.2 Degraded operation: CT3 loses power (SCN-COMMS-R05)

Read with FIG-02-02-02 beside it, the timeline shows how a physical failure propagates through logical functions. At T+3 s the onboard functions do exactly what the logical view intends: they can no longer receive new authority and stop within the granted path they already hold. At T+10 s the central function reacts, fail-closed, to an absence. From T+60 s the effect is no longer about communication but about production, because of where the stopped trucks are. And the recovery from T+900 s is not one event but a sequence — power, site, link, reconnection, reconciliation, resume — each of which can fail on its own. Section 8.3 turns that sequence into the Field Technician's and controller's checks.

During the outage the eight trucks' event recorders keep their link-loss events, stop positions and last acknowledged paths with onboard timestamps; at T+900 s these are uploaded and compared with the AMS log (ch-02-08), which is where the clock offsets of section 4.7 matter.

The FMS is in server room A but on its own servers; it keeps planning and recording throughout, but none of its assignments can become a mission until T+120 s. Manned vehicles that already hold permissions keep them — the trucks are stopped, and no new movement can be granted into their areas — but no new permission can be issued digitally; access needed for safety goes by radio under the site procedure. The remote stop, which does not depend on the AMS, remains available throughout (ch-01-04, REQ-01-04-005).

6.4 Degraded operation: a shared failure domain

Suppose instead that the power feed to server room A fails [A]. In the logical view the FMS and the AMS are separate elements; in the deployment they share room A. The AMS fails over to room B as in 6.3, but the FMS is down too, and with it the time source. The standby now runs on its own clock, so the AMS marks its time source holdover and keeps ordering records by receive time until the source returns [H]. Production decisions go to the Pit Supervisor by radio. This is the cost of the "one node, many functions" allocation of section 4.3, and the reason REQ-02-02-003 separates the standby from the primary's power and room.

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

Where the standby lives. Same rack: cheapest, protects only against a server fault. Same room, other rack: protects against a rack fault. Separate room with its own feed and fire compartment [R]: protects against the common causes that matter most. The reference mine takes the third option [A]; the price is a network path between the rooms that must itself be redundant.

When to replicate. The reference AMS logs and replicates each state change before sending it (REQ-02-02-004). On a LAN between two rooms this adds milliseconds to a decision whose timing class is a second — an easy trade. Replicating afterwards would save little time and could leave the standby unaware of the last grants.

How fast to fail over. A 3 s heartbeat timeout [A] is long compared with the network and short compared with the 5 s by which the trucks have stopped. Shorter would risk false failovers; longer would only lengthen the stop. Whatever the timeout, the new primary fences the old one.

Where to put AMS functions. Central at the reference mine, by the rule of section 4.6. An edge node at CT3 was considered and rejected [A]: it would share CT3's power, so it gives no benefit in SCN-COMMS-R05, and it would add a holder of authority, a version to manage and a display path through the backhaul.

How CT3 is powered. CT3 is mobile, so it carries its own supply [A]. A second supply or a battery that covers the time needed to reach the trailer would shrink the outage of SCN-COMMS-R05; a remote power alarm would bring the T+60 s recognition forward. Both belong to Volume 11.

Time source. One site source in room A [A] is simple, but makes the time source part of room A's failure domain. Nodes therefore need a defined holdover behaviour and a status flag, so that a lost source degrades ordering gracefully rather than silently.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations

Laid against the two timelines, the deployment determines four timing points [H]:

  • Detection on the vehicle (T+3 s in SCN-COMMS-R05). Trucks detect link loss from missing messages. The detection time must be shorter than the time to the end of the granted path at speed, or the granted path is what stops the truck — which is also safe. Both ends of this are onboard; nothing central is involved.
  • Detection centrally (T+10 s). The AMS marks trucks communication lost from missing reports. Its timeout is longer than the vehicles' so that a truck is never shown as connected after it has already stopped for link loss.
  • Recognition by people (T+20 to T+60 s). The step from eight alarms to "CT3 down" is fast only if the site status shows CT3 and the deployment record links the trucks to CT3. Without the served_by data the controller's knowledge fills the gap (ch-01-03).
  • Restoration (T+900 to T+960 s). Reconnection is staggered as the trucks re-register; reconciliation cannot finish before the last truck has reported, and its comparison uses timestamps from both sides. The 60 s between T+900 and T+960 s is a budget for those steps [A], not a guarantee.

In SCN-AMS-RESTART the corresponding points are the heartbeat timeout (T+3 s), the stop within the granted paths (T+5 s), the takeover (T+30 s) and the unblocking of grants (T+120 s). The interval from T+5 to T+120 s is what the deployment costs when the primary fails; shortening it means faster takeover and faster vehicle reports, never granting before reconciliation is complete (ch-02-08).

8.1 What the control room sees, loses and keeps

The difference between the two is the first thing a controller must recognise. A failover affects every truck at once and shows itself as a failover alarm; a comms-site failure affects the trucks in one area and shows itself as a cluster of comms-lost alarms plus a site down on the status display. The responses differ: during a failover the controller waits for reconciliation and keeps people where they are; during a site failure the controller holds traffic into the affected area and sends the Field Technician to the site.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — after a CT3 power failure: order of checks before the RL 330 bench resumes

  1. AHS Controller (T+20 s): acknowledge the comms-lost alarms; check the site status of CT1–CT3. If the stopped trucks are all on lower R05, R09, R10 or R11 and CT3 is down, treat the event as one site failure.
  2. AHS Controller (T+90 s): hold traffic bound for AA-North at AP-02 and AP-04; record the cause (CT3) and the locations.
  3. AHS Controller (T+120 s): dispatch the Field Technician to CT3; grant entry only under positive communication at the access point named. Tell the technician which trucks are stopped where; trucks without a link must be passed only as the site procedure for comms-lost trucks allows (ch-16-07).
  4. Field Technician — power: at CT3, find and correct the cause of the power loss; confirm that the supply is stable (generator running and fuelled, or battery charging, breakers set) [A]. Report to CR1.
  5. Field Technician — site equipment: confirm that the radio equipment has restarted without alarms, and that the trailer, mast and antennas are undamaged and in their recorded position (CT3 is mobile; a moved trailer changes the coverage). Report to CR1.
  6. AHS Controller with the communications team — link: confirm that the backhaul from CT3 is up and that CT3 shows up on the site status. Do not release anything yet.
  7. AHS Controller — truck reconnection: confirm that every one of the eight trucks has re-registered and is reporting, and that the manned-vehicle devices in the area report again. A truck that does not reconnect stays held, with its frozen reservations.
  8. AHS Controller — reconciliation: wait until the AMS reports reconciliation complete and no conflict is open. Do not force a truck to continue during reconciliation.
  9. Field Technician: leave the area under positive communication and report the exit to the controller; the controller confirms the exit and processes the release of the technician's permission.
  10. AHS Controller (from T+960 s), with the Pit Supervisor's approval: release holds in stages, first on lower R05 and R09, then on the bench; confirm that the first trucks pass without new comms alarms. Close out the delay records.

The order matters. Power before site, site before link, link before reconnection, reconnection before reconciliation, and reconciliation before any truck moves near where people have been working. A technician who reports "green lights" has completed step 5, not the restoration.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

  • The eight trucks stopped in CT3's domain: DLY-OD-05 (stopped on communication loss), an Operating Delay. CT3 is one of three sites, so this is not a site-wide loss and not DLY-SE-02 (ch-01-03). At 900 s each, the eight trucks lose 2.00 truck-hours directly (section 12, example 1).
  • Trucks held at AP-02 and AP-04: DLY-OD-04 (held for access permission or work zone) [H], as in ch-01-03.
  • Upper bound for the event: if every circuit stops for the 900 s outage with no catch-up, 37 trucks lose 9.25 truck-hours (ch-01-03).
  • AMS failover: every truck stops from about T+5 to T+120 s and longer during the staged resume; 1.18 truck-hours to T+120 s for 37 trucks, coded DLY-OD-12 (stopped on AMS failover or reconciliation), an Operating Delay [H].

The record must carry the cause (CT3, or the AMS failover) and the locations, so that every interval can be traced to one cause afterwards. Availability of the deployment itself — how often and how long CT3 or the AMS is unavailable — is not a GMG time category for trucks; it is tracked as a reliability measure (Volume 18).

8.5 Training and competency implications

Controllers need a physical mental map next to the logical one: where each comms site is, which areas each is believed to serve, where the servers are and who maintains each. They must be able to tell a failover from a site failure within a minute. Field Technicians need the restoration sequence of section 8.3 as a checklist, and the knowledge that CT3's position is part of its configuration. Pit Supervisors need to know that a failover stops the whole fleet by design, and that nothing should be "forced" while the AMS reconciles. The map is refreshed whenever the deployment record changes, because CT3 moves with the pit.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-02-02-001 [H] The integration authority shall keep a deployment record that allocates every logical function of the AHS to the physical nodes on which it runs and states, for each node, its location, power supply, network path to the AMS and the role and system owner that maintain it. Rationale: failure domains and maintenance paths can only be found from the physical allocation (sections 4.2, 4.3). Verification: inspection; site walk-down against the record.

REQ-02-02-002 [H] For each physical component whose failure can stop more than one truck — each comms site, backhaul link, server, and server-room power supply — the deployment record shall list the road segments, trucks, manned-vehicle devices and functions that lose service when it fails. Segment membership shall be derived from recorded coverage data identifying the comms site or sites serving each segment, not from distance. Rationale: the CT3 domain is not a circle (section 4.4); mine.yaml does not record the serving site (REQ-01-03-005). Verification: inspection; test by switching off a comms site in a controlled window and comparing the trucks that lose their link with the record.

REQ-02-02-003 [H] The AMS standby shall not share with the AMS primary any power supply, room or fire compartment, or network switch whose single failure would stop both. Rationale: common-cause failure dominates the availability of a redundant pair (sections 4.5, 6.4). Verification: inspection; test (removal of power to the primary's room during a controlled window).

REQ-02-02-004 [H] The AMS shall write every change of missions, reservations, permissions, closures and zones to a log that is replicated outside the primary's failure domain before the change is sent to any vehicle or device, and the standby shall start from that log. Rationale: authority sent but not logged is authority the standby does not know it has given (section 4.5). Verification: test (primary stopped immediately after a grant; the standby's log contains the grant); inspection.

REQ-02-02-005 [H] During an AMS failover and until reconciliation is complete, the control-room display shall show that a failover is in progress and shall mark every AMS-derived value as stale with its age. Rationale: the controller must not act on a picture the AMS can no longer vouch for (sections 6.3, 8.1). Verification: demonstration with the SCN-AMS-RESTART timeline.

REQ-02-02-006 [H] All servers, comms equipment, onboard computers and field terminals shall take time from a common site time reference and record their synchronisation status. Every event record shall carry its timestamp, its source and the source's synchronisation status; records from a source whose status is unsynchronised or unknown shall not be used to order events. Rationale: offsets larger than the gap between events reverse their order (section 4.7). Verification: test (offset injected on one truck; ordering falls back to receive time); inspection.

REQ-02-02-007 [H] Event logs shall be kept so that no single server, room or comms-site failure destroys the only copy. Vehicles shall record their safety-related events and their exchanges with the AMS onboard while the link is down and upload them with their original timestamps when it returns. Rationale: investigations and reconciliation need the records from the failed part of the deployment (section 4.7). Verification: test (log content compared after a primary failure and after a comms-site outage); inspection.

REQ-02-02-008 [H] Any AMS function deployed at an edge node or onboard shall, on losing contact with the central AMS, grant no authority beyond what the central AMS had already granted, and shall be listed in the deployment record with its failure domain. Rationale: moving a function outward moves safety responsibility (section 4.6). Verification: test (edge node isolated from the central AMS); inspection.

REQ-02-02-009 [H] A comms site shall be declared restored only after power stability, site equipment and position, backhaul link, and the reconnection of every truck and device in its failure domain have been confirmed and the AMS has reported reconciliation complete. Each confirmation shall be recorded with its time and the confirming role. Rationale: "green lights" are not a restored network (section 8.3). Verification: inspection of incident records; drill.

REQ-02-02-010 [H] The deployment record shall identify every physical component that the remote-stop path shares with the AMS servers or with the site wireless network. Rationale: whether the remote stop survives a comms-site or server failure must be visible, not assumed (section 4.4; analysis in ch-02-04). Verification: inspection; analysis.

10. Verification and validation

Verification of a deployment combines analysis, inspection and controlled tests [H]:

  • Walk-down and record inspection (REQ-02-02-001, -010): every node, its supply and its network path checked on site against the deployment record; the trailer's position compared with its recorded position.
  • Failure-domain tests (REQ-02-02-002): in a planned window, switch off one comms site and record which trucks and devices lose their link. The result is coverage evidence for the served_by data, and the only reliable way to settle questions such as whether R24 depends on CT3.
  • Failover tests (REQ-02-02-003 to -005): stop the primary, then remove power to its room, at a quiet time and with the fleet in a known state; check the timeline of section 6.3, the standby's log and the CR1 display. Repeat after every AMS release, since failover behaviour is part of compatibility (ch-02-01).
  • Time and log tests (REQ-02-02-006, -007): inject a clock offset on one truck, interrupt its link and check that the logs survive and are ordered correctly.

Validation asks whether the people can work with the deployment as built: whether a controller tells a failover from a site failure in time, and whether the Field Technician and controller follow section 8.3 in order. Drills of SCN-COMMS-R05 and SCN-AMS-RESTART serve both purposes; site acceptance testing and commissioning are in Volume 18 (ch-18-07).

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Calling a pair redundant without checking what it shares. Power, room, switch and software are the usual common causes.
  • Drawing failure domains as circles. Coverage follows terrain and line of sight; the domain must come from coverage data.
  • Forgetting the backhaul. A site with power can still be cut off; and a relay site's domain includes everything behind it.
  • Putting an edge function in the same failure domain as the radio it is meant to back up.
  • Treating time as free. Unsynchronised clocks make logs lie and reconciliation guess.
  • Keeping the deployment only in people's heads. CT3 moves; the record must move with it.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Treating eight comms-lost alarms as eight faults. Check the site status first.
  • Declaring the network restored on green lights. Follow the sequence: power, site, link, reconnection, reconciliation.
  • Forcing a truck to continue during reconciliation. It undermines the fail-closed design that made the outage safe.
  • Issuing permissions by radio "until the AMS is back" without the site procedure. The radio is for safety, not a substitute for the AMS's record of who is where.
  • Not escalating a failover because "it came back by itself". The cause must be found before it happens again.

Example 3 — what a failover costs. 37 trucks stopped from T+5 s to T+120 s: 37 × 115 = 4255 truck-seconds = 1.18 truck-hours, before the staged resume. At 50 km/h (13.9 m/s) a truck covers 69.4 m in the 5 s before it stops.

Example 4 — latency and distance. d=vΔt at 40 km/h for illustrative latencies: central 0.3 s → 3.33 m; edge 0.05 s → 0.56 m; onboard 0.01 s → 0.11 m. Only decisions that can tolerate metres of travel may be central.

Example 5 — redundancy and common cause. Unavailability per server u = 0.001 and of a shared supply up = 0.0005 [A]; 8760 hours a year. Single server: 8.76 h/year. Independent pair: u2 × 8760 = 0.0088 h/year. Pair on one supply:

U≈up+u2

which gives 4.39 h/year. The shared supply, not the servers, sets the availability.

Example 6 — clock offset reverses a log. Last extension issued at T−1 s (AMS clock), truck stopped at T+3 s (truck clock): gap 4 s. Truck clock 2 s behind: stop stamped T+1 s, order kept. Truck clock 5 s behind: stop stamped T−2 s, order reversed. During the 900 s outage the eight trucks buffer 7200 position records at one per second.

15. Key takeaways

  • The logical architecture says what each function owns; the physical deployment decides what fails together.
  • A failure domain is found per component and must be derived from data: the CT3 domain is named by the scenario and is not a circle around the trailer.
  • A component's domain for production can be much larger than its domain for communication: eight trucks lose the link, all 37 stop producing.
  • Redundancy is worth only its independence; a shared supply makes a pair little better than one server.
  • Every truck stopping on an AMS failover is the design working; the standby must rebuild truth before granting.
  • Deploy a function as close to the vehicle as its timing class requires, and no closer; moving it outward moves safety responsibility.
  • One time reference, records with source and sync status, and no single copy of any log.
  • Restoring a comms site is a sequence: power, site, link, reconnection, reconciliation — then resume.
