1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

At 14:05 on a DAY shift the AHS Controller's screen fills with communication-lost alarms: two trucks on lower R05, two on R09, two on R10, two on R11. Within a minute the Pit Supervisor is on the radio: "Send the bench trucks out by the west ramp, then." But the west-ramp detour starts on R09, and R09 is one of the roads that has just gone silent. A Field Technician at the MIA asks whether to enter by AP-02 or AP-04 to reach the pit-floor trailer CT3. And the person on call for the site network asks the question that decides how fast anything happens next: "What exactly are you seeing — which trucks, since when, and what still works?"

None of these is a radio-engineering question. They are about how the AHS depends on its network:

  • What is the mine network made of, and which AHS traffic crosses which part of it?
  • When one comms site fails, which functions are lost where — and which, like voice radio and the remote stop, are not on the AHS data network at all?
  • Why does the loss of CT2, a fixed tower at the crest, cost far more than the loss of CT3, the trailer whose failure is the reference scenario?
  • Which pit exit and which access point remain usable under valid AMS authority when a site is down or degraded?
  • How must the network be divided so that a field terminal cannot become a path to the trucks?

This chapter covers:

  • the layered mine network — vehicle, access, backhaul, core and control room — and the traffic classes that cross it;
  • the dependency map of the reference mine: which operational functions each comms site carries, per area, from the serving data in network.yaml;
  • single points of failure created by the backhaul chain CT3 → CT2 → CT1 → core, and redundancy options with their cost in complexity;
  • network segmentation as the input from the network to the security zones of ch-04-09 (SCN-CYBER-FT);
  • how the loss of position reports and field-terminal messages is made detectable on both sides;
  • pit exits and manned entry under valid authority during an outage or a degradation (SCN-COMMS-R05).

The AMS is the handbook's term for the layer that coordinates traffic [H]. Every serving area, backhaul link, power supply and link parameter is an illustrative assumption of the reference mine [A] (network.yaml). No vendor product is described, and no latency, loss or capacity value is a recommended design value. Radio standards appear only as topics (ch-11-02).

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Describe the layered network that connects control room CR1, FMS / AMS servers, comms sites CT1–CT3 and on-board units, and assign each traffic class (AMS control, permissions, telemetry, voice, video, position reports, RTK corrections from GB1) to the layers it crosses.
  2. Analyse single points of failure in the reference mine topology, in particular the mobile trailer CT3, and propose redundancy options with their cost in complexity.
  3. Specify segmentation boundaries between the AHS network, mine IT and plant networks as inputs to the cybersecurity design in ch-04-09.
  4. Specify how position reports and field-terminal permission messages are carried so that their loss is detectable both by AMS and by the device in the field.
  5. Analyse which pit exits (R05 via P1, R24 via P3) remain usable under valid AMS authority when CT2 or CT3 fails, given the marginal coverage of R05, R09 and R24.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Identify which operational functions (AMS authority, field-terminal access requests, RTK, voice radio, position reporting) are lost in each area when CT1, CT2 or CT3 fails.
  2. Explain to the Pit Supervisor why moving CT3 with the pit floor is an AHS change that needs planning, not only a network task.
  3. Decide which pit exit and access point (AP-02 or AP-04) to use for haul traffic and manned entry when one comms site is degraded.
  4. Report a suspected comms-site fault to the network team with the information they need (affected trucks and segments, time of onset, symptoms seen on the HMI).

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine has three comms sites (mine.yaml): CT1, a fixed tower at the MIA; CT2, a fixed tower at the pit crest 54 m from P1 (ch-02-02); and CT3, a mobile trailer on the pit floor 158 m from Intersection Z. GB1, the RTK base station, stands at the MIA. mine.yaml gives every road segment a coverage class — good, marginal or poor — and network.yaml, added for this volume, records which site serves which part of each segment, how each site reaches the core, and how it is powered [A].

Three facts in that data shape every chapter of Volume 11.

The pit climbs out through marginal coverage. Both ramps, R05 and R24, are marginal, as are R09–R11, R19, R20 and R04; R22 is poor. Loaded trucks spend most time at low speed on the roads with the weakest links.

CT3 serves an area no other site reaches. Lower R05 runs along the wall below CT2, in its radio shadow; from 1000 m below P1 to P2 only CT3 serves it. The bench roads R09, R10 and R11 and the loading links R19 and R20 are served by CT3 alone. When CT3 loses power (SCN-COMMS-R05), 1981.7 m of road — 30.4 % of AA-North — has no serving site. The eight trucks on it stop within their granted paths, and because lower R05 and R09 are the first segments of both exits from the pit floor, no truck in the pit can leave under valid authority.

The backhaul is a chain. CT3 reaches the core by a radio link to CT2; CT2 by fibre to CT1; CT1 by fibre to the core in the CR1 server room. CT3's traffic therefore depends on CT2 and CT1 as well as on itself. A failure of CT2 takes CT3's area with it and removes service from 20 of the 25 segments — 12.49 km of the 15.27 km road network, every production area of the mine. A failure of CT1 removes all of it.

CT3 fails most visibly, because it runs on a trailer generator and moves with the pit; CT1 and CT2 cost most when they fail, because everything behind them on the chain goes too. Controllers, Pit Supervisors and the network team need one picture of these dependencies, drawn from the same data, before the first alarm.

3. Terminology and foundations

Mine network. The communication infrastructure that carries AHS traffic between the vehicles, the field devices and the central systems: on-board radios, comms sites, backhaul links and the network core (glossary mine-network).

Comms site. A location with radio equipment that gives vehicles and devices access to the network — CT1, CT2 and CT3 at the reference mine (glossary comms-site). A mobile comms trailer is a comms site on a towable trailer with its own power, moved as the pit advances; CT3 is one.

Backhaul. The links that connect comms sites to the network core, by fibre or by point-to-point radio. At the reference mine: CT3 → CT2 radio link, CT2 → CT1 fibre, CT1 → core fibre, GB1 → core fibre (network.yaml [A]). A site's backhaul chain is the sequence of sites its traffic crosses to reach the core; CT3's is CT3, CT2, CT1, core.

Network layer. In this handbook [H] the mine network has five layers:

  • vehicle — the on-board radio and the units behind it on each autonomous truck, and the position reporting unit, field terminal and radio of each manned vehicle;
  • access — the radio link between a vehicle or device and a comms site;
  • backhaul — the links from the comms sites to the core;
  • core — the network core and the servers in the CR1 server rooms (FMS, AMS primary and standby, log store, time source; ch-02-02);
  • control room — the displays and consoles in CR1.

Serving site. A comms site that serves a point on a road, as recorded in network.yaml. A point is served if at least one of its serving sites is up together with its whole backhaul chain. Where two sites serve the same span the coverage overlaps; where the serving site changes along a road there is a handover (ch-11-02). The coverage class of mine.yaml says how good the link is; the serving site says which equipment it depends on.

Traffic class. A group of messages with the same purpose, timing and consequence of loss, handled alike by the network (glossary traffic-class). The handbook uses eight classes on the AHS data network (section 4.3); voice and the remote stop are outside it.

Network segmentation. Dividing the network into segments that can exchange traffic only through controlled boundaries, so that a device on one segment cannot reach equipment on another (glossary network-segmentation). It is the network's means of implementing the trust boundaries of ch-04-09.

Valid AMS authority. A truck may drive only within a granted path that the AMS has issued and the truck has received (ch-10-04). A road has valid authority only where the AMS can reach the truck. A pit exit is usable under valid authority if a loaded truck can reach the exit crest along a route on which every segment is served.

Single point of failure and failure domain. A single point of failure is a component whose failure alone stops a function (glossary single-point-of-failure). Its failure domain is everything that loses service when it fails (ch-02-02). For a comms site the failure domain includes every site behind it on the backhaul.

Dependency map. A table, derived from recorded data, of which operational functions remain in each area for a given component failure [H]. LAB-11-01-01 builds it for the reference mine.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 The layered mine network

FIG-11-01-01 draws the reference mine's network in its five layers.

Read from the bottom up, a granted-path extension for DT17 on R08 leaves the AMS primary in server room A, crosses the network core, the CT1 fibre and the CT2 fibre to the crest tower, and goes out on CT2's radio to DT17's on-board radio — or on CT3's, since both serve R08. A position report from LV07 on R06 takes the reverse path. An RTK correction enters at the core from GB1, which has its own fibre, and then follows the same path down to the truck.

Three things are drawn outside the layers on purpose:

  • Voice radio. network.yaml records voice as a separate trunked radio system that does not use CT1–CT3 [A], consistent with ch-02-02 and ch-01-06: in SCN-COMMS-R05 the Field Technician at CT3 reports by voice radio while CT3 is down (ch-02-02), and positive communication keeps voice and the digital exchange as two channels (ch-01-06). A voice repeater on CT3's mast or supply would share CT3's failure domain (section 7.2).
  • The remote stop. The stop radio RSR, the console RST-CR1, the portable transmitters RST-P1 and RST-P2 and a stop receiver on each truck (equipment.yaml, ch-02-04) do not use the site network or the AMS; the network carries only stop state reports. This is zone Z7 of ch-04-09.
  • Mine IT and the plant (zone Z6) reach the core only through boundary TB1.

Each layer answers a different question when something fails. Vehicle: one truck? Access: one place? Backhaul: everything behind a site? Core: everything? Control room: only what the controller sees?

AMS control, permissions and position reports cross every layer, and none has a path that avoids the comms site serving the vehicle. RTK corrections enter the core from GB1, so a failure of GB1 or its fibre degrades localisation everywhere while every other class keeps working — a picture unlike a comms-site failure. Priorities between classes are the subject of ch-11-03.

"Lost" means that AMS authority, field-terminal requests, position reports and RTK corrections are all lost there; "kept" that all four work. In every column voice radio and the remote stop are kept — voice by assumption [A], the remote stop by design (ch-02-04).

In no area does a comms-site failure remove some network functions and leave others: they share one path to the vehicle. What differs is the consequence of the loss, set by design: trucks stop within their granted paths (ch-02-08, ch-10-04), permissions fail closed (ch-02-07, ch-10-06), stale positions keep areas locked (ch-02-04). Nothing on the vehicle side waits for the network.

With CT3 down, the loaded routes of all four loading units cross lost road: EX01 and EX02 on lower R05; EX03 on R19, R10, R09 and R05; EX04 on R20, R11, R09 and R05. All eight SCN-COMMS-R05 trucks of LAB-02-08-01 — DT21 and DT32 on lower R05 (at 1189.6 m and 1050.0 m from P1), DT24 and DT35 on R09, DT26 and DT20 on R10, DT33 and DT37 on R11 — stand on road that only CT3 serves. The registry names lower R05 and R09–R11; the serving data add R19 and R20, where a truck loading at EX03 or EX04 would also have been lost.

4.5 Segmentation boundaries as inputs to ch-04-09

Ch-04-09 places seven security zones over the architecture (used here unchanged). Zone Z3 is the site wireless network — CT1, CT2, CT3 and their links — and two trust boundaries cross it: TB3 between the central systems (Z2) and the trucks (Z4), and TB4 between the manned-vehicle devices (Z5) and the central systems. TB1 links Z2 with mine IT, planning and remote access (Z6). Z7, the remote stop, has no boundary with Z2 or Z3.

This chapter divides the traffic of Z3 into four network segments [A] that make those boundaries true on the network:

Segment Traffic classes Endpoints Boundary implemented What must be impossible
Truck segment AMS control, telemetry and event upload, video, map and software distribution AMS and FMS servers (Z2); on-board units of DT01–DT40 (Z4) TB3 Any endpoint other than the central servers sending to a truck
Device segment Digital access permissions, position reports Field terminals and PRUs (Z5); the AMS device gateway (Z2) TB4 A device reaching a truck, another device, or any server other than the device gateway
Management segment Network and device management Comms-site equipment and radios (Z3); network-management systems of the network team (Z2) Inside Z3 / Z2 Configuration of a site from the truck or device segment, or from Z6 except through TB1 and change control
Correction segment RTK corrections GB1 (source); trucks (receivers) TB3 (one way) Any source other than GB1 injecting corrections

Three design rules follow [H]:

  • No device-to-truck path. A terminal and a truck may share a radio, never a segment: ch-04-09's rule that no message from Z5 reaches Z4 except through the AMS's decisions becomes a property of the network (REQ-11-01-004).
  • One way in from outside. Z6, including C01's ready / not ready signal, enters only through TB1 at the core.
  • Segments fail together. Segmentation changes who can talk to whom, not what fails: a CT3 outage removes all four segments in CT3's area.

SCN-CYBER-FT (section 6.4) shows what the segments contribute: the malformed requests from LV07's terminal could reach only the AMS device gateway, and the network can say through which site they entered.

The segment names, their number and the assignment of classes are assumptions for teaching [A]. How a site implements segments — and which standard it follows for zones and conduits — is a security design question for ch-04-09 and the site; IEC 62443 is cited there only within its catalogue scope [F] (SRC-STD-IEC62443).

4.6 Making loss detectable on both sides

A permission and a position report are only as good as the two ends' knowledge of whether they arrived. Ch-02-07 gives every message a sequence number and a timestamp; this chapter adds what the network must allow [H]:

  • The AMS must know a device has gone quiet. Periodic, numbered PRU reports make absence visible; the AMS marks a position stale after a configured time, shows its age and keeps the area locked (ch-02-04).
  • The device must know it is not heard. A field terminal shows a request as not confirmed until the AMS's confirmation arrives, tells its user to use voice radio if it does not (fail closed, ch-10-06), and shows its own link state from the AMS's periodic messages.
  • Both sides must tell late from lost. On marginal road losses come in bursts: a short rule raises events on a working link; a long one detects a real outage later.

LAB-11-01-01 measures that trade-off with the burst-loss models of network.yaml [A], for a report every second:

Coverage class Mean loss Mean burst (reports) False stale per device-hour, N = 1 N = 2 N = 3 N = 5
good 0.0045 2.5 13.29 1.88 0.59 0.04
marginal 0.0727 5.0 131.74 57.03 31.49 9.9
poor 0.2714 6.67 329.62 181.28 129.28 67.77

Here N is the number of consecutive missing reports after which the AMS shows a position as stale, so a real outage is noticed after N seconds. On a good segment, three missing reports almost never happen by chance (0.59 a device-hour); on a marginal segment they happen 31.49 times a device-hour, and even five happen 9.9 times. A single rule for the whole mine is either slow on good road or noisy on marginal road. The design consequence is not a threshold value — the site sets those [A] — but a display that distinguishes late (some reports missing, within the class's normal bursts) from stale (beyond them), and thresholds that are configured per coverage class (REQ-11-01-005).

The field terminal faces the same bursts. If one attempt needs both the request and its confirmation to arrive, the lab gives the probability that a request is confirmed within k attempts:

Coverage class k = 1 k = 2 k = 3 k = 5
good 0.9921 0.9982 0.9998 1.0
marginal 0.8899 0.9453 0.967 0.9899
poor 0.638 0.7581 0.8246 0.9042

On marginal road about one first attempt in nine fails; on poor road, such as R22 at the dump, more than a third. A terminal that retries silently would leave its user waiting; one that shows not confirmed — use radio turns a network property into a clear instruction. Repeated requests must also be idempotent at the AMS, so that a retry is never a second permission (ch-02-07).

4.7 Pit exits and manned entry under valid authority

A loaded truck in the pit can leave by R05 to P1 (AP-02) or by R09, W and R24 to P3 (AP-04). usable_exits in ahs_sim.network asks, for a start point and an outage, whether the shortest route to each crest that avoids lost road still uses that exit's ramp.

Outage From P2, Z, EX01-Q, EX02-Q, EX03-Q, EX04-Q From W
None, or GB1 down R05-P1 and R24-P3 R05-P1 and R24-P3
CT3 down none R24-P3 only
CT2 down none none
CT1 down none none

With CT3 down the west ramp itself is served — R24 is CT2's — but the way to it is not: every route from the pit floor to W uses R09, and every route from the bench queue points to W uses R10 or R11. Only a truck standing at W could climb R24 under authority. This is the network-level reason why rerouting over the west ramp does not help in SCN-COMMS-R05 (ch-16-07). With CT2 down both ramps are lost: upper R05 and R24 are served by CT2 alone.

For manned entry the question is how far a person can drive while their field terminal and PRU still work. The lab follows AA-North road from each access point while it is served:

Outage AP-02 (P1) AP-04 (P3)
None All 12 AA-North segments All 12 AA-North segments
CT3 down R05 to 1000.0 m from P1; then 339.6 m of lost road to P2 R24 to W; then all of R09 (462.0 m) lost to Z
CT2 or CT1 down Not covered at the access point Not covered at the access point

With CT3 down the pit floor itself — R06, R07, R08 and the EX01 and EX02 loading links, where CT3's trailer pad lies — is still served by CT2. It is an island: reachable only across lost road, by 339.6 m of lower R05 from AP-02 or by the 462.0 m of R09 from AP-04. On that lost stretch the person's position is not reported and their terminal cannot send a release; two stopped trucks stand on each stretch (DT21 and DT32 on lower R05, DT24 and DT35 on R09). The crossing must therefore be planned on voice radio, with reporting points before and after it, under the controls of ch-16-07.

Degraded, not failed. A site that is up but performing badly raises loss and latency on the road only it serves. The exposure of an exit route to a degraded site is the length of it that only that site serves:

From Route Length (m) Only CT3 (m) Only CT2 (m)
EX02-Q via R05 to P1 2567.8 339.6 1000.0
EX02-Q via R09, R24 to P3 2334.2 462.0 1425.0
EX03-Q via R05 to P1 3009.7 1228.7 1000.0
EX03-Q via R24 to P3 1852.2 427.2 1425.0

With CT2 degraded, R05 is the less exposed exit from both loading areas. With CT3 degraded, the ore trucks from EX02 are slightly less exposed on R05 (339.6 m against 462.0 m), but the bench trucks from EX03 are far less exposed on R24 (427.2 m against 1228.7 m), because their R05 route crosses R10, R09 and lower R05. A degradation can therefore justify moving bench traffic to the west ramp at the cost of a longer haul to D01 (not computed here). Whether to do so is an operational decision (section 8.2); the network only says where the exposure is.

4.8 Single points of failure and redundancy options

The backhaul chain is the dominant single point of failure. LAB-11-01-01 compares four options [A] with the reference. None is part of the reference mine; PF2 is a hypothetical second pit site with no position or coverage model (no mine.yaml entry).

Option What it adds Sites Backhaul links Overlap segments CT1 down CT2 down CT3 down
O0 reference — 3 3 6 25 seg., 15.27 km, 0 exits 20 seg., 12.49 km, 0 exits 6 seg., 1.98 km, 0 exits
O1 CT2 dual-homed Second, separately routed backhaul CT2 → core 3 4 6 4 seg., 1.88 km, 2 exits unchanged unchanged
O2 CT3 dual-homed Second backhaul CT3 → CT1, bypassing CT2 3 4 6 unchanged 10 seg., 8.41 km, 0 exits unchanged
O3 second pit site PF2 overlapping CT3's area, backhaul to CT2 4 4 11 unchanged unchanged 0 seg., 0.0 km, 2 exits
O4 combined O1 + O2 + PF2 backhauled to CT1 + CT1 also serving R01–R03, R21, R23, R25 4 6 17 4 seg., 1.88 km, 2 exits 4 seg., 3.75 km, 0 exits 0 seg., 0.0 km, 2 exits

"Exits" are pit exits usable from P2. The options teach four lessons.

  1. Backhaul redundancy removes chain effects, nothing else. O1 shrinks a CT1 failure from the whole mine to the four roads only CT1 serves (R13–R16, 1.88 km) and leaves both exits open. O2 keeps CT3's area alive when CT2 fails, but no exit opens, because both ramps are CT2's.
  2. Only overlapping coverage removes a site's own area. O3 alone cures SCN-COMMS-R05: a CT3 failure loses no road and both exits stay open. Backhauled through CT2, it adds nothing when CT2 fails.
  3. The ramps are a coverage problem. Even O4 loses upper R05 and R24 when CT2 fails (3.75 km with R04 and R22), and with them both exits. Only a site that can see the ramps would change that (ch-11-04).
  4. Every option costs complexity. Each link is equipment to power, monitor, secure and record; each overlap adds handover boundaries (ch-11-02). O4 doubles the backhaul links and nearly triples the overlap, and a second path that has silently failed looks redundant on paper. Unmonitored redundancy is a belief, not a property (REQ-11-01-003).

A second backhaul is also a radio-engineering claim that must be checked in the field. The lab's mast-top distances are 1398.2 m for the existing CT3–CT2 link, 2358.5 m for CT2–CT1 and 3696.8 m for a candidate CT3–CT1 link, and the assumed pit shell shows line of sight for all three. That shell smooths walls and benches (terrain_rl); a "yes" from it is not a survey (ch-11-04).

None of the options touches power, the trigger of SCN-COMMS-R05. A battery on the trailer that bridges the time to reach it would shorten the outage, and a power alarm reaching the core independently of CT3's radio link would let the controller tell a power failure from a backhaul failure (ch-02-02 section 7.1) [R].

4.9 Trade-offs and model limits

  • Fixed versus mobile sites. A trailer follows the pit; the price is own power, a radio backhaul through another site and a serving area that changes with every move (SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE).
  • Chain versus star backhaul. A chain is cheap and follows the geometry; a star removes chain effects but needs a path from the pit floor to the core that may not exist.
  • Segmentation versus operability. More segments narrow what a compromised device can reach and make the network harder to diagnose; four is the smallest set that implements TB3 and TB4 separately [A].
  • Model limits. The dependency map uses serving spans, not measured coverage; it has no capacity, latency budget or queueing (ch-11-03), no time dimension (ch-11-07) and no packet-level simulation. Voice is modelled only as present or absent. All values are illustrative [A].

Network team (site function [A]) Operates comms sites, backhaul and the management segment; keeps serving data and the deployment record current; diagnoses site and backhaul faults Change serving areas, segments or sites without AHS change management

The network team is a site function [A], not one of the seven mine.yaml roles. At the reference mine it is the communications team of ch-02-02 and ch-04-09; in the field it works through the Field Technician. Two boundaries need written procedure: between the AHS Controller, who sees the symptoms, and the network team, who can diagnose the cause (section 8.3); and between the network team, who owns the equipment, and AHS engineering, who own the serving data on which the AMS and the dependency map rely (REQ-11-01-001).

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation

Every segment is served, both exits are usable from every pit start point, and the site status shows each comms site, backhaul link and GB1. A network that works but is not healthy — CT3 low on fuel, the CT3–CT2 link losing more than usual — is where the next outage starts, so normal operation includes watching backhaul and power.

6.2 Degraded operation — SCN-COMMS-R05 (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

The registry has four key events: CT3 down at T+0 s; trucks detect link loss and stop within their granted paths at T+3 s; trucks marked communication-lost and surrounding reservations frozen at T+10 s; CT3 restored and reconciliation at T+900 s. The rows at T+20, 60, 90 and 120 s are those of ch-01-03 and ch-02-02; T+200, T+419.3 s and the remote-stop latch are those of ch-16-07; T+480 s is that of ch-02-02; T+932 and T+962 s are those of ch-02-08. The rows at T+30 s and T+180 s are this chapter's additions [A]. T+0 s is 14:05:00 (ch-02-02).

The network fails at one place and the AHS reacts at every layer: vehicles at T+3 s, core at T+10 s, control room from T+20 s. At T+30 s the network adds localisation: pit-floor trucks, which CT3 also serves, still report through CT2, so the cause lies in CT3 or its link to CT2. Whether CT3 lost power or only its link the view cannot say — both look like silence — which needs the technician at the pad or an independent power alarm (section 4.8).

The technician's devices work down R05 to 1000 m below P1, stop on the 339.6 m of lower R05 where DT21 and DT32 stand, and work again on the pit floor. In the lost stretch the technician is visible only by voice radio, which is why ch-16-07 latches an area remote stop for the passage.

6.3 Abnormal operation — loss of CT2 (illustrative case [A])

No registered scenario covers CT2. As a contrast case, suppose that CT2 loses power on a DAY shift [A]. The dependency map says what follows: every truck on 20 of the 25 segments loses its link and stops within its granted path; only R12–R16 — the roads around V, PK1, FB1, G01 and the MIA, served by CT1 — keep service. Both pit exits, the crusher road, the dump road and the dump are lost. Field terminals and PRUs work only in the CT1 area; neither access point to AA-North is covered. CT3 is up on its own trailer power, but with no backhaul it serves nothing. Trucks at PK1 and FB1 can still be dispatched; nothing else can.

Comms-lost alarms come from every area except the MIA side, and the site status shows CT2 down and CT3 unreachable. A controller who reads the second line as a CT3 fault has misread a backhaul effect; the status must show CT3 unreachable because of CT2 (REQ-11-01-002). A CT2 outage is coded as communication-loss stops per truck (DLY-OD-05), not as a site-wide loss (DLY-SE-02) — coding_rules (section 8.4).

6.4 Abnormal operation — SCN-CYBER-FT seen from the network

The registry's timeline (T+0 s request rejected, T+10 s security alert acknowledged and incident process started) is walked in ch-04-09. From the network, three facts matter. The terminal is at MIA-WS and attaches through CT1, the only site serving R16. Its traffic is on the device segment, so the malformed requests could reach the AMS device gateway and nothing else. And the network team can confirm, from the management segment's records, through which site and at what times the terminal was attached — evidence the AMS log alone does not contain. Nothing in the scenario touches the truck segment.

6.5 Planned change — SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE

The registry scenario (primary chapters ch-11-02, ch-11-04) shows why a move is an AHS change: at T−3600 s the Pit Supervisor agrees the window, reassigns the EX03 and EX04 trucks and restricts the affected segments; at T+0 s CT3 goes off air; at T+5400 s it is on air at the new position; at T+7200 s Survey records the drive test; at T+9000 s restrictions are lifted in stages. The move changes the serving spans, and with them the dependency map, the usable exits and the manned-entry reach (REQ-11-01-008).

7. Engineering view

7.1 Design decisions and trade-offs

  • One network, segmented. Separate radio systems for devices and trucks would remove shared failure as well as shared trust, at the price of a second set of sites. The reference mine uses one segmented data network [A] and keeps the two functions that must survive its failure — voice and the remote stop — on separate systems.
  • Authority only over served road. The AMS determines usable exits and routes from the serving data and the site state, never from the road network alone (REQ-11-01-009). A route through a dead zone is not a route.
  • Serving data as configuration. Which site serves which span is configuration under change control, held once and read by the AMS, the dependency map and the site status. A drawing on a wall is not.
  • Detection on both ends. Periodic, numbered position reports and confirmed field-terminal requests make absence visible; class-specific staleness rules keep false events tolerable on marginal road (section 4.6).
  • Redundancy where it removes most. O1 removes the largest chain effect for one link; O3 cures the reference scenario. Which to build is a site decision, made by comparing failure domains and complexity from the same data [R].

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along the shared timeline

  • T+0 to T+3 s — detection on the vehicle. Each truck notices missing AMS messages and stops within its granted path. The network's only role is to have stopped delivering; the reaction is wholly onboard.
  • T+3 to T+10 s — detection in the core. The AMS's communication-lost timeout is longer than the vehicles' (ch-02-02), so the AMS never shows as connected a truck that has already stopped for link loss.
  • T+10 to T+30 s — localisation. The network-health view shows sites and backhaul links separately, beside which trucks still report, so that "CT3 or its link" is read at T+30 s. Its data travel on the management segment, lost in CT3's area too, so a site that stops reporting is shown unknown, not up.
  • T+120 to T+419.3 s — manned entry. Field-terminal and PRU traffic of the technician leave coverage at 1000 m below P1 and return on the pit floor; the AMS must show the technician's position as stale during the crossing, not as stationary at the last report (ch-02-04).
  • T+900 to T+962 s — restoration. Reconnection is staggered as trucks re-register; the AMS reconciles before it grants (ch-02-08), and the network team declares CT3 restored only with the confirmations of REQ-02-02-009. Telemetry and event uploads buffered during the outage must not delay AMS control traffic on CT3's link when everything reconnects at once (priorities: ch-11-03).

Decision aid — which exit and which access point [H]:

  1. Is the site down or degraded? If down, use the exit table of section 4.7: with CT3 down there is no exit from the pit floor or bench — hold at AP-02 and AP-04; with CT2 or CT1 down, no exit at all.
  2. If degraded, compare the exposure of each exit route to the degraded site (section 4.7). With CT2 degraded keep R05. With CT3 degraded keep ore traffic on R05 and consider bench waste traffic on R24, weighing the longer haul.

8.3 Procedure excerpt — reporting a suspected comms-site fault

Step Role Action
1 AHS Controller Acknowledge the comms-lost alarms; open the site status and the network-health view.
2 AHS Controller Determine the pattern: one area (site), all but one area (backhaul), or localisation everywhere (GB1).
3 AHS Controller Check that the trucks in the area have stopped within their granted paths and that their reservations are frozen; do not release anything.
4 AHS Controller Inform the Pit Supervisor: affected area, trucks, usable exits (from the dependency map), held access points.
5 AHS Controller Report to the network team by telephone or voice radio, then in writing: suspected site; onset time (first alarm, AMS clock); affected segments and trucks; what still works (sites up, trucks still reporting nearby); symptoms on the HMI; RTK state; recent changes or work at the site.
6 Network team Confirm receipt; state what the management segment shows; give the first estimate and the next update time.
7 Pit Supervisor Decide manned entry (section 8.2) if a Field Technician must go to the site.
8 Field Technician On arrival, report by voice radio what is found (power, equipment, position) and the expected restoration time.
9 AHS Controller When the site is reported restored, wait for re-registration and reconciliation before any release (ch-02-08); record each confirmation with time and role (REQ-02-02-009).

At T+180 s in SCN-COMMS-R05 the report of step 5 reads, for example: "Suspect CT3. Onset 14:05:00. Comms-lost: DT21, DT32 on lower R05; DT24, DT35 on R09; DT26, DT20 on R10; DT33, DT37 on R11. Pit-floor trucks on R06–R08 still reporting; CT2 and CT1 up; RTK normal elsewhere. No work planned at CT3 today." That is the information of REQ-11-01-010.

8.5 Training and competency implications

  • Controllers: read site, backhaul or GB1 from the alarm pattern; know the remaining exits and access points; practise the fault report of section 8.3.
  • Pit Supervisors: a CT3 move changes which roads have authority; a degraded site can justify moving traffic.
  • Field Technicians and drivers: where terminals and PRUs stop working, what not confirmed means, how to use reporting points across lost road.
  • Network team: chain effects, outage cost in truck-hours, serving data as AHS configuration.

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-11-01-001 [H] The integration authority shall keep, for each comms site, backhaul link and GNSS base station, a dependency map that states per area which AHS functions (AMS authority, digital access permissions, position reports, RTK corrections, voice radio, remote stop) are lost when that component fails. The map shall be derived from the recorded serving data and backhaul chains and regenerated whenever either changes. Rationale: controllers, Pit Supervisors and the network team must share one picture of what each failure costs (sections 4.4, 8.1); builds on REQ-02-02-002. Verification: inspection of the map against network.yaml; test (LAB-11-01-01, dependency map cases)

REQ-11-01-002 [H] The control-room site status shall show each comms site, each backhaul link and each GNSS base station separately, and shall show a site that cannot be reached because of a failure on its backhaul chain as unreachable via that component rather than as an independent failure. Rationale: a CT2 failure must not be read as a second fault at CT3 (section 6.3). Verification: test (simulated CT2 and CT3 outages in site test), inspection of the display

REQ-11-01-003 [H] Every component whose single failure leaves no pit exit usable under valid AMS authority from the pit floor shall be listed in the deployment record as a single point of failure, with the mitigation adopted or the decision to accept it. Where redundancy is adopted, each redundant path shall be monitored and its loss alarmed. Rationale: CT1, CT2 and CT3 each remove both exits (section 4.7); unmonitored redundancy is not redundancy (section 4.8). Verification: inspection of the deployment record; test (LAB-11-01-01, options); site test of each redundant path

REQ-11-01-004 [H] The site network shall carry AHS traffic in separate segments for trucks, manned-vehicle devices, network management and RTK corrections. A device on the device segment shall be able to reach only the AMS device gateway. Networks outside the AHS shall reach the AHS only through the core boundary TB1 of ch-04-09. Rationale: implements trust boundaries TB3, TB4 and TB1 on the network (section 4.5; SCN-CYBER-FT). Verification: inspection of the network configuration; test (attempted connections from the device segment to a truck, a site and a server other than the gateway)

REQ-11-01-005 [H] Position reports shall be periodic and carry a sequence number and timestamp. The AMS shall show the age of every position and distinguish late from stale, with staleness thresholds configurable per coverage class; a stale position shall keep the vehicle's permitted area locked. Rationale: burst loss on marginal road makes a single threshold either slow or noisy (section 4.6); ch-02-04. Verification: test (LAB-11-01-01 detection table; injected burst loss in SIL, ch-18-02); inspection

REQ-11-01-006 [H] A field terminal shall show a request as not confirmed until the AMS's confirmation is received, shall show its own link state, and shall instruct its user to use voice radio when no confirmation arrives within its configured time. The AMS shall treat repeated requests idempotently. Rationale: permissions fail closed; on marginal and poor road first attempts often fail (section 4.6); ch-02-07, ch-10-06. Verification: test (terminal in simulated loss); inspection of the HMI

REQ-11-01-007 [H] The deployment record shall state, for voice radio and for the remote stop, every component they share with CT1–CT3 — including masts, power supplies and enclosures — and each shared component shall be analysed for its effect during a comms-site failure. Rationale: their independence from the data network is what remains when a site fails (sections 4.1, 7.2); ch-04-04, REQ-02-02-010. Verification: inspection; analysis

REQ-11-01-008 [H] A move of a comms site, or any change to its serving area, backhaul or power, shall be handled as an AHS change: the affected segments shall be restricted before the site goes off air, the coverage at the new position shall be measured before the serving data are changed, and the dependency map shall be regenerated before the restrictions are lifted. Rationale: serving data determine authority, exits and alarms (section 6.5; SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE). Verification: inspection of the change record; demonstration in a planned move

REQ-11-01-009 [H] The AMS shall determine the pit exits and routes available under valid authority from the serving data and the current state of each comms site and backhaul link, and shall not plan or offer a route that includes road without a serving site that is up. Rationale: the west-ramp detour starts on R09, which CT3 alone serves (section 4.7). Verification: test (LAB-11-01-01 exit table; SIL outage cases, ch-18-02)

REQ-11-01-010 [H] The procedure for reporting a suspected comms-site fault shall require the suspected component, the onset time by the AMS clock, the affected segments and trucks, what is still working nearby, the symptoms on the HMI, the RTK state and any recent work at the site, and shall name the role that receives the report and the time of the next update. Rationale: the network team diagnoses from what the control room saw (sections 8.3, 6.2). Verification: inspection of the procedure; exercise

10. Verification and validation

  • Data consistency. The dependency map, the exit table and the site status must be generated from the same serving data. LAB-11-01-01 checks that its topology reproduces ahs_sim.network for every single outage and that the SCN-COMMS-R05 trucks of LAB-02-08-01 lie on CT3-only road.
  • Analysis. The options table compares failure domains; availability belongs to Volume 18.
  • Software-in-the-loop. Outages of CT2 and CT3 and burst loss on marginal segments are injected in SIL (ch-18-02) to check REQ-11-01-002, -005, -006 and -009.
  • Site test. Each site is taken off air in a planned window, trucks clear, to confirm that status, exits and devices behave as the map predicts; each redundant path is failed separately.
  • Coverage validation. Serving spans are confirmed by drive test after installation and after each move (ch-11-04, SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE).
  • Segmentation test. Connection attempts from the device segment to trucks, sites and servers other than the gateway are expected to fail and to be logged.
  • No standard clause is cited for these tests, and no regulatory expectation is asserted.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Failure domains drawn as circles instead of read from serving data.
  • Treating CT3 as the weak point and forgetting that CT2 and CT1 carry it.
  • A second backhaul that nobody monitors.
  • Field terminals and trucks on one segment because they share a radio.
  • An unrecorded voice repeater on a comms-site mast and supply.
  • One staleness threshold for good and marginal road.
  • A detour planned from the road network alone.

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Sending trucks to the west ramp during a CT3 outage.
  • Reading "CT3 unreachable" during a CT2 outage as a second fault and dispatching to the pit floor.
  • Letting a person cross lost road without voice-radio reporting points.
  • Reporting "the network is down" to the network team without area, trucks, onset time and what still works.
  • Treating a CT3 move as a network job and lifting restrictions before the drive test.
  • Releasing frozen reservations because the site lights are green, before reconciliation.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — the CT3 failure domain in metres. Lost road with CT3 down (lab): lower R05 from 1000.0 to 1339.6 m (339.6 m), R09 462.0 m, R10 427.2 m, R11 360.6 m, R19 212.1 m, R20 180.3 m; total 1981.7 m. AA-North has 6511.9 m of road, so the share is 1981.7 / 6511.9 = 30.4 %. With CT2 down the lost road is 12.49 km of 15.27 km: 81.8 %. CT3's domain is the more frequent; CT2's is about six times longer.

Example 2 — the pit-floor island. With CT3 down, R06, R07, R08, R17 and R18 are still served by CT2. From AP-02 a technician is in coverage for 1000.0 m of R05, crosses 339.6 m of lost road and is in coverage again at P2. From AP-04 coverage reaches W after 1425.0 m of R24; R09, 462.0 m, is lost to Z. AP-02 crosses the shorter lost stretch, and that is the access point ch-16-07 uses.

Example 3 — degraded CT3 and the bench traffic. A loaded truck from EX03-Q to P1 crosses R10, R09 and lower R05: 427.2 + 462.0 + 339.6 = 1228.8 m, which the lab reports as 1228.7 m from unrounded lengths. To P3 it crosses only R10: 427.2 m. Moving bench traffic to R24 while CT3 is degraded cuts its CT3-only exposure by about two thirds.

Example 4 — why three missing reports are not an outage on R05. The marginal burst model has g = 0.020 and b = 0.20 (network.yaml). Then πB=0.020/0.220=0.0909 and the mean loss is 0.9091×0.010+0.0909×0.70=0.0727; a bad spell lasts 1/0.20=5 reports on average. Since a bad spell loses 70 % of reports and lasts five, runs of three losses are common: the lab counts 31.49 per device-hour.

Example 5 — what a second backhaul for CT2 buys. In the reference, CT1's failure domain is all 25 segments (15.27 km) because CT2 and CT3 reach the core through CT1. With O1, a separately routed fibre from CT2 to the core, it shrinks to R13–R16 (1.88 km) and both exits stay open. One added link removes 13.39 km from CT1's domain; it changes nothing for a CT2 or CT3 failure.

15. Key takeaways

  • The mine network has five layers; AMS control, permissions and position reports cross all of them, and none can avoid the comms site serving the vehicle.
  • A comms-site failure removes every data-network function in its area at once; voice and the remote stop remain because they are separate systems — voice by assumption, which the deployment record must confirm.
  • Through the backhaul chain CT3 → CT2 → CT1, CT2's failure domain is 81.8 % of the road network and CT1's all of it; CT3's is 30.4 % of AA-North.
  • With CT3 down no pit exit is usable from the pit floor or bench; the pit floor is a served island reachable only across lost road.
  • Backhaul redundancy removes chain effects; only overlapping coverage removes a site's own area; the ramps are a coverage problem.
  • Segmentation turns the trust boundaries of ch-04-09 into properties of the network: no device-to-truck path.
  • Loss must be detectable at both ends, and staleness rules must respect the burst behaviour of each coverage class.
  • Moving CT3 changes authority, exits and alarms: it is an AHS change.
