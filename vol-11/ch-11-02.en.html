1. Overview

1.1 Why this chapter matters

Late on a DAY shift the AHS Controller notices a pattern on the network-health page. Over the last hour, several empty trucks descending R05 have shown a short "link degraded" flag, never long enough to stop them, and always at about the same place: some 1000 m below the crest P1. Loaded trucks climbing past the same point show it too, but rarely. Nothing has stopped. Then the Pit Supervisor calls with two items for next week: the pit-floor trailer CT3 must move to follow the floor, and Survey wants R05 closed for a windrow rework, which will send the whole pit over the west ramp R24.

The controller's flag and the supervisor's two plans are the same subject seen from three sides: how the radio network of an open pit hands trucks from one site to the next, how it is rebuilt as the pit moves, and how much it must carry when traffic concentrates on one ramp. The questions are:

  • Is the flag at 1000 m a fault, a coverage hole, or the normal signature of a handover?
  • Would a different radio technology — Wi-Fi mesh, private LTE or 5G, satellite — remove it, or move it?
  • How long may a handover interrupt AMS traffic before trucks slow down or stop?
  • What does moving CT3 do to the AHS, and how should the move be planned?
  • How much capacity does a cell need for 40 trucks, the ancillary fleet, voice and occasional video, and what changes when R24 carries all pit traffic?

This chapter covers:

  • what the AHS asks of any radio technology, and a qualitative comparison of Wi-Fi / mesh, private cellular and satellite links for the reference mine, without vendor or performance figures;
  • the handover model of the reference mine (network.yaml): where hard handovers lie on M-20481 and on the west-ramp detour, how long their gaps are, and how the gap relates to the granted-path horizon of ch-10-04;
  • how to tell a handover from a coverage hole and from a site failure in the data a control room has;
  • options for serving a moving pit face, and the planned relocation of CT3 (SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE) as the walkthrough;
  • a capacity worked example and the concentration on R24 during an R05 closure (SCN-MAP-R05).

Coverage estimation, drive tests and coverage attributes are the subject of ch-11-04; latency, loss and QoS of ch-11-03. Every rate, size, gap, horizon and threshold in this chapter is an illustrative assumption [A], not a recommended value. Statements about how a radio technology behaves are [TBV]: they must be verified for the system a site actually chooses.

1.2 Learning objectives — Engineering

After this chapter an engineering reader will be able to:

  1. Compare Wi-Fi mesh, private LTE / 5G and satellite links for an open pit on coverage, capacity, latency behaviour, mobility handling and deployment effort, without quoting vendor performance figures.
  2. Explain how handover between cells affects a truck descending R05 from CT2 to CT3 coverage, and model the resulting communication gap [A].
  3. Assess options for serving a moving pit face (mobile trailers, mesh relays, vehicle-mounted nodes) against the reference mine's pit progression.
  4. Calculate the capacity a cell must provide for the reference fleet (40 trucks, ancillary devices, voice and occasional video) as a worked example with stated [A] inputs.
  5. Explain how cell or mesh design must change when the west ramp R24 becomes the only pit exit during an R05 closure and carries the whole pit's traffic.

1.3 Learning objectives — Operations

After this chapter an operations reader will be able to:

  1. Distinguish the symptoms of handover problems from those of coverage holes in controller and network tools for trucks on R05.
  2. Plan, with the network team, the relocation of a mobile comms trailer as the pit advances, including the AHS impact window and the areas affected.
  3. Request network checks on R24 before a planned R05 closure diverts pit traffic onto the west ramp.
  4. Record and escalate repeated link drops at the same location so that they are treated as a coverage defect rather than as individual truck faults.

2. Operational motivation: the problem at the reference mine

The reference mine has three comms sites (mine.yaml, network.yaml) [A]:

Site Position Mast Backhaul Power Serves
CT1 MIA tower, RL 435 30 m fibre to the core in CR1 site supply MIA roads R13–R16; R12 together with CT2
CT2 Pit crest tower, 54 m from P1, RL 440 30 m fibre to CT1 own supply at the crest every segment not listed otherwise, incl. upper R05 (0–1000 m from P1), R24, R25, the haul roads and both destinations
CT3 Mobile trailer on the pit floor, RL 305 15 m radio link to CT2 trailer generator lower R05 (1000 m to P2), R09–R11, R19, R20; the pit floor R06–R08, R17, R18 together with CT2

Three facts follow from this table and drive the chapter.

Every haul cycle crosses a hard handover. Where two sites both serve a road, a truck can change site without a break. Where they do not, it cannot. On R05 CT2 serves the upper 1000 m and CT3 the rest; no site serves both sides, so every truck on R05 changes site at one point. With R05 open the baseline fleet passes it 155.5 times an hour, the same count of ramp passes that ch-01-07 found. network.yaml assumes that the AMS hears nothing from a truck for 1.5 s while it changes site [A].

The pit floor moves; CT3 must follow. CT3 is on a trailer because the floor advances. Each move takes it off air. While it is off air, the segments only it serves have no AMS authority. Those include lower R05 and R09, the starts of both pit exits, so a move done naively stops the whole pit (section 6.2).

Closing R05 concentrates traffic on one site. With R05 closed for rework (SCN-MAP-R05) the pit drives over R09, W, R24, P3 and R25. R24 is served by CT2 alone (network.yaml) and is classed marginal. It then carries 145.5 passes an hour, with about 9.6 trucks on it at any moment, and every truck changes site at Intersection W, where it may also be waiting for an intersection grant.

None of this depends on the radio technology. Any technology faces the same walls, the same moving floor and the same concentration on R24; it changes how the problems are solved and what that costs.

3. Terminology and foundations

Access network and backhaul. The access network is the radio link between a vehicle or field device and a comms site. The backhaul carries a site's traffic to the network core in CR1 (ch-11-01). At the reference mine CT3's backhaul is a radio link to CT2, so CT3 depends on CT2 (network.yaml; Network.backhaul_chain).

Comms site and mobile comms trailer. A comms site is a fixed or mobile radio site (glossary comms-site). A mobile comms trailer is a comms site built on a trailer so that it can be moved as the pit changes; CT3 is one.

Serving site. The site that carries a vehicle's traffic at a given place. network.yaml assigns serving sites per segment, either as a list (each site can serve the whole segment) or as spans along the segment [A].

Wi-Fi and mesh. Wireless LAN equipment built to the IEEE 802.11 family of standards; the current revision is IEEE 802.11-2024 (SRC-STD-IEEE80211, catalogue level only) [F]. In a mesh network nodes relay traffic for each other (glossary mesh-network), so a node far from any wired point can still reach the core over several radio hops.

Private cellular network. A cellular radio system (LTE or 5G) operated for one site's own use rather than as a public service. LTE and 5G are specified by 3GPP in numbered releases (SRC-STD-3GPP, catalogue level only) [F]. No feature of any release is relied on in this chapter.

Satellite link. A link through a satellite, used at mines mainly where no terrestrial route exists. Its behaviour depends on the orbit and the service [TBV].

Cell. The area one site (or one sector of a site) serves, for any technology.

Handover (radio). The change of a moving vehicle's serving site. In ahs_sim.network a handover is hard where the set of serving sites changes so that no site serves both sides: the truck must leave one site before the next one carries its traffic. Where coverage overlaps — a site common to both sides — the model counts no handover.

Handover gap. The time during which a vehicle has no usable link while it changes site. network.yaml assumes 1.5 s with no AMS traffic [A]. Real gaps depend on the technology, its configuration and the radio conditions at the boundary [TBV].

Link drop. In this chapter, a run of consecutive lost messages long enough to be counted by the network-health tool; LAB-11-02-01 counts three or more lost 0.5 s message slots (1.5 s) [A]. A link drop is not the same as the vehicle's link-loss detection, after which the truck stops within its granted path (3 s in SCN-COMMS-R05, [A]).

Granted path, horizon, class C stop. From ch-10-04: the granted path is what a truck may drive; the AMS extends it so that it reaches a horizon H ahead; the truck must always be able to stop within it, here with the class C stopping distance of ch-04-05.

Capacity and demand. Demand is the traffic a cell must carry (kbit/s); capacity is what a designed system can carry. This chapter calculates demand only.

4. Technical explanation

4.1 What the AHS asks of a radio network

Before any technology is compared, the AHS's demands should be written down, because they do not change with the technology. The handbook's architecture (ch-02-02, ch-02-07, ch-02-08) gives six of them:

  1. Authority must be continuous where trucks drive. Every reservation extension and every digital access permission crosses the network. Where the network is lost, the AMS cannot extend authority; the truck stops within its granted path, at the latest at its end. That the truck stays within its grant is set by design (ch-10-04), but it costs production.
  2. Vehicle-side safety must not depend on the network. A truck that loses its link stops within its granted path on its own (ch-02-08). The independent remote stop uses its own stop radio RSR, not the AHS data network (ch-04-04, equipment.yaml). A radio design may make the network better; it must never become a safety function.
  3. Permissions fail closed. A lost, stale or unacknowledged permission is not granted (ch-02-07, ch-10-06). A radio network that delays messages turns into stops, never into unsafe grants.
  4. Coverage must hold where people and trucks wait. Queue points, intersections, park-up PK1 and the access points AP-02 and AP-04 are where trucks and field crews spend time and request permissions (ch-11-04).
  5. Capacity must hold where traffic concentrates. The ramps, the pit floor and, during an R05 closure, R24.
  6. The network must follow the mine plan. The pit changes shape every week; a design that cannot be moved and re-verified cheaply will drift away from the roads it serves.

GB1's RTK corrections reach the trucks over the same sites (network.yaml), so wherever the AHS link is lost, RTK corrections are lost too. A site that fails therefore removes authority and degrades positioning in the same area — a common cause the design must expect (ch-05-02 treats GNSS and RTK).

4.2 Three families of radio technology

FIG-11-02-01 compares the three families on the criteria of the learning objectives. It is qualitative on purpose. Published figures for real equipment depend on configuration, spectrum and site conditions, and quoting them would turn a teaching comparison into an unverified product claim.

Wi-Fi / mesh. Many small nodes, each with a short reach, some relaying for others. The attraction for an open pit is flexibility: a relay node on a trailer, a light tower or a vehicle can be placed at a new face in hours, and the mesh finds a path back to a wired node. The cost is that every relay hop shares airtime with the hops before it, so capacity and latency at the end of a long chain depend on everything upstream [A]. Mobility is handled by the client moving from node to node; how quickly it does so, and whether a gap occurs, depends on the equipment and its settings [TBV]. Whether the bands used are licence-exempt or licensed is a spectrum question for the site (section 4.9) [TBV].

Private LTE / 5G. Fewer, larger cells from high sites, a core network that keeps track of every device, and handovers that the network controls [TBV]. The attraction is a planned, managed system with fewer sites to power. The cost is a core network to run (identities, subscriptions, software) and the same physics: a crest tower cannot see below a pit wall, so trailers or relays are still needed on the floor. Spectrum arrangements — licensed, shared or otherwise — are again a site question [TBV].

Satellite. At the reference mine a satellite link is not a candidate for per-truck AMS traffic [R]: a truck below a pit wall may not see the sky, and the path is long [TBV]. It is a candidate for backhaul from a remote installation or for non-control traffic.

A site may combine families — for example cellular access from the crest with mesh relays at the face, or a satellite backhaul for a remote dump — [A]. The combination does not change the six demands of section 4.1; it changes where the handovers lie and who operates which part.

4.3 The handover model of the reference mine

network.yaml encodes the serving sites; ahs_sim.network.handovers_along walks a route and reports every point at which the set of serving sites changes with no site common to both sides. handover_gaps_s turns each into a time window using per-leg speeds. LAB-11-02-01 runs both on M-20481 and on the detour of SCN-MAP-R05, with the steady-state speeds of ahs_sim.haul_cycle and the class C stops of ch-10-04:

Route Handover Distance along route Sites Speed Gap starts Driven in 1.5 s gap Class C stop
M-20481 loaded, EX02-L → C01 (4500.5 m) R05, 1000.0 m from P1 1773.9 m CT3 → CT2 11.4 km/h T+280.3 s 4.8 m 10.7 m
M-20481 empty, C01 → EX02-L R05, 1000.0 m from P1 2726.6 m CT2 → CT3 40.0 km/h T+299.1 s 16.7 m 84.0 m

Times are from the start of the loaded or empty trip; the speed is that of the leg on which the handover lies.

Three points are worth noticing.

  • One handover per trip, at a fixed place. The overlap on the pit floor (R06–R08 are served by CT2 and CT3) means trucks pick up CT3 without a break on the floor; the hard boundary is only where the CT3-only part of R05 meets the CT2-only part. That is a design choice of the serving map, not a property of the radio.
  • The empty descent is the critical case. At 40 km/h the truck covers 16.7 m in the gap and needs 84.0 m to stop.
  • On the detour the handover is at an intersection. R09 is CT3-only and R24 CT2-only, so the change happens exactly at W — where ch-10-04 found the reservation demand of the detour concentrated (W held 78.5 % of the time). A truck waiting at the W stop line for a movement grant that is delayed by a handover waits 1.5 s longer; a truck that has its grant crosses W without traffic.

4.4 The handover gap and the granted path

A handover gap is harmless if the truck still has enough granted path when it ends. Let the AMS extend the grant so that its end lies H ahead at every extension, let the worst case be that the last extension arrived one extension period te before the gap began, and let the gap last Tg. At the end of the gap the granted path ahead is H−v(te+Tg). The truck needs at least its class C stopping distance sC(v) there, so the margin is

m=H−v(te+Tg)−sC(v),Tcrit=H−sC(v)v−te

where Tcrit is the longest gap that needs no slowing. If m<0 the ADS must begin to slow before the gap ends; it never drives beyond what it can stop within (ch-10-04). LAB-11-02-01 uses te=1.0 s and the class C stops of ch-04-05 and ch-10-04 [A], and compares two horizons: the 200 m horizon of the ch-10-04 R05 snapshot, and the speed-adaptive horizon Hreq=sC+vte that ch-10-04 recommends to avoid holding road that is not needed.

Handover Margin, 200 m horizon Tcrit, 200 m Speed-adaptive horizon Its margin in a 1.5 s gap Horizon with gap allowance
R05, empty descent 88.3 m 9.44 s 95.1 m −16.7 m 111.7 m
R05, loaded climb 181.4 m 58.57 s 13.8 m −4.8 m 18.6 m
W → R24, loaded 173.3 m 43.89 s 20.6 m −6.1 m 26.7 m
W → R09, empty 127.8 m 16.83 s 59.7 m −12.5 m 72.2 m

Two lessons follow.

The vehicle's link-loss detection binds first. With a 200 m horizon the granted path would run short only for gaps longer than 9.44 s on the empty descent. Long before that, at 3 s, the truck detects link loss and stops within its granted path (SCN-COMMS-R05, LinkLossParams.detect_s) [A]. The lab's gap sweep shows it plainly: for gaps of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 s no truck is affected; at 3.0 s and above, every truck at every handover makes a link-loss stop. A handover gap that grows past the detection time — through a misconfiguration, interference at the boundary or an overloaded target site — turns into a DLY-OD-05 stop at the same chainage on every pass.

A horizon tuned for efficiency must include the handover. The speed-adaptive horizon of ch-10-04 holds exactly the class C stop plus one extension period. Through any handover gap, however short, it runs short by the distance driven in the gap: 16.7 m on the empty descent. The truck would slow at every pass. A speed-adaptive horizon must therefore add vTg where a hard handover lies ahead — 111.7 m instead of 95.1 m on the R05 descent — or the AMS must know the handover chainages and extend early before them [R]. Either way, the AMS needs the handover locations as data, which is one reason the serving map belongs in the AHS's configuration and not only in the network team's tools.

4.5 Handover, coverage hole or site failure?

The control room sees symptoms, not causes. Three network causes produce stops or link flags on R05, and each has a different signature in data the control room already has:

Cause Where When Which trucks Typical duration
Handover problem At one chainage (a serving-map boundary), both directions On every pass, or on passes at a certain speed Any truck Short and similar each time
Coverage hole or marginal span Over a span of road Randomly, more often in bad conditions Any truck Variable
Comms-site failure Every segment the site alone serves All at once All trucks in that area Until the site is back
Truck fault Wherever that truck is Follows the truck One truck Variable

LAB-11-02-01 makes the first two concrete. It simulates 50 empty descents of R05 with the marginal-class burst-loss model of network.yaml (GilbertElliott.for_class) for every 0.5 s message slot, forces the 1.5 s handover gap at 1000 m, and counts link drops (three or more consecutive lost slots) per 100 m bin of chainage [A]:

Chainage bin (m from P1) 0 100 200 300 400 500 600 700 800 900 1000 1100 1200 1300
Handover only 8 11 10 9 9 6 6 3 10 5 57 5 10 5
Handover + poor span 500–700 m [A] 10 8 14 5 7 40 29 7 11 13 43 8 15 2
Handover only, detector ≥ 4 slots (2 s) 5 6 6 5 4 3 2 3 4 1 9 1 9 3

The handover is a spike in one bin: 57 drops in 50 passes, against 3 to 11 in every other bin, which is the background of a marginal link. A coverage hole is a raised plateau over its span (40 and 29). And the detector matters: a tool that counts only drops of 2 s or more does not see a 1.5 s handover as a spike (9 drops, no more than the 1200 m bin). A network-health tool whose threshold was set to suppress nuisance flags can hide the one problem that is the same on every pass.

The operations lesson is to look at where drops happen, accumulated over many trucks and passes, not at each drop on its own. Ten single "link degraded" events on ten different trucks are not ten truck faults when they all lie in the same 100 m. ch-16-07 made the same point for comms-loss alarms: grouped by the site that serves the segment, eight trucks form one group; grouped by truck, eight unrelated events.

4.6 Serving a moving pit face

The pit floor, the RL 330 bench and the faces of EX01–EX04 move. The options for keeping them covered are:

Option How it follows the pit What it depends on Main cost
Mobile comms trailer (CT3 today) Moved to a new pad, re-aimed, re-verified Its backhaul (radio link to CT2) and its generator Each move takes it off air; the move is an AHS change
Mesh relay nodes (on trailers or light towers) Added or moved near the face The relay chain back to a wired node Capacity and latency through the chain; many nodes to maintain
Vehicle-mounted node [A] (e.g. on a shovel) Moves with the machine The machine's power and position, and a relay path The network moves when the machine does; a parked or tramming shovel changes coverage
Additional fixed site on a crest Not at all; covers by reach Line of sight into the pit Shadow below the walls remains

Each option puts part of the AHS's authority on a moving object. The segments only CT3 serves are the AHS's exposure to the trailer: when it is off air, they have no authority. Choosing a trailer position is therefore an AHS decision as much as a network one; ch-11-04 estimates coverage from candidate positions, this chapter covers what the move does to operations.

What CT3 off air does. ahs_sim.network.affected_segments(net, ["CT3"]) gives R05 (from 1000 m), R09, R10, R11, R19 and R20. Because lower R05 and R09 are the beginnings of both pit exits, usable_exits finds neither R05-P1 nor R24-P3 usable from the pit floor. LAB-11-02-01 checks which loading units can still reach their destination:

Case Segments without authority Pit exits usable Loading units cut off Free-cycle rate cut off Not moved in 3.5 h
CT3 off air, network.yaml as is R05, R09, R10, R11, R19, R20 none EX01, EX02, EX03, EX04 17 101.5 t/h 59 855 t

The rates are free-cycle upper bounds for the baseline allocation (no queueing, as in ch-01-07); realised rates are lower. The window of 3.5 h runs from the restriction at T−3600 s to the start of the staged lift at T+9000 s of SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE [A].

The first line is the trap: taking CT3 off air without preparation stops every loading unit, because no truck can climb out of the pit. The registered scenario reassigns the trucks of EX03 and EX04 and keeps the rest of the pit working. That is only possible if lower R05 keeps a serving site while CT3 moves — a temporary relay, or a CT2 sector re-aimed for the window [A]. In other words, the relocation must be make-before-break: whatever will carry lower R05 during the move must be on air and verified before CT3 goes off air [R]. Without it, the move is a planned whole-pit outage and should be scheduled as one.

4.7 Capacity: how much must a cell carry?

The capacity a cell needs depends on how many devices it serves at once and what each sends. Both are estimated here with stated assumptions; the method matters more than the numbers.

How many trucks a site serves. By Little's law, the expected number of trucks in an area is the sum over loading units of trucks × (time per cycle in the area) / (cycle time). LAB-11-02-01 applies it to the free cycles of the baseline allocation (37 producing trucks), with loading and dumping times placed on the loading and dump links, and counts for each site the trucks it may have to serve (every segment where it is a serving site) and those only it can serve:

Site May serve, R05 open Only it serves, R05 open May serve, R05 closed Only it serves, R05 closed
CT1 0.0 0.0 2.6 0.0
CT2 29.6 22.0 31.8 25.2
CT3 15.0 7.4 9.2 5.2

CT2 is the busy site in both states; the closure moves about two trucks' worth of demand onto it. Design should use the bound — all 40 trucks in one cell — where a cell can plausibly hold them, for example at park-up or during a site-wide restart, and the expected value for normal operation.

What each device sends [A]:

Device Uplink Downlink
Autonomous truck position and state report 2 × 300 B/s; telemetry 1 × 2000 B/s → 20.8 kbit/s AMS grant, extension and acknowledgement 1 × 500 B/s → 4.0 kbit/s
RTK corrections from GB1 — 1 × 1000 B/s per stream → 8.0 kbit/s
Manned vehicle (12 light vehicles, 4 dozers, 3 water trucks) position report 1 × 200 B/s; field-terminal messages 0.1 × 500 B/s —
Shovel (4) loading interface 1 × 300 B/s —
Voice, if carried on the AHS network 4 calls × 32 kbit/s 4 calls × 32 kbit/s
Occasional video (one stream) 4000 kbit/s —

The demand of a cell is then

D=∑iniribi·8·10−3 kbit/s,Ddesign=D·koh·khead

with ni devices of class i sending ri messages per second of bi bytes, a protocol overhead factor koh=1.3 and a headroom factor khead=2 [A]. Section 12 works it through for 40 trucks. Its result is the main design lesson: one video stream is 79.9 % of the uplink demand, and the AHS control and position traffic of the whole fleet is 17.6 %. A cell sized for video has ample room for control traffic by volume. The danger for control traffic is not volume but priority: a video stream that is allowed to queue ahead of AMS messages adds delay to every extension (ch-11-03). Capacity planning and QoS design are two halves of one decision.

One configuration choice moves the downlink a long way: RTK corrections sent once per cell (broadcast or multicast) cost 8.0 kbit/s; sent separately to each of 40 trucks and 19 manned vehicles they cost 472.0 kbit/s [A]. Whether a given technology and its configuration support a shared stream is [TBV].

4.8 When R24 is the only exit

During SCN-MAP-R05 the west ramp carries the pit. Four things change for the radio design, and each should be checked before the closure, not during it (section 8.3):

  1. Concentration. R24 carries 145.5 passes an hour; about 9.6 trucks are on it at any moment, against 11.7 on R05 when R05 is open. CT2 may have to serve 31.8 trucks instead of 29.6. The capacity of the CT2 cell or sector facing R24 must hold that number with headroom, including any video that the closure work itself brings (for example a camera at the rework).
  2. Single server on a marginal ramp. R24 is served by CT2 alone and is classed marginal. With R05 closed, a degradation of CT2's coverage on R24 has no detour left; it stops the pit's only exit. ch-11-04 evaluates a second server for R24 (a relay at P3 or at W [A]) and shows that CT3, which also sees R24 in the coverage estimate, is a poor choice for that role: CT3 already carries the start of the detour on R09, so adding it to R24 does not remove the common cause.
  3. A handover at an intersection. Every truck changes site at W. A handover where trucks may be waiting for an intersection grant adds its gap to the grant's delivery time. Moving the CT2/CT3 boundary off the intersection — for example so that CT2 also serves the upper part of R09, or CT3 the first part of R24 — would put the handover on open road, where a 1.5 s gap costs nothing (section 4.4) [A].
  4. RTK and permissions. Field crews who work on the R05 rework enter through AP-02 and request permissions there; the light vehicles that support the detour use AP-04 at P3. Both access points must have coverage good enough for field-terminal acknowledgements (ch-11-03), or the controller must fall back to voice confirmation as ch-10-06 describes.

4.9 Spectrum and regulation

Every radio technology uses spectrum, and in Australia the use of radio spectrum is regulated [TBV]. Which arrangements apply to a private network at a mine — licensed, shared, licence-exempt with conditions — and what they require of the operator are regulatory questions this handbook does not answer (SRC-REG-AU-SPECTRUM-LICENSING, [TBV]). The handbook's rule is procedural [H]: the spectrum basis of every radio link that carries AHS traffic must be identified and checked during site readiness (ch-19-02), and any change of band, power or site location is a change to be assessed against it.

4.10 Trade-offs and model limits

  • Serving map, not radio. network.yaml says which site serves which span; it models no signal level, interference or load, and its handover point does not move with conditions.
  • One gap value of 1.5 s for every handover [A]; below the detection time the result is insensitive to it, above it binary.
  • Steady speeds from ahs_sim.haul_cycle; a truck braking for W is slower than the leg speed in the table.
  • Demand only. The loss model per class and the device rates are [A]; no technology's capacity is modelled, and queueing inside the network is ch-11-03's subject.

6. Normal, abnormal and degraded operation

6.1 Normal operation — handovers on every pass

Every truck on R05 crosses the CT2/CT3 boundary at 1000 m once per direction; with a 200 m horizon none slows (section 4.4). The network-health tool records a spike of short link drops at that chainage (section 4.5). That spike is normal and should be on the controller's reference picture of the shift, so that a change in it — longer gaps, more drops, a shift in position — is noticed.

6.2 Abnormal operation — SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE: planned relocation of CT3 (shared timeline for sections 7 and 8)

The registry gives five key events. This chapter adds [A] intermediate times where the walkthrough needs them, and assumes, as section 4.6 explains, that lower R05 is covered temporarily so that EX01 and EX02 keep working [A].

6.3 Degraded operation — SCN-COMMS-R05: the same segments, unplanned

When CT3 loses power (registry: T+0 s CT3 down; T+3 s trucks detect link loss and stop within their granted paths; T+10 s marked communication-lost, reservations frozen; T+900 s CT3 restored, reconciliation), eight trucks are inside the area (ch-02-08, ch-16-07). Without the temporary cover of section 6.2, lower R05 and R09 are lost together and no exit remains (ch-01-03, ch-16-07). The technology does not change this: any single site that alone serves the starts of both exits is a common cause. A design that gives lower R05 or R09 a second server that does not depend on CT3 (not through CT3's backhaul or power) would keep one exit open in an unplanned CT3 failure as well.

6.4 Abnormal operation — SCN-MAP-R05: R24 carries the pit

From T−3600 s, when R05 is closed and the AMS replans over the west ramp (registry), until T+2700 s, when routes return to R05, every truck changes site at W and climbs R24 on CT2 alone. The checks of section 4.8 should have been done before T−3600 s. During the closure, link drops on R24 are the signal to watch: a drop pattern that was background on R24 when it carried only bench traffic becomes a production risk when it carries the pit.

7.3 Interface and timing considerations along SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE

  • T−3600 s. The restriction is an AMS function: no new grants into R09–R11, R19, R20. Trucks already inside finish and leave; the AMS must not rely on the network team's schedule to know the area is clear.
  • T−600 s. "Clear" is a positive condition: no truck holds a reservation and no permission is active in the restricted segments. It is checked in the AMS, not assumed from the dispatch plan.
  • T+0 s. Taking CT3 off air is the network team's action, under the Field Technician's own digital access permission in AA-North (ch-10-06). From this moment the AMS treats the segments as having no serving site.
  • T+5400 s. "On air" is not "in service". The site must pass its backhaul check (CT3 depends on CT2) before the drive test starts.
  • T+7200–7500 s. The serving map in the AHS configuration and the coverage attributes (ch-11-04) change together, under one change record, with the drive test as evidence. The AMS loads the new serving map before any grant in the area.
  • T+9000 s. The first grant on R09 goes to a truck whose state is reconciled; the area opens in stages, as after any restoration (ch-10-08). The handover points have moved with CT3; the controller's reference picture of drop locations must be refreshed.

8. Operations view

8.1 What the control room sees

  • Site status for CT1, CT2, CT3 (on air, backhaul, power) and the serving map: which segments each site alone serves.
  • Link-drop map: drops per 100 m of chainage per segment over the shift, with the handover points marked. The normal R05 spike at 1000 m is shown as expected; a growth of the spike, a new spike or a plateau is flagged.
  • Per-truck link flags (degraded, lost) with the segment and chainage.
  • Restrictions in force (segments with no new grants), with the reason and the responsible role.
  • Pit exits under valid authority — R05-P1 and R24-P3 — recalculated whenever a site's state or a restriction changes.

8.3 Procedure excerpts

A. Planned relocation of CT3 (SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE).

  1. Pit Supervisor, network team (T−7 d [A]): agree the new position and the window. Ask the AHS Controller to run the exits check: with CT3 off air, which pit exits remain under valid authority? If none, either plan temporary cover of lower R05 or treat the window as a planned outage of AA-North.
  2. Network team, Survey (T−1 d [A]): install and drive-test the temporary cover. Record the result.
  3. Pit Supervisor (T−3600 s): confirm the window on shift. FMS / AHS Controller: reassign the EX03 and EX04 trucks; restrict R09, R10, R11, R19, R20. Inform the Shovel Operators of EX03 and EX04 and the plant.
  4. AHS Controller (T−600 s [A]): confirm no truck reservation and no active permission in the restricted segments. Tell the Field Technician "area clear" by radio and record the time.
  5. Field Technician (T+0 s): take CT3 off air. Report by radio.
  6. Field Technician (T+5400 s): report CT3 on air and the backhaul to CT2 confirmed.
  7. Survey (T+5400–7200 s): drive the affected segments under permission; submit the drive-test record (ch-11-04).
  8. Network team, AHS Controller (T+7500 s [A]): update the serving map from the record; the AHS Controller checks that every segment to be reopened has a serving site in the new map.
  9. AHS Controller (T+9000 s): lift restrictions one segment group at a time, starting with R09; release the first truck only after its state is reconciled; inform the Shovel Operators.
  10. AHS Controller (end of shift): refresh the reference drop map: the handover points have moved.

B. Before a planned R05 closure (SCN-MAP-R05). At least one shift before T−3600 s [A] the AHS Controller asks the network team for: CT2 status and recent drop map on R24 and at W; capacity headroom of the CT2 cell or sector facing R24 with 31.8 trucks expected; coverage at AP-04 and AP-02 for field terminals. A plateau of drops on R24, or no headroom, is reported to the Pit Supervisor as a reason to delay the closure or to add a second server first (ch-11-04).

C. Repeated link drops at one place. When the drop map shows three or more trucks [A] dropping in the same 100 m bin in one shift, or a known spike growing, the AHS Controller records a coverage defect (location, segment, chainage, directions, times, trucks) in the network team's queue instead of a truck fault for each truck, and tells the Maintenance Planner so that the trucks are not taken out of service for radio checks they do not need.

8.4 KPIs and delay codes affected

  • CT3 relocation. EX03 and EX04 left without trucks while their circuit is restricted: coded DLY-OD-04 (held for a work zone, extended to a loading unit left without trucks by a held circuit, coding_rules) [H]. Reassigned trucks waiting at EX01 or EX02: DLY-NP-01. A truck that stops in the area because it was not clear in time: DLY-OD-05. Physical Availability does not change — no truck is down — but Operating Delay rises and production falls for the window. Planned network work that holds trucks or starves loading units is coded DLY-OD-04 (coding_rules).
  • Handover problems. Stops at a handover are DLY-OD-05 (stopped on communication loss); they are coded as the same cause on every truck and linked to one coverage-defect record.
  • Unplanned CT3 loss. DLY-OD-05 inside the dead zone; DLY-OD-04 for trucks held short of its frozen reservations (coding_rules, ch-10-04).
  • Site-wide loss. DLY-SE-02 (site-wide loss of wireless network) only if the whole site is affected, not for one comms site.

8.5 Training and competency implications

Controllers learn to read the link-drop map and tell the four signatures of section 4.5 apart, to run the exits check before planned site work, and to treat "on air" and "in service" as different states. Pit Supervisors learn that a trailer move is an AHS change with a production window and that make-before-break is their planning choice. Network-team members learn the AHS consequences of a serving-map change and the entry rules for the pit (ch-10-06).

9. Requirements and acceptance criteria

REQ-11-02-001 [H] The AMS shall hold the serving map (which comms site serves which segment span) as controlled configuration, and shall not grant a reservation or permission in a segment span that has no serving site in service. Rationale: authority cannot be maintained where no site serves; the AMS must know this before granting, not learn it from a stop. Verification: test (LAB-11-02-01, affected_segments and usable_exits for CT3 off air), inspection of configuration control

REQ-11-02-002 [H] Where a reservation horizon is adapted to speed, the AMS shall add the distance driven during the assumed handover gap at every hard handover ahead within the horizon, or shall extend the granted path before the handover so that the same margin is obtained. Rationale: without it, every truck slows at every handover (section 4.4: −16.7 m on the R05 descent). Verification: test (LAB-11-02-01 margin table), analysis

REQ-11-02-003 [H] The site network design shall keep the handover gap at every hard handover on a haul route below the vehicles' link-loss detection time with a margin defined by the site [A], and the handover settings shall be under change control. Rationale: a gap above the detection time stops every truck at the same place on every pass. Verification: test (drive test with gap measurement at each handover), inspection

REQ-11-02-004 [H] Hard handovers shall not be placed at intersections, queue points, access points or park-up areas unless no alternative exists and the reason is recorded. Rationale: at these places trucks wait for grants and people request permissions; a gap there adds delay to the messages that matter most (section 4.8, W on the detour). Verification: inspection of the serving map against the AHS map

REQ-11-02-005 [H] A planned outage of a comms site shall be preceded by an exits check (which pit exits remain under valid authority) and, for a site that alone serves a pit exit, either by verified temporary cover of that exit (make-before-break) or by scheduling the window as a planned outage of the area. Rationale: CT3 off air without preparation leaves no pit exit and stops every loading unit (section 4.6). Verification: inspection of the relocation plan; test (LAB-11-02-01 relocation_case)

REQ-11-02-006 [H] After a comms site is moved or reconfigured, the AMS shall reopen affected segments only after the serving map has been updated from a drive-test record of those segments and the first truck to enter has been reconciled. Rationale: "on air" is not "in service"; a serving map that does not match the radio would grant authority where none can be maintained. Verification: inspection (change record with drive-test evidence), test (SCN-COMMS-CT3-MOVE walkthrough)

REQ-11-02-007 [H] The network-health tools available to the control room shall accumulate link drops by segment and chainage across trucks and passes, with a detection threshold no longer than the shortest handover gap expected. Rationale: a handover is visible only as a location spike; a 2 s detector misses a 1.5 s gap (section 4.5). Verification: test (LAB-11-02-01 drop histograms), inspection

REQ-11-02-008 [H] Capacity planning for each cell shall state the device counts (expected and bound), per-device demand, overhead and headroom assumptions, and shall include the concentration on R24 during an R05 closure. Rationale: capacity is a design decision; undeclared assumptions cannot be checked when the fleet or the routes change. Verification: inspection; analysis (section 12, Example 2)

REQ-11-02-009 [H] The spectrum basis of every radio link that carries AHS traffic shall be identified and checked during site readiness, and a change of band, transmit power or site location shall be assessed against it. Rationale: regulatory requirements for spectrum use are not stated in this handbook [TBV]; the check must happen. Verification: inspection (site-readiness record, ch-19-02)

10. Verification and validation

  • Model checks. LAB-11-02-01's tests reproduce shared numbers from other chapters: 155.5 passes per hour on R05 and 145.5 on R24 (ch-01-07), the 84.0 m and 10.7 m class C stops and the 95.1 m and 13.8 m horizons (ch-10-04), the 5865.1 m detour (SCN-MAP-R05: about 5.87 km). Agreement shows the lab uses the same mine and models; it does not validate the radio assumptions.
  • What must be measured on site. Handover gap at each hard handover in both directions and at operating speed; drop locations over a representative period; demand at concentration points with the actual message rates; backhaul behaviour from each candidate trailer position. These replace the [A] values.
  • Operational claims. That controllers tell handover spikes from coverage plateaus is validated in training with recorded or synthetic drop maps.
  • Technology claims. Every [TBV] statement of FIG-11-02-01 is to be verified for the system a site selects (SRC-PUB-RADIO-TECH-BEHAVIOUR); the handbook does not verify vendor claims.

11. Common mistakes

11.1 Design mistakes

  • Choosing a radio technology before writing down the AHS's demands (section 4.1), and then discovering that the pit floor still needs a trailer.
  • Treating a handover as a network-internal detail: placing the boundary at an intersection, or tuning horizons without a gap allowance.
  • Planning a trailer move as a network task and finding at T+0 s that neither pit exit has a serving site.
  • Sizing capacity from the average truck count and ignoring that one video stream dominates the uplink.
  • Giving a marginal ramp a second server that shares the first server's common cause (CT3 on R24, section 4.8).

11.2 Operational mistakes

  • Logging each short link flag as a truck fault and sending trucks to the workshop for radio checks.
  • Reading "CT3 on air" as "area open" and lifting restrictions before the drive test and serving-map update.
  • Raising the drop-detector threshold to silence nuisance flags, and with it hiding the handover spike.
  • Closing R05 without asking for the R24 checks; finding the marginal span when 9.6 trucks are on it.

12. Worked examples

Example 1 — Handover margin on the R05 descent. DT16, empty, at 40 km/h = 11.11 m/s. Class C stop 84.0 m (ch-04-05). Horizon 200 m, extension period 1.0 s, gap 1.5 s [A]. Granted path at the end of the gap: 200 − 11.11 × 2.5 = 172.2 m; margin 172.2 − 83.95 = 88.3 m (lab: 88.3 m). Longest gap without slowing: (200 − 84.0) / 11.11 − 1.0 = 9.44 s. With the speed-adaptive horizon 84.0 + 11.1 = 95.1 m, the margin is 95.1 − 11.11 × 2.5 − 84.0 = −16.7 m: DT16 would have to slow. Adding the allowance 11.11 × 1.5 = 16.7 m gives 111.7 m. The detection time of 3 s is below 9.44 s, so with a 200 m horizon it is the link-loss detection, not the granted path, that limits the tolerable gap.

Example 2 — Cell demand for 40 trucks [A]. Uplink per truck: 2 × 300 × 8 = 4800 bit/s plus 1 × 2000 × 8 = 16 000 bit/s = 20.8 kbit/s; 40 trucks 832.0 kbit/s. Manned vehicles: 19 × (1 × 200 + 0.1 × 500) × 8 = 38.0 kbit/s. Shovels: 4 × 300 × 8 = 9.6 kbit/s. Voice 4 × 32 = 128.0 kbit/s. Video 4000 kbit/s. Uplink total 5007.6 kbit/s; with overhead 1.3 and headroom 2: 13 019.8 kbit/s. Downlink: trucks 40 × 500 × 8 = 160.0 kbit/s, RTK 8.0 kbit/s (one stream), voice 128.0 kbit/s; total 296.0 kbit/s, design 769.6 kbit/s. Video is 79.9 % of the uplink, control and position traffic 17.6 %. Without voice and video the uplink is 879.6 kbit/s. With RTK sent per device (59 streams) the RTK downlink alone is 472.0 kbit/s.

Example 3 — Production at stake in a CT3 move. Free-cycle rates of the baseline allocation (ch-01-07 method): EX01 5036.8 t/h, EX02 4557.4 t/h, EX03 3722.8 t/h, EX04 3784.6 t/h; total 17 101.5 t/h [A]. With CT3 off air and no temporary cover, all four are cut off: over the 3.5 h window, 59 855 t not moved (upper bound). With lower R05 covered, only EX03 and EX04 are cut off: 7507.4 t/h, 26 276 t. The difference, 33 579 t, is what make-before-break is worth in this window — before counting the risk of a CT3 failure while the trailer is being reinstalled.

Example 4 — Handover rate. With R05 open the R05 boundary sees 155.5 passes an hour, so 155.5 handovers an hour at one place, one every 23 s; in a 12 h shift 1866. A 1 % chance per handover of a gap long enough to stop the truck [A] would mean about 19 link-loss stops a shift at the same chainage — a pattern the drop map shows long before the stops do.

15. Key takeaways

  • No radio technology removes the pit's geometry: crest sites cannot see below the walls, so the floor needs moving sites, whichever family is used.
  • At the reference mine every haul cycle crosses one hard handover: at 1000 m on R05, or at Intersection W on the detour.
  • With a 200 m horizon, a 1.5 s gap costs nothing; at 3 s the truck's link-loss detection stops it — the detection time, not the horizon, is the binding limit. Speed-adaptive horizons need a gap allowance.
  • A handover shows as a spike of drops at one chainage, a coverage hole as a plateau, a site failure as all trucks at once. Look at where drops happen, across trucks.
  • Taking CT3 off air without temporary cover leaves no pit exit. Relocations of sites that alone serve an exit must be make-before-break.
  • Video dominates capacity; control traffic needs priority more than bandwidth. R24 during an R05 closure is the concentration point to check in advance.
  • Spectrum use is regulated; the basis is checked during site readiness, not assumed [TBV].
